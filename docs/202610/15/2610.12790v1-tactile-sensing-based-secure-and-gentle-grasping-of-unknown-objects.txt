Tactile Sensing-based Secure and Gentle
Grasping of Unknown Objects
Feng Han
Abstract—Reliable robotic manipulation requires maintaining
a firm grasp while strictly avoiding both contact slip and struc-
tural damage to the object. Satisfying these inherent secure-
and-gentle grasping constraints is challenging for unknown
objects. We present a novel online data-driven predictive control
framework for safe dynamic robotic grasping of unknown
objects. The proposed system features a pair of compact, single-
channel tactile-sensing modules that characterize the gripper-
object dynamic interaction in real time, entirely bypassing the
need for prior knowledge or offline training. A finite-state-
machine-based grasping force probing mechanism is developed
to establish the initial grasping force and explicitly identify
grasping force constraints. Through online dynamics learning,
a Gaussian Process (GP) model captures gripper-object interac-
tion dynamics. The model uncertainties are further used to form
robust grasping constraints, which are embedded into a model
predictive control scheme. We demonstrate error convergence
in a probabilistic sense. The proposed control framework runs
at 100 Hz in real time to dynamically manipulate and handle
objects rather than static holding. Extensive experimental eval-
uations across diverse contact surface topologies and various
types of objects validate superior performance and efficiency
compared to baseline strategies. The proposed method is able
to manipulate objects ranging from delicate items, such as raw
tofu and marshmallows, to rough objects, such as rocks.
Index Terms—Robotic Grasping, Interaction Dynamics, Tac-
tile Sensing, Gaussian Process Regression, Model Predictive
Control.
I. INTRODUCTION
Robotic grasping and manipulation offer great efficiency
and dexterity across a broad spectrum of automated tasks.
Notable examples include automated fruit harvesting in mod-
ern agriculture [1]–[3], dynamic food sorting and picking in
the food industry [4]–[6], and the delicate and ultra-precise
grasping and transporting of live organs during robotic
surgery [7], [8]. With prior knowledge and offline training,
these grasping tasks can be achieved safely. However, reliable
robotic grasping of unknown objects remains one of the
most fundamental yet challenging objectives in autonomous
robotics [9]–[11]. When a robotic gripper interacts with
unseen objects, the inherent but mathematically unknown
grasping force constraints must be satisfied. The grasping
force should provide secure but delicate contact to avoid
contact slip, damage, or breakdown [9], [12], [13]. These
constraints are related to the object’s mass, material compli-
ance, friction, and contact interface topology, etc., which are
completely unknown. Even the same object under different

in different interaction dynamics [14]. For instance, grasping

Human grasping and manipulation of unknown objects
take advantage of the coordination of vision and haptic-based
force feedback [10], [15]–[17]. The advanced biostructure of
human fingers is capable of adapting to the object’s geometry,
size, surface topologies, etc. When handling objects out of the
line of sight, human manipulation can still achieve success
by relying fundamentally on blind haptic reflexes [17], [18].
During this process, humans learn the unseen object’s me-
chanical properties, “feel” the interaction mechanism online,
and then adjust the applied force to reach a secure-and-gentle
grasp [14], [17].
For robotic grasping, accurate sensing plays a significant
role in characterizing the robotic gripper-object interaction
mechanism and dynamic grasping control. Sensing technol-
ogy can be categorized into contactless (i.e., vision-based)
and contact (i.e., tactile/haptic-based) groups. Vision-based
machine learning agents can recognize and label object types
and prescribe a grasping force [9], [13], [16], [19]–[21].
However, such pipelines heavily depend on a pre-trained
network over massive object datasets [22], [23]. Vision sys-
tems inherently suffer from critical degradation in occluded
spaces and an inability to monitor visually unrecognized
micro-slip at the interface [12], [13], [24], [25]. Furthermore,
the computational cost of vision-based grasping is usually
demanding, and the real-time control frequency is relatively
low [9], [26]. For instance, in [26], video processing is
used to handle tactile sensing feedback, and the control
frequency can only reach 1 Hz. Tactile sensing provides
an alternative by perceiving physical interactions through
direct contact [14], [19], [27], [28]. Nevertheless, tactile
sensing requires accurate calibration, and haptic feedback is
inherently susceptible to distortions caused by complex con-
tact surface topologies, localized stress concentrations, and
vibrations [29], [30]. The uncertainties in sensing propagate
into interaction dynamics characterization and control design.
Under dynamic grasping with a time-varying reference force,
the control system design must respect secure-and-gentle
grasping constraints [9], [26].
To overcome the challenges in unknown object grasping,
we propose a multi-phase, vision-free interaction dynamics
learning and predictive control architecture. The proposed
tactile mechatronics can adapt to different contact inter-
face topologies and detect potential object slip or yielding

A. Related Work
Robotic grasping and manipulation encompass multi-
faceted innovations, including advanced gripper design, sens-
ing and perception, actuation, modeling, and control. In the
following, we review the most relevant work in the literature.
1) Robotic Grasping: Robotic grasping has been used in
various applications due to its dexterity, ranging from fruit
harvesting in agriculture [1]–[3] and food-feeding in care-
giving [15], [31] to raw material picking in manufacturing.
For different applications, various gripper mechanisms have
been developed, including rigid [13], [14], [32] and soft
grippers [27], [28]. For instance, a compliant three-finger
gripper is designed with embedded strain gauges in [19],
while the work in [3] designs a hybrid robotic gripper com-
bining six soft auxetic fingers with a rigid exoskeleton. Soft
grippers can provide direct lifting force, whereas parallel-jaw
grippers usually apply a normal contact force, with lifting
force generated indirectly through friction [14].
2) Tactile Sensing: Emerging tactile sensing technology
provides direct information about the gripper-object contact
interface at the interface [33]–[35], which resembles ad-
vanced human grasping feedback. To provide rich contact
information, multi-tactile sensing arrays have been designed
and integrated into gripper systems [9], [14], [16], [35].
However, the high nonlinearity of tactile sensors requires
advanced calibration to address hysteresis, dead zones, sat-
uration, etc. A popular approach in the literature is to use
machine learning-based methods to interpret raw measure-
ments and bypass the calibration process. Tactile sensing,
in principle, measures pressure and is thus sensitive to
the gripper-object contact profile, area size, slip, and vi-
brations, leading to inherent uncertainties and case-specific
feedback [29], [30]. Integrated with vision systems, visual-
tactile sensing combines the advantages of both modalities,
providing rich feedback information for decision-making and
control design [9], [13], [26]. However, the computational
costs become very demanding, resulting in a low real-time
control frequency (i.e., less than 50 Hz).
3) Grasping Control: A critical component in robotic
grasping is contact force design and control. Since interaction
dynamics are case-specific, most existing works either ignore
the hidden nonlinear gripper-object interaction dynamics or
embed them implicitly into the learning process [19]. The
most popular approach designs the grasping force based on
prior datasets [13], [22], [23]. Empowered by machine learn-
ing techniques, robotic grasping can perceive and recognize
objects, with grasping forces designed accordingly to enable
object-specific strategies. In [27], [36], raw tactile feedback
is used for object recognition within a neural network. How-
ever, when grasping unknown, delicate objects, failures are
commonly observed. Secure-and-gentle grasping requires ad-
equate force without aggressive contact. The boundary grasp-
ing forces vary depending on the object’s mass, compliance,
surface roughness, and contact topology [14], [16], [17], and
they must be identified online. Meanwhile, most works in
the literature focus on object grasp-and-hold tasks [14], [37]
rather than dynamic grasping force control, which is critical
in the presence of environmental uncertainties.
Additionally, PID, MPC [9], [26], and machine learning-
based control strategies [16], [19], [27], [38], [39] have
been proposed in the literature. Physical model-based control
primarily focuses on gripper kinematic movement control
rather than explicit gripper-object interaction dynamics [9],
[27]. The MPC approach enforces constraints on gripper
movement and contact force to ensure safe and secure
grasping. In [9], an MPC is designed using gripper kinematics
and vision-tactile sensing feedback. However, the constraints
are only imposed on the kinematic movement of the gripper.
Because the interaction dynamics remain unknown, there
are no constraints involving the interaction force. Machine
learning-based control is also popular given the complicated
gripper-object interaction dynamics. In [27], raw tactile sens-
ing data are directly processed in a neural network with-
out calibration. End-to-end reinforcement learning neglects
interaction dynamics, and the grasping force is designed
through reward shaping [19], [38]. Learning from human
grasping is another alternative [16], [39]. However, these
learning mechanisms rely on offline training and have low
transparency and adaptation to unknown objects.

alteration of the grippers and supportive mechatron-
ics. Through GP-based calibration, the tactile sensing
provides high-fidelity measurements regardless of the
tactile contact interface topologies.
2) The proposed novel finite-state machine safely and
explicitly identifies the object’s geometry, compliance,
and object-specific grasping force constraints online in

B. Problem Formulation
The target of this work is to build a tactile grasping control
system for unknown objects while respecting the unknown,
underlying constraints. To establish mathematical tractability
and define the scope of this work, we first make the following
assumptions regarding the dynamic grasping process.
A1 Initially, the object resides within the workspace of
the gripper, such that Vobj ∩IWG̸ = ∅, where Vobj
in FI represents the volume occupied by the object’s
geometry.
A2 The grasping system has zero a priori knowledge of the
object, including its mass m, friction characteristics,
internal material stiffness κ, and surface topology.
However, the object’s mass and stiffness are bounded
as m ∈(0, mmax] and κ ∈[κmin, κmax].
A3 The tactile sensor only registers the single-channel
interaction load along the ex direction, despite the ef-
fective contact region potentially spanning a continuous
surface area.
A4 The system imposes no geometric or distribution con-
straints on the contact topology between the object and
the tactile sensor.
The assumption that the object initially resides within
the intersecting volume of the workspace is reasonable
in the sense that tactile-sensing grippers can search for
the object without vision and reconstruct its 3-dimensional
structure [40]–[42]. Furthermore, A2 is made based on the
grasping capability (the maximum grasping force) of the
physical system. Meanwhile, A3 and A4 are realistic and
practical. Due to the limited area of the gripper fingers, the
tactile sensor must be compact and capable of adapting to
various contact surface topologies. Given the above system
setup and assumptions, we present the problem statement.
Problem statement: Given a completely unknown ob-
ject and the tactile-sensing grasping system under Assump-
tions A1 through A4, this work aims to design an online
machine learning-based dynamic grasping control framework
to manipulate and transport the object while respecting the
unknown constraints in (6).
C. Overview of the Proposed Approach
To resolve the challenges of parametric uncertainties (A2),
completely unobserved shear and friction states (A3), and
unmodeled contact topologies (A4), we propose a vision-
free active grasping architecture. The proposed approach
tightly couples tactile mechatronics, constraint identification,
online interaction dynamics characterization, and predictive
control; see the schematic diagram in Fig. 2. The framework
is organized into three core pillars:
1) Tactile Sensing Mechatronics: To interface directly
with unknown object profiles and contact topologies
(A4), we design a grasping interaction sensing module
using affordable tactile sensors (< $5). The designed
sensing module automatically adapts to various contact
types. The severe nonlinearities and cross-coupling
inherent in raw sensor measurements are systematically
resolved using a machine learning-based calibration.
2) Finite State Machine for Object Probing and Con-
straint Identification: Because the minimum physical
slip threshold Fobj,min and structural destruction limit
Fobj,max are unknown parameters (A2, A3), an FSM is
deployed to manage operational execution phases and
identify these unknown constraints.
3) Online Interaction Dynamics Learning and MPC
Control: To accommodate unmodeled elastic material
variations (A2), a data-driven non-parametric model
learns the gripper-object interaction dynamics finter
online. This adaptive interaction model is embedded
directly inside a nonlinear MPC engine alongside the
identified constraints. The model uncertainty is ex-
plicitly incorporated into the control design to ensure
robust predictive control.
By integrating these three strategies, the proposed archi-
tecture bypasses the need for global vision networks or exact
material profiles, achieving highly robust and safe dynamic
grasping of unmodeled payloads.
III. GRIPPER TACTILE SENSING SYSTEM
In this section, we present the mechatronic design of a
compact tactile sensing system for grasping control. The
tactile sensing system provides real-time force feedback on
the gripper-object interaction.
A. Tactile Sensor Mechatronics

layer is detailed in Fig. 3. Each parallel finger is integrated
with a dedicated tactile sensing module featuring a single-
channel FSR. Under external normal pressure, the spatial dis-
tribution of internal transduction varies, thereby altering the
material’s conductivity. This dynamic conductance change
is subsequently mapped to a continuous analog signal and
measured via a standard voltage divider circuit. The raw
analog signal is then sampled using a microcontroller.

active sensing area, ensuring that irregular geometric bound-

signal saturation. Furthermore, this composite design inher-
ently possesses the structural ability to damp high-frequency
tactile noise induced by micro-slip transitions and dynamic
structural vibrations.

Gentle and secure grasping of unknown objects requires
appropriate grasping force design and the identification of the
constraints Csecure and Cgentle. In this section, we propose an
FSM-based probing strategy to capture the object’s geometry
and compliance, design the contact force, and identify these

V. DYNAMIC GRASPING VIA PREDICTIVE CONTACT
CONTROL
The transition from baseline control to a predictive frame-
work is motivated by three critical limitations: (1) the object’s
stiffness may vary dynamically due to nonuniform material
compliance; (2) the PID loop ignores the coupled contact
dynamics (finter); and (3) the PID control design does not take
constraints into consideration, where overshoot or oscillation
can cause constraint violations. To address these deficiencies,
this section introduces an integrated model learning and
predictive control framework.
A. Online Interaction Dynamics Learning
The grasping constraints are identified online, and the
object is already in the grasping phase. Therefore, the charac-
terization of the interaction dynamics must also be achieved
online. This is based on the consideration that finter is a shared
dynamic property between the gripper and the object under
the current specific contact topology. Instead of traditional
system identification, we consider a data-driven method.
To identify the unmodeled interaction dynamics without
relying on precise analytical models, a multi-frequency sine-
excitation grasping force reference (subject to the identified
constraints) is injected into the system under PID control.

A. Experimental Setup
The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 5. The robotic arm
(model Piper from Agilex Robotics) is a 6-DOF manipulator
equipped with a two-finger gripper; see Fig. 5(a). The gripper
fingers are driven by a single DC motor through position
control. The robotic arm communicates with the host PC via
a CAN (Controller Area Network) bus, and the feedback and
commands are compiled into standard ROS (Robot Operating
System) topics. The gripper dimensions are dfinger = 70 mm
and bfinger = 22 mm, and the maximum width between the
two fingers is 100 mm. The tactile sensing system is shown
in Fig. 5(b), following the design presented in Sec. III.
The FSR sensor (model FSR 402) is manufactured by
Interlink Electronics, and its active circular sensing region
has a diameter of 12.7 mm. Its thickness is less than 0.5
mm. The overall thickness of the custom-made tactile sensing
module is approximately 4 mm. Measurements from each
FSR sensor are collected through a standard voltage divider
circuit with a constant resistor of 2000 Ohms using a Teensy
4.0 microcontroller. The raw analog data falls in the range
of [0, 4095]. According to the user manual, the maximum
effective force measurement is around 10 N. The raw tactile
sensing measurements are transmitted to the host machine
through a customized ROS topic and synchronized with the
robotic arm’s measurement and control signals.
The GP-based tactile sensing calibration model is trained
offline using raw tactile sensing data and force measured by
a load cell; see Fig. 5(c). We use the gripper (with the tactile
module) to squeeze the industrial load cell and collect the
data. The measurement from the load cell is treated as the
ground truth. The training dataset contains 2000 data points
selected from a larger dataset, with a depth of n = 5. The
gripper-object interaction dynamics are trained online using
a total of 1500 data points, with a depth of d = 3.
The control parameters are selected offline using only a
foam block and a 3D-printed part. Since we assume that
the object is completely unknown during experimental im-
plementation, all the aforementioned setups and parameters
are fixed as constants. The primary parameters used are
Kp = 2.55 × 10−5, Ki = 1 × 10−6, and Kd = 3 × 10−6
for the PID controller; mmax = 0.5 kg, FContact,max = 5 N,

VII. DISCUSSIONS
In this section, we present results for several special
cases. We further analyze the computational cost, parameter
selection, and limitations of the framework.
A. Special Cases
1) Delicate Objects: For delicate objects, secure and gen-
tle grasping is of critical importance. For instance, tofu is
an extremely soft material, and human grasping frequently
results in structural damage. As reported in [9], computa-
tionally expensive vision-tactile array sensing and control
strategies were unable to successfully grasp soft objects such
as boiled eggs or raw meat. Here, we consider the even more
challenging tasks of grasping an extremely soft marshmallow
and raw tofu.
Fig. 12(a) and Fig. 12(b) show the robotic grasping of
a marshmallow (m = 2.21 g, κstatic = 89 N/m) and raw
tofu (m = 38 g, κstatic = 20 N/m). In Table I, the evaluated
stiffnesses of the tofu and marshmallow are the lowest among
all tested objects and fall below the lower limit (κmin = 200),
which violates assumption A2. Nevertheless, we demonstrate
that the proposed approach can still successfully handle these
delicate objects. Because the tactile sensor requires sufficient
deformation to trigger activation, the marshmallow must be
compressed somewhat firmly to be lifted (see the actual width
versus measurement width in Table I). This explains why
the evaluated stiffness of the marshmallow exceeds that of
the tofu. To lift the extremely soft and light marshmallow,
a force of 0.7 N is required; see Fig. 12(a). Similarly,
for the tofu, the minimum grasping force is 0.5 N. For

The proposed control under robust constraint enforcement

object handling and grasping. To show the effectiveness

introduce disturbances. As shown in Fig. 14, when the
grasping force reaches the lower bound, we apply the distur-

disturbances, and the grasping force accordingly increases
to maintain contact. Conversely, under a constant grasping

C. Parameters Selections
The proposed method requires some pre-defined constants.
The effects of some constraints are validated or corrected
through online trials. For instance, the maximum force calcu-
lation in (19) depends on the user-defined parameters ϵmax, ξ,
which may generate a large force for a soft object. However,
such a calculation is corrected online through maximum force
online verification (see (19)). While out of many constants,
the slip coefficient λ1 in (16) is the most critical parameter.
A smaller λ1 indicates a riskier grasping, and a larger λ1
may result in over-conservativeness. During implementation,
we used λ1 = 0.75. To investigate these parameters on
system performance. In Fig. 15, we show the minimum
force identified online under different λ1 values for some
representative objects. For a more risky λ1, the overall lower
force bound becomes smaller. However, when the gripper-
object contact topology becomes irregular, under slip, the
contact may lose compared to a surface contact. For instance,
when λ1 = 0.65, for the rock with irregular-contact topology,
the grasping fails (most time) due to loss of contact.
Similarly, the PID control also plays a significant role. An
aggressive PID control may cause the identified constraints
to be either more conservative or more risky. Meanwhile,
under the assumption of unknown object properties, the
PID controllers cannot be designed to be object-specific.
To avoid an unrealistic estimation of the contact force
bound. During the minimum force hunting and maximum
force verification phases, the rate of the reference force is
designed to be 2%, followed by a rest period before the next
increment/decrement. This reduces the influence of the PID
control’s transient performance.
D. Computational Cost
By leveraging computationally efficient mechatronics and
modern computing resources, the computational cost of this
work is mitigated through the following three aspects.
First, compared to [9], [14], [16], [35], the proposed
tactile-sensing mechatronics does not require an array of
tactile sensor units. For each finger, only a single single-
channel unit is needed, which significantly reduces latency
and the overhead associated with lower-level raw data pro-
cessing and transmission. In [14], multiple three-dimensional
accelerometers are used to construct a tactile sensor array,
yielding a fingertip force measurement frequency of only
5 Hz. In contrast, the tactile force measurement in this work
achieves a real-time sampling frequency exceeding 1000 Hz.
Second, compared to visuo-tactile sensors, the proposed
tactile-sensing system eliminates the need for computation-
ally expensive computer vision or image processing. In [26],
video processing techniques are used to handle tactile sens-
ing. As a result, the control frequency of the MPC reaches
only 1 Hz, which is inadequate for real-time force control.
In [9], a visuo-tactile system is employed for robotic grasp-
ing. To reduce computational cost, state prediction is replaced
by a neural network model; nevertheless, the overall control
frequency remains low at 50 Hz.
The primary computational burden of the proposed ap-
proach stems from online interaction dynamics learning and
state prediction within the nonlinear MPC. A large data depth
d and dataset size M (we set M = 1500) in (22) place heavy
demands on online model training. In MPC optimization,
both mean and variance evaluations are required, and con-
straints vary over the prediction horizon. To address this, we
deploy GP model training, evaluation, and MPC optimization
on a GPU by leveraging its parallel computing capabilities.
Consequently, a real-time control frequency of 100 Hz is
achieved with no observable delay. The linear MPC-based
control can also be executed on a resource-constrained ma-
chine (Intel Core i7-1260P x 16) at 50 Hz, albeit with a
smaller dataset size and a 10-second latency during GP model
training. Importantly, under the ROS protocol, the grasping
force command from the preceding step persists during this
10-second delay, preventing system failure. Therefore, the
proposed method remains feasible even in computationally
constrained setups.
E. Limitations
There are several limitations of the proposed approach
compared to the work in the literature as follows.
• One of the major limitations of the proposed work is
the assumption that the object initially resides within
the gripper’s workspace. Under A1, the proposed frame-
work does not require a vision system for object search.
However, in practice, the object may lie outside the
workspace of the gripper, necessitating active object
search.
• Furthermore, for irregular objects, the grasp location on
the object is critical. Even when the object is within the
gripper’s workspace, contact should be established at a
location that maximizes the contact area. The contact
area may shrink during online minimum force hunting,
potentially leading to a loss of contact. Currently, the
proposed algorithm lacks a module to adjust contact
locations dynamically.
• Compared to [9], the tactile sensing module measures
only normal forces, leaving friction forces and torques


19
unknown. Under this setup, the identified minimum
force depends on user-defined parameters (e.g., λ1),
which may not closely reflect the ground truth.
Future work should focus on addressing these limitations.
In particular, to relax A1, tactile fingers could be utilized
to search for the object and reconstruct its three-dimensional
surface contour. Additionally, the initial contact establishment
can be optimized.
VIII. CONCLUSIONS
This work proposes a tactile sensing-based dynamics
learning and control framework for the robotic grasping
of unknown objects. The framework encompasses multiple
modules, including compact intelligent tactile mechatron-
ics, object property probing, constraint identification, online
grasping dynamics learning, and predictive control. The
tactile sensing system measures gripper-object interaction
forces, while the object probing module, resembling human
grasping behavior, actively evaluates the object’s stiffness,
size, slip, and deformation. Fundamentally, we identify case-
specific intrinsic constraints on the grasping force to achieve
secure and gentle grasping of unknown objects. The in-
teraction dynamics are formally learned online using GP
regression, and predictive control is applied to dynamically
regulate the grasping force while enforcing these constraints.
The system safely grasps objects as delicate as raw tofu
and marshmallows, as well as objects as rigid as metal
blocks. Crucially, the proposed grasping system operates
without offline training for object identification, complex
tactile mechatronics, or vision systems, enabling computa-
tionally efficient control for real-time applications. Extensive
experimental results validate the proposed framework across
diverse scenarios. In the future, we plan to pursue two major
directions: leveraging the tactile fingers to reconstruct 3D
surface contours for optimizing contact establishment, and
extending the proposed approach to high-DoF multi-fingered
dexterous hands integrated with multi-axis tactile arrays to
map complex shear forces and torsional sliding moments.
