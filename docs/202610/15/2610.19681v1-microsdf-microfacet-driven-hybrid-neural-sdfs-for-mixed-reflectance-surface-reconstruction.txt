MicroSDF: Microfacet-Driven Hybrid Neural SDFs for
Mixed-Reflectance Surface Reconstruction
Lejia Ye , Yuhua Xu, Yulan Guo , Senior Member, IEEE, Lian Xu , Xiaohu Zhang
Abstract—Accurate 3D reconstruction in real-world environ-
ments remains a significant challenge due to the coexistence of re-
flective and non-reflective surfaces, which pose distinct modeling
demands. Existing methods often treat these surface types sepa-
rately, limiting their generalizability and physical plausibility. To
bridge this gap, we propose MicroSDF, a novel neural implicit
framework that facilitates geometry and reflectance modeling
through microfacet theory. Our approach incorporates three core
innovations: (i) a microfacet-guided geometry model that extracts
multi-scale surface normals (macroscopic and microfacet) from a
signed distance field (SDF), regularized by a proposed microfacet
normal consistency loss to enforce physically plausible surface
orientations; (ii) an enhanced dual-branch color model, where the
specular branch leverages the microfacet normals to model high-
frequency reflectance, and the vanilla branch, unlike prior works,
uses reflection direction (instead of viewing direction) to better
model diffuse and low-frequency specular components; and (iii) a
detection-guided color blending strategy that adaptively fuses the
color outputs based on reflection priors, providing more physi-
cally intuitive blending than implicitly learned blending weights.
Combined with a tailored multi-stage optimization scheme, the
proposed MicroSDF achieves robust and high-fidelity recon-
struction across reflective and non-reflective surfaces. Extensive
experiments on DTU, Shiny Blender, Ref-NeRF, and DeepVoxels
datasets demonstrate state-of-the-art performance, establishing a
new direction for physically grounded neural reconstruction.
Index Terms—Neural surface reconstruction, Surface render-
ing, Microfacet theory.

Current reconstruction paradigms face significant limitations
when applied to reflective surfaces. For example, traditional
Multi-View Stereo (MVS) methods rely on the assumption of
multi-view consistency, which is commonly violated in reflec-
tive regions due to their strong view-dependent appearance.
While recent neural implicit representations, especially neural
Signed Distance Field (SDF) methods [3], [7], [8], have signif-
icantly advanced 3D reconstruction, their underlying rendering
formulations often neglect the physical properties of highly
reflective surfaces. Consequently, these methods struggle to
disentangle view-dependent appearance from true geometry in
reflective regions, leading to inaccurate reconstructions.
The difficulty of handling reflective surfaces is also encoun-
tered in inverse rendering, which aims to infer the scene’s
geometry, material properties, and lighting from observed
images. This is, however, fundamentally ill-posed: multiple
combinations of material parameters, lighting, and shape can
produce the same observed color, making decomposition from
color supervision solely inherently ambiguous [4], [9], [10].
To address this, recent inverse rendering approaches [11]–
[13] have incorporated physically grounded reflectance mod-
els, enabling more plausible and interpretable rendering re-
sults. While our goal differs, these advances raise a natural

question: can physical principles be similarly integrated into
neural implicit reconstruction frameworks to better handle
reflective surfaces? Inspired by this, we turn to microfacet
theory [14], a foundational model in graphics that captures
surface reflectance behavior through statistical distributions of
microscopic surface normals. A key insight from this theory
is the distinction between reflective and non-reflective regions:
specular regions exhibit small variance in microfacet normals,
while non-specular regions show large variance.
Building upon this insight, we propose MicroSDF, a unified
neural SDF framework that explicitly embeds microfacet-
inspired priors into the reconstruction process, for handling
both reflective and non reflective surfaces. It inherits the
coarse-to-fine learning paradigm of recent neural SDF methods
[3], [4], [7], [8], [15]–[18] , with sequential geometry and
color networks. More specifically, MicroSDF introduces three
key innovations: (i) Microfacet-guided geometry modeling.
We sample local surface normals from a finer-scale geometry
hierarchy around each query point and concatenate them
with the numerical normal at the current level. To ensure
physically consistent reflectance behavior, we introduce a
microfacet normal consistency loss that encourages alignment
between the microfacet normals and local surface normal in
reflective regions, enabling coherent reflection directions. (ii)
Microfacet-driven color modeling. We employ a dual-branch
color network, as illustrated in Fig. 1, in contrast to prior
methods [5], [6], our specular branch incorporates microfacet
normals as additional inputs to model high-frequency reflective
behavior, and the vanilla branch uses the reflection direction
as input to better capture diffuse and low-frequency reflective
effects. This design ensures that these two branches focus
on complementary aspects of color modeling. (iii) Detection-
guided color blending. While the specular branch enables
reflectance modeling, correctly identifying where to apply it
remains challenging. Prior works [5] have used implicitly
learned blending weights from an auxiliary MLP, which often
fail to reliably identify specular highlights from true geometry.
In contrast, we propose a detection-guided blending module
that leverages per-pixel specular likelihoods from a pretrained
detector (e.g., StableDelight [19]) to guide the fusion of color
predictions from the two branches. This enables physically
intuitive color blending, reducing artifacts and improving
reconstruction robustness across mixed-material scenes.
Through these designs, the proposed MicroSDF achieves
robust and high-fidelity reconstruction of reflective and non-
reflective surfaces within a unified framework. The proposed
method consistently improves performance in challenging re-
flective regions while preserving accuracy on standard scenes.
In summary, our main contributions are fourfold:
1) We propose MicroSDF, the first neural implicit surface
reconstruction framework that explicitly integrates mi-
crofacet theory in a principled manner. The proposed
MicroSDF offers a unified solution to robustly handle
complex real-world scenes with both reflective and non-
reflective surfaces.
2) We propose a microfacet normal consistency loss that
aligns microfacet and local surface normals in specular
regions, enhancing physically meaningful geometry that
supports accurate view-dependent appearance.
3) We propose an enhanced dual-branch color model that
incorporates microfacet normals into the specular branch
to capture high-frequency reflections, and uses reflection
direction in the vanilla branch to model diffuse and low-
frequency reflections. Guided by a detection-based re-
flectance prior, this design enables robust and physically
intuitive color blending.
4) The proposed MicroSDF achieves the state-of-the-art
performance across diverse benchmarks, including DTU,
Shiny Blender, Ref-NeRF, and DeepVoxels, showing
the effectiveness on both reflective and non-reflective
surfaces.
II. RELATED WORKS
A. Multi-view Stereo
Multi-view stereo (MVS) aims to reconstruct fine-grained
scene geometry from multi-view images. Traditional MVS
methods can be classified into four categories based on the
output scene representation: volumetric-based methods, mesh-
based methods, point cloud-based methods, and depth map-
based methods [20]. Among them, depth map-based methods
[21]–[24] are the most flexible, estimating depth maps for each
view by utilizing photometric consistency between reference
and neighboring images, then fusing all the depth maps into
dense point clouds. A typical example is COLMAP [21],
[22], which employs a robust patch-based matching strategy
to estimate depth maps and then fuses them into a complete
point cloud representation. Surface reconstruction methods,
such as screened Poisson Surface Reconstruction [25], are then
employed to reconstruct surfaces from the point clouds.
The advent of deep learning has transformed MVS by
leveraging data-driven techniques for feature extraction and
depth estimation. MVSNet [26] pioneered deep learning in
MVS by using CNNs to construct 3D cost volumes and regress
depth maps. Inspired by MVSNet, a series of outstanding
works have emerged [27]–[31]. MVSNet++ [27] improved
upon MVSNet by introducing feature pyramid structures
and instance normalization, fusing multi-level information
for accurate 3D point localization and generating smooth
depth maps through tailored loss functions and a curriculum
learning framework. Subsequent works like RA-MVSNet [28]
and ET-MVSNet [29] enhanced accuracy through recurrent
aggregation and epipolar constraints, while GeoMVSNet [32]
incorporated geometric priors for better depth prediction. NR-
MVSNet [30] further refined the coarse-to-fine strategy with
a normal consistency-based depth hypotheses module and
a depth refinement module with reliable attention, produc-
ing smoother and more accurate depth maps, particularly
in texture-less and repetitive-texture regions. More recently,
Transformer-based methods, such as MVSFormer [33], MVS-
Former++ [34], and EI-MVSNet [31], have improved perfor-
mance by modeling long-range view dependencies, with EI-
MVSNet leveraging epipolar-guided cost volume construction
and an interval-aware depth estimation module to enhance
depth estimation accuracy. However, these deep learning-based
MVS methods typically require large training datasets.

While the above traditional and learning-based MVS meth-
ods have shown promising results in general scenarios, they
face fundamental limitations when dealing with reflective
surfaces. The core photometric consistency assumption under-
lying these methods breaks down for glossy materials, making
accurate reconstruction challenging.
B. Neural Implicit Surface Based Reconstruction
In recent years, representation methods such as Neural
Radiance Fields (NeRF)
[35] and 3D Gaussian Splatting
(3DGS) [36] have revolutionized traditional 3D reconstruction,
advancing tasks such as novel view rendering [37], [38],
human performance capture [39], human pose and shape
estimation [40], object segmentation [41] and Multi-View 3D
detection [42].
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [36] offers high rendering
quality and efficiency and has been widely adopted for novel
view synthesis. However, achieving accurate geometry remains
challenging because it lacks an explicit surface representa-
tion, which makes reliable surface extraction and geometry-
dependent downstream tasks difficult. To improve geometric
fidelity, several recent methods align Gaussian primitives with
the underlying surface to enable surface extraction, includ-
ing 2DGS [43], BBSplat [44], SuGaR [45], GOF [46] and
Rade-GS [47]. However, their geometric accuracy in prac-
tice remains generally inferior to that of neural SDF based
methods. Several other variants of 3D Gaussian splatting [48]–
[50] have integrated domain-specific physical principles into
the 3DGS framework for different objectives. For example,
WaterSplatting [48] combines 3DGS with an underwater
light transport model, explicitly accounting for absorption
and scattering to enable fast underwater scene reconstruction.
RainyGS [49] incorporates physically based raindrop and
shallow-water simulations to synthesize dynamic rain effects
such as water accumulation and surface waves in reconstructed
scenes. LITA-GS [50] leverages Kubelka–Munk theory to
introduce illumination-invariant physical priors, thereby fa-
cilitating illumination-agnostic novel view synthesis under
challenging lighting conditions. In contrast to these 3DGS
based methods that are tailored to specific tasks such as
underwater reconstruction, rain synthesis and illumination-
robust rendering, our work focuses on surface reconstruction
and geometric accuracy by adopting an implicit SDF rep-
resentation and employing microfacet theory as a geometric
regularizer, with the explicit goal of achieving high-fidelity
surfaces and accurate geometry.

Numerous studies [18], [55]–[59] have demonstrated the
potential for mutual enhancement through the integration of
NeRF with traditional MVS approaches. Among these, several
methods [18], [58], [59] have incorporated external geometric
priors, such as depth maps, point clouds, and normal maps,
to provide intermediate supervision for SDF-based networks,
thereby improving geometric reconstruction quality. Notably,
[18] identified a fundamental bias between rendered colors
and geometry, and theoretically established that direct su-
pervision of the zero-level set of SDF networks using point
cloud information offers unbiased advantages and facilitates
neural implicit surface learning. However, the effectiveness
of these approaches heavily relies on the quality of external
supervision. Both externally supervised geometric information
and native NeRF techniques face challenges in reconstructing
reflective surfaces, as NeRF’s view-dependent color prediction
mechanism, while effective for diffuse materials, fails to
accurately model the complex light interactions and view-
dependent effects of reflective surfaces. Furthermore, external
supervision often relies on multi-view consistency assump-
tions, which may not hold in real-world scenarios.
C. Reflective Surface Reconstruction
Recent research in inverse rendering
[13], [60]–[64] has
increasingly embraced physics-based rendering models to ad-
dress the challenge of reflectance interpretation. While these
methods employ various basis functions to approximate the
rendering equation, they primarily focus on improving ren-
dering quality, without explicit investigation and evaluation of
the geometry. Notably, Neural Microfacet Fields (NMF) [64]
shares a similar high-level motivation with our work: both ex-
plicitly leverage microfacet-based physical modeling as priors
to reduce the ambiguity of learning from images, particularly
for reflective surfaces. However, NMF and our MicroSDF
are designed for different primary tasks: NMF targets vol-
umetric inverse rendering with explicit material–illumination
decomposition, whereas MicroSDF focuses on high-precision
geometric reconstruction. In terms of how microfacets are
incorporated, NMF embeds microfacet modeling into a vol-
umetric representation by assigning local normals and mi-
crofacet BRDF parameters to every volumetric sample along
the ray, and performs surface-style Monte Carlo reflection
integration to jointly recover materials and lighting and en-
able relighting, with geometry represented implicitly by a
density field rather than an explicit SDF surface. In contrast,
MicroSDF introduces microfacets only on the explicit SDF
surface, defining microfacet normals at the surface that are
used both as inputs to a specular branch to capture reflective
appearance and as a consistency prior that regularizes surface
orientation, thereby prioritizing accurate geometry recovery in
scenes with mixed reflective and non-reflective regions without
performing explicit material–illumination decomposition.

III. THE PROPOSED MICROSDF
In this section, we introduce the proposed MicroSDF, a
unified neural surface reconstruction framework that integrates
microfacet theory to jointly model geometry and reflectance
for both reflective and non-reflective surfaces. An overview
of the proposed framework is illustrated in Fig. 2. This
section elaborates the overall design of our method, beginning
with rendering preliminaries (Section III-A), followed by two
core components: (i) microfacet-guided geometry modeling
(Section III-B), which extracts physically consistent surface
normals from a signed distance field, and (ii) microfacet-
driven color modeling (Section III-C), which employs a dual-
branch network with a proposed detection-guided blending
process. We also describe our multi-stage optimization strategy
(Section III-D), which enables progressive refinement and
ensures physically faithful reconstruction.

B. Microfacet-Guided Geometry Modeling
1) Geometry Network: The geometry network, parameter-
ized by ΘG, is designed for capturing the underlying 3D
structure of the scene. ΘG takes as input the sampled 3D
coordinates x and the external point cloud P , and outputs the
corresponding surface normal vectors n, geometric features
F , and SDF values. To encode the spatial properties of the
scene, the 3D coordinates x are first fed into an iNGP,
which produces geometric features F . These features are then
further passed through an MLP to predict the SDF value. To
facilitate effective training, especially in the early stages, the
external point cloud P is used to directly supervise the SDF
predictions. The resulting outputs provide essential geometric
context for the subsequent color modeling stage.
2) Dual-Scale Normal Extraction: Accurately modeling
surface reflectance, especially specular components, requires
not only capturing the overall surface shape but also account-
ing for fine-grained normal variations. To this end, we propose
to extract dual-scale normals that compute both macroscopic
and microfacet normals for each sampled 3D point. This dual-
scale formulation enables our model to capture both global
surface structure and fine-grained normal variations, which are
critical for consistent geometry reconstruction and physically
plausible specular reflection modeling.
Given a sampled 3D point xi, two types of normals are
computed as follows: (i) Macroscopic normals n are esti-
mated via central differences along coordinate axes with a
decreasing step size ϵ during training. For example, the x-
directional gradient is computed as:

2) Detection-guided blending: Handling reflective and non-
reflective surfaces within a unified framework requires not
only accurate modeling of each component but also precise
spatial allocation of where these components should domi-
nate. A key challenge in dual-branch color networks lies in
ambiguity when decomposing color into low-frequency and
high-frequency specular components.
To address this, we propose a detection-guided color blend-
ing mechanism that leverages external, physically interpretable
priors to control the blending between the vanilla branch (re-
sponsible for low-frequency specular effects) and the specular
branch (capturing high-frequency specular highlights). Unlike
UniSDF [5] and AniSDF [6], which infer blending weights
via an auxiliary MLP, making the fusion process susceptible
to dataset bias and prone to misidentifying specular cues, we
employ StableDelight [19], a diffusion-based specular detector,
to per-pixel specular confidence maps directly from the input
images. These detection scores encode the likelihood of each
pixel exhibiting high-frequency specular reflection, thus pro-
viding explicit, spatially varying blending weights grounded
in physical intuition. The final pixel color is computed as:

where Spec denotes specular score.
D. Optimization
Our optimization process consists of three key components:
geometry initialization, a multi-stage training strategy, and our
loss function design.
1) Geometry Initialization: To provide a reliable starting
point for the geometry network ΘG, we initialize the SDF
Φ(x) using dense point cloud information obtained from tra-
ditional 3D reconstruction algorithms (e.g., OpenMVS [68]).
These point clouds help constrain surface positions and iden-
tify empty regions in 3D space.
Inspired by the regularization approach in SIREN [69], we
encourage the SDF values of surface points to approach 0.
However, unlike SIREN [69], which incorporates normal con-

2) Four-stage Training Strategy: Although the effectiveness
of coarse-to-fine training has been well demonstrated, our
experiments show that, with sufficient training at the initial
level, certain intermediate levels can be skipped. This enables
a more direct transition to the finest level, where analytical
gradients can replace numerical ones for improved efficiency.
Based on this insight, we propose a four-stage training strategy
comprising: (i) geometry initialization (Geometry Init.), (ii)
thorough training at the first level (1st Level), (iii) progres-
sive coarse-to-fine processing (Coarse-to-Fine), and (iv) final
training at the finest level (Finest Level).
This training strategy significantly reduces the total number
of training iterations compared to conventional coarse-to-fine
training approaches, while yielding superior quantitative re-
sults (see Table VI in Supplementary Material). Our microfacet
sampling process is mainly performed during the thorough
training at the first level and the progressive coarse-to-fine
processing stages.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Implementation Settings
Datasets. Following prior works [3], [5], [7], [10], [16], we
evaluate the proposed method on three widely used bench-
marks: (i) DTU [75]: The DTU multi-view dataset contains
15 real-world scenes, each captured from 49 or 64 viewpoints
with high-resolution images. Each scene contains objects
with diverse materials and complex geometries, including
both reflectance and non-reflectance surfaces. DTU provides
ground-truth 3D models and camera trajectories, making it a
standard benchmark for quantitative evaluation of point cloud
reconstruction.(ii) Shiny Blender [65]: The Shiny Blender
dataset is a synthetic benchmark consisting of 6 reflective
objects rendered under controlled settings. We use it to provide
additional quantitative evaluation on reflective surfaces. (iii)
Ref-NeRF [65]: The Ref-NeRF dataset is designed to evaluate
reconstruction quality for reflective objects in uncontrolled
real-world scenarios. It includes three challenging scenes,
i.e., “sedan”, “garden spheres”, and “toycar”, each featuring
multi-view images of highly reflective surfaces. Ref-NeRF
specifically tests an algorithm’s robustness to specular reflec-
tions. (iv) DeepVoxels [76]: To evaluate performance in more
realistic settings, we select two scenes (a glossy sphere and
a coffee cup) from this dataset. Both scenes were captured
with handheld cameras and processed via COLMAP [21], [22],
featuring reflective surfaces under non-ideal conditions.

geometry (e.g., DTU dataset), we employ the Chamfer Dis-

accuracy of the reconstructed point cloud. Following the

aligns with the visible ground truth (lower is better). We also
evaluate surface orientation with the MAE between rendered
normals and ground-truth normals over the valid foreground
mask. Consistent with Ref-NeuS, ACC is not reported for
the ball and teapot scenes. For datasets lacking ground-truth
geometry (e.g., Ref-NeRF and DeepVoxels), we conduct a
qualitative evaluation. We focus on the preservation of fine
geometric details, especially for reflective surfaces, such as the
accurate reconstruction of depressions, as well as the overall
reconstruction plausibility in both reflective and non-reflective
areas.

V. CONCLUSION
In this work, we presented MicroSDF, a novel framework
for 3D reconstruction of scenes with both reflective and non-
reflective surfaces. Grounded in physical principles, MicroSDF
integrates microfacet theory to guide both geometric and color
modeling. Specifically, we introduce a microfacet consistency
loss to enforce physically meaningful surface geometry for
reflective regions, while leveraging microfacet normals to
facilitate specular color learning. This is further enhanced
by a detection-guided color blending strategy, which provides
external supervision for physically plausible color modeling.
Together, these components enable our framework to effec-
tively handle both reflective and non-reflective surfaces in a
unified manner.
Extensive experiments across diverse benchmark datasets
validate the effectiveness and robustness of our framework.
On the DTU dataset, which mixes reflective and non-reflective
surfaces, MicroSDF achieves state-of-the-art performance with
an average Chamfer Distance of 0.61, outperforming existing
methods. On the synthetic reflective Shiny Blender benchmark,
our method also attains state-of-the-art quantitative results,
demonstrating its strong capability in handling highly specular
scenes. For real-world reflective datasets such as Ref-NeRF
and DeepVoxels, we provide qualitative evaluations that show
high-fidelity reconstruction of reflective regions and improved
surface consistency. These results indicate that MicroSDF
generalizes well across both controlled synthetic environments
and real-world scenarios. Moreover, the proposed integration
of microfacet-driven priors offers a principled way to incor-
porate physically grounded reflectance modeling into neural
reconstruction frameworks. This idea is potentially compatible
with recent 3D Gaussian Splatting approaches for improved
reconstruction of reflective and specular surfaces, which we
consider a promising direction for future work.
