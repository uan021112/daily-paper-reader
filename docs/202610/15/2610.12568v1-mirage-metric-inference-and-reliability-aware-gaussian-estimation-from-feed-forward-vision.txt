ABSTRACT
Feed-forward 3D priors enable high-quality reconstruction from image streams
but often lack reliable metric scale and suffer from long-term trajectory drift. In
contrast, LiDAR–inertial estimation provides metrically accurate motion but lacks
the image-space consistency needed for high-fidelity reconstruction. We propose
MIRAGE, which anchors feed-forward visual geometry to LiDAR–IMU metric
motion and regulates online pose corrections according to correspondence support
and cross-modal agreement, jointly enabling accurate metric pose estimation and
high-fidelity Gaussian reconstruction. Experiments on Oxford Spires, M2DGR
and INS show that MIRAGE achieves accurate metric trajectory estimation, out-
performing COLMAP, a widely used but computationally expensive reference for
vision-only benchmarks. Motivated by the scarcity of long-horizon monocular
benchmarks for large-scale indoor reconstruction, we further build a multimodal
data-collection platform and use MIRAGE to establish accurate 6-DoF reference
trajectories for a new vision-only benchmark. We benchmark recent monocular
large-scale reconstruction methods on this dataset, providing a systematic evalua-
tion of their performance in long-horizon indoor scenes.

INTRODUCTION
Recent advances in 3D scene representation and camera pose estimation have substantially improved
the quality and practicality of image-based reconstruction. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) (Kerbl
et al., 2023) represents scenes with optimizable anisotropic Gaussian primitives and an efficient dif-
ferentiable rasterizer, enabling high-fidelity real-time novel-view synthesis. In parallel, feed-forward
geometric models (Leroy et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a;b) have made substantial progress in jointly
inferring camera poses and scene geometry from unposed images. These advances have further
driven the development of increasingly capable online tracking, mapping, and on-the-fly reconstruc-
tion systems (Matsuki et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024; Keetha et al., 2024; Meuleman et al., 2025;
Wu et al., 2025). More recent methods combine SLAM frameworks with data-driven feed-forward
priors to further improve pose estimation and reconstruction quality in streaming scenarios (Li et al.,
2026b; Ren et al., 2026). However, purely visual reconstruction remains subject to global similarity
ambiguity, while accumulated errors in learned geometry and pose estimation can lead to scale and
motion drift over long sequences. These limitations highlight the need for an explicit metric anchor
to achieve both high-fidelity reconstruction and a physically grounded trajectory.
LiDAR–IMU sensing provides the metric scale and motion constraints missing from purely visual
reconstruction. However, simply introducing a metric anchor does not resolve how feed-forward
visual geometry should influence online pose refinement. Visual priors can improve local alignment
and reconstruction quality, yet unreliable corrections may compromise metric consistency, while
overly suppressing them can forfeit useful image-space refinement. Since the reliability of visual
corrections varies with correspondence support and cross-modal agreement, a fixed fusion strategy
is insufficient. The key challenge is therefore to selectively exploit feed-forward visual geometry
while preserving a metrically consistent trajectory.
We propose MIRAGE1, an on-the-fly reconstruction framework that combines feed-forward visual
geometry with LiDAR–IMU sensing to jointly achieve metrically accurate pose estimation and high-
fidelity Gaussian reconstruction. MIRAGE uses LiDAR–IMU motion as a metric anchor and selec-
tively incorporates visual pose corrections according to correspondence support and cross-modal
agreement, retaining useful visual refinement without compromising metric consistency. Given
the stabilized metric trajectory, MIRAGE further leverages image residuals and LiDAR geome-
try for structure-aware Gaussian refinement, improving reconstruction quality while keeping the
camera poses fixed. Experiments on Oxford Spires (Tao et al., 2026), M2DGR (Yin et al., 2022)
and INS (Deng et al., 2025a) demonstrate that MIRAGE achieves accurate and metrically consis-
tent trajectory estimation while maintaining high-fidelity, photorealistic Gaussian reconstruction.
Notably, MIRAGE achieves higher trajectory accuracy than COLMAP, despite COLMAP being a
computationally expensive method widely used to establish reference trajectories for vision-only
benchmarks. This demonstrates MIRAGE’s potential to provide reliable reference trajectories for
benchmarking vision-only reconstruction methods.
Long-horizon benchmarks for monocular reconstruction in large-scale indoor environments re-
main limited. To fill this gap, we develop a remotely operated capture platform with hardware-
synchronized cameras, LiDAR, and IMU, and collect ten long-horizon sequences across diverse
indoor scenes, as shown in Figure 1. MIRAGE is then used to establish accurate 6-DoF reference
trajectories for these sequences, forming a vision-only benchmark for large-scale indoor reconstruc-
tion. We further evaluate recent long-horizon monocular reconstruction methods on this benchmark.
Our contributions are threefold:
• Reliability-gated metric trajectory inference. We anchor feed-forward visual geometry to
LiDAR–IMU metric motion and treat visual pose updates as correction proposals, selectively
committing them according to correspondence support and cross-modal consistency to improve
image-space alignment without compromising metric trajectory accuracy.
• Pose-fixed structure-aware Gaussian refinement. We decouple scene refinement from trajec-
tory estimation by fixing the metric camera poses and using image residuals together with LiDAR
depth evidence to diagnose and repair oversized, misplaced, and missing primitives, improving
geometric detail and appearance without perturbing the recovered trajectory.

RELATED WORK
2.1
VISUAL POSE ESTIMATION AND STREAMING GAUSSIAN RECONSTRUCTION
Learned geometry models (Wang et al., 2024; Leroy et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a; Lin et al.,
2025b) provide dense priors for pose estimation and reconstruction. Recent methods incorporate
these priors into visual tracking and global optimization (Murai et al., 2025; Maggio et al., 2026;
Maggio & Carlone, 2026), exploit geometric context for reconstruction (Chen et al., 2026; Xie et al.,
2026), or combine feed-forward predictions with global structure-from-motion optimization (Deng
et al., 2026; Pan et al., 2026).
Gaussian SLAM (Matsuki et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024; Keetha et al., 2024) extends 3DGS (Kerbl
et al., 2023) to incremental tracking and mapping. Other approaches predict Gaussians from image
streams (Chen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025), optimize scenes incrementally (Meuleman et al., 2025;
2026), or combine learned priors with online pose and map optimization (Lin et al., 2025a; Li et al.,
2026b; Jiang et al., 2026; Ren et al., 2026). Monocular reconstruction nevertheless remains scale-
ambiguous, and learned priors do not guarantee metric accuracy or drift-free trajectories over long
traversals.
2.2
LIDAR–INERTIAL ESTIMATION AND MULTIMODAL GAUSSIAN MAPPING
LiDAR–inertial estimators (Shan et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2026) recover metric mo-
tion from range and inertial constraints, while visual–LiDAR systems (Shan et al., 2021; Lin &
Zhang, 2022; Zheng et al., 2022; 2025; Zhao et al., 2025) incorporate image observations. Mul-
timodal Gaussian mapping (Hong et al., 2024; Lang et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025; Jiang et al.,
2024; Deng et al., 2025b; Cui et al., 2024) uses metric poses and LiDAR geometry for initializa-
tion and photometric refinement. Recent methods further incorporate Gaussian-map observations
into localization (Hong et al., 2025; Lang et al., 2026) or feed-forward priors into estimation and
mapping (Wang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a). MIRAGE regulates pose corrections using visual and
geometric evidence to support both metric accuracy and rendering quality.
2.3
BENCHMARKS FOR MULTIMODAL LOCALIZATION AND RECONSTRUCTION
Visual benchmarks (Sturm et al., 2012; Dai et al., 2017; Knapitsch et al., 2017) support tracking
or reconstruction evaluation without mobile 3D LiDAR streams. ScanNet++ (Yeshwanth et al.,
2023) combines high-resolution RGB, surveyed laser geometry, and mobile RGB-D/IMU data. Mo-
bile datasets vary in sensing and supervision: MulRan (Kim et al., 2020) lacks camera images,
Hilti-Oxford (Zhang et al., 2023) uses grayscale cameras, and FAST-LIVO2’s self-collected se-
quences (Zheng et al., 2025) lack pose ground truth. RGB–LiDAR–IMU datasets (Tao et al., 2026;
Yin et al., 2022; Jiao et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024) cover diverse environments
and platforms, while recent multimodal benchmarks (Deng et al., 2025a; Lang et al., 2026) provide
reference geometry and poses for localization and rendering evaluation. Nevertheless, synchronized
RGB–LiDAR–IMU benchmarks with full 6-DoF reference trajectories over long, object-rich indoor
traversals remain scarce. Our extended captures in laboratories, offices, and dining spaces address
this gap, supporting joint evaluation of trajectory accuracy and downstream Gaussian reconstruction.

3.2
POSE ESTIMATION
We anchor visual pose estimation to LiDAR–IMU motion through a local-to-global pipeline. Track-
ing combines dense visual correspondences with LiDAR-derived metric depth to estimate frame-to-
keyframe poses (Section 3.2.1). Local optimization refines keyframe poses using visual consistency
constraints and metric position anchors (Section 3.2.2). Loop closure selectively corrects these an-
chors, and global trajectory fusion combines them with local visual motion to produce per-image
camera poses (Section 3.2.3).

3.2.3
LOOP CLOSURE AND GLOBAL TRAJECTORY FUSION
We selectively update the metric camera anchors through observation-supported loop correction
and combine them with the locally refined visual trajectory through metric-anchored visual motion
fusion to obtain per-image camera poses.
Observation-supported loop correction. We generate initial loop candidates following (Liu et al.,
2026) and rigidly align the resulting pose-graph optimization (PGO) proposals to the current metric
trajectory without scale fitting. We assess each proposal from two complementary perspectives:
(1) geometric consistency, evaluating improvements in revisit alignment and the risk of disrupting
local structure; and (2) visual consistency, evaluating reprojection agreement with available image
correspondences. These cues determine the accepted correction magnitude. Rotation updates are
gated separately and require additional geometric benefit relative to their risk; otherwise, the current
metric orientations are retained, as shown in Figure 2. Accepted corrections update the metric
anchors and propagate to subsequent metric poses. Details are provided in Appendix A.2.3.

baseline camera trajectory. We then incorporate visual motion between these anchors, using the

3.3
GEOMETRY-AWARE GAUSSIAN RECONSTRUCTION
We reconstruct scenes using LiDAR to constrain geometry and RGB images to supervise appear-
ance. Inspired by PLANING (Jiang et al., 2026), we use a triangle–Gaussian representation that
couples explicit surface geometry with Gaussian appearance.
5


Streaming Gaussian reconstruction. For each incoming keyframe, we initialize triangle geometry
from LiDAR depth and normals, and Gaussian appearance from RGB observations. We then jointly
optimize geometry and appearance through differentiable rendering: depth and normal consistency
constrain geometry, while photometric losses supervise appearance. When camera poses are up-
dated, we transform the associated primitives accordingly to maintain map consistency. Details are
provided in Appendix A.3.
Geometry-aware Gaussian refinement. Following prior work (Li et al., 2026b; Meuleman et al.,
2025; Zhang et al., 2025), we refine the streamed scene offline to improve global scene consistency
and reconstruction accuracy. With camera poses fixed, LiDAR depth constrains geometry, while
RGB images refine appearance. During refinement, we periodically diagnose and repair regions with
high rendering error using motion-compensated LiDAR depth and multi-view consistency checks.
(1) Oversized Gaussians whose depths agree with LiDAR measurements but fail to resolve image
detail are split into smaller primitives. (2) Gaussians with persistent depth disagreement are re-
placed with primitives reinitialized using LiDAR depth and local normals. (3) At locations with
valid LiDAR depth but insufficient Gaussian coverage, we insert new primitives using depth for
position, local normals for orientation, and RGB observations for color. Alongside the photomet-
ric loss, we apply a point-to-plane penalty constructed from LiDAR depth and local normals to
newly created or reinitialized Gaussians, penalizing position errors along the normal direction while
allowing tangential adjustment. Repair criteria and loss details are provided in Appendix A.3.2.
3.4
A BENCHMARK FOR LONG-HORIZON INDOOR RECONSTRUCTION
We introduce a benchmark for long-horizon monocular streaming reconstruction. Our platform
(Fig. 1, right) integrates a wide-angle RGB camera, two LiDARs, and one LiDAR’s built-in IMU.
The dual-LiDAR configuration combines broad coverage for robust localization with dense sampling
for detailed reconstruction. Custom hardware synchronization and calibrated extrinsics align the
sensor streams. We collected sequences through low-speed teleoperation to limit motion blur and
removed blurred images through manual inspection.
The dataset comprises 10 sequences across four categories: laboratories and dining areas with nu-
merous objects and reflective surfaces, an office, and long, low-texture corridors that challenge vi-
sual tracking. It contains 71,536 RGB images at 2880×1860 resolution, spanning 59.6 minutes and
1.15 km, with trajectories approaching 200 m. We provide synchronized RGB–LiDAR–IMU data,
sensor intrinsics and extrinsics, global LiDAR reference point clouds, and MIRAGE-derived 6-DoF
reference poses. MIRAGE’s trajectory accuracy is validated on Oxford Spires (Tao et al., 2026) and
M2DGR (Yin et al., 2022). Platform details and dataset statistics appear in Appendix A.4.
We evaluate RGB-only methods on global trajectory accuracy and downstream reconstruction qual-
ity. We report absolute trajectory error (ATE) RMSE after Sim(3) alignment to the reference poses.
For reconstruction evaluation, we train standard 3DGS (Kerbl et al., 2023) with each method’s pre-
dicted poses held fixed. Held-out novel view synthesis (NVS) PSNR serves as a downstream indi-
cator of local multi-view pose consistency.

4.2
TRAJECTORY ACCURACY
MIRAGE completes all 15 sequences and achieves the lowest reported online ATE on 12 while main-
taining a moderate latency of 0.73 s/frame (Table 1). Averaged equally across sequences, its ATE is
4.22 cm versus 7.49 cm for EllipseLIO, a 43.7% reduction. On large-scale Oxford Spires traversals
and low-texture INS scenes, LingBot-Map exhibits substantial trajectory errors, while Fast-LIVO2
and Gaussian-LIC2 show severe errors or failures in our reproductions. MIRAGE maintains ATE be-
low 10 cm on every sequence. These results are consistent with its reliability-aware design: metric
anchors constrain drift, while correspondence support and cross-modal agreement regulate visual
corrections to exploit useful evidence and limit unreliable updates. With offline HBA, MIRAGE
achieves a mean ATE of 2.96 cm versus 3.31 cm for Voxel-SLAM, with lower errors on 11 of 15
sequences.

4.5
VISION-ONLY EVALUATION ON THE MIRAGE BENCHMARK
Table 4 evaluates RGB-only pose estimates through global trajectory accuracy and downstream
fixed-pose 3DGS rendering. ABot-Recon achieves the lowest mean Sim(3)-aligned ATE among
methods completing all ten sequences (1.58 m), while GLUEMAP achieves the highest mean down-
stream PSNR among visual baselines (22.80 dB). Their differing rankings suggest that global trajec-
tory agreement alone does not determine reconstruction quality: local multi-view pose consistency
also matters. Long indoor traversals, repetitive structures, and low-texture corridors challenge drift
control and correspondence estimation, exposing complementary strengths and limitations of current
visual methods.
5
CONCLUSION
We presented MIRAGE, which anchors feed-forward visual priors to LiDAR–IMU motion for met-
ric pose estimation and Gaussian reconstruction. Visual and geometric evidence guide trajectory
corrections, while LiDAR depth and image residuals drive Gaussian repair with camera poses fixed.
Experiments on Oxford Spires, M2DGR, and INS demonstrate accurate trajectories and high-fidelity
rendering. Our synchronized multimodal benchmark comprises 10 sequences across four indoor
scene categories, with 71,536 RGB images spanning 59.6 minutes and 1.15 km. With MIRAGE-
derived 6-DoF reference trajectories, sensor calibration, and global LiDAR maps, the benchmark
provides the community with a common testbed for evaluating trajectory accuracy and downstream
novel-view synthesis in monocular streaming reconstruction, supporting further advances in long-
horizon indoor reconstruction.

A.2.4
METRIC-ANCHORED VISUAL MOTION FUSION
We apply Equation 2 between consecutive accepted metric anchors with visual support, using the
following interpolation and update rules:
(1) Interpolation. Metric anchors and visual endpoint poses are interpolated at the same sensor
timestamps using linear position interpolation and shortest-arc quaternion SLERP. An interval is
fused only after both anchor estimates become available.
(2) Anchor updates. Accepted loop corrections trigger recomputation of the affected fused poses
using the updated anchors and the stored visual trajectory. The visual translation residual remains
in world coordinates, while the visual rotation residual is composed with the interpolated metric
orientation.
(3) Boundary handling. Before the right anchor becomes available, poses follow available LiDAR–
IMU odometry relative to the latest accepted anchor and are revised upon finalization. A camera-
only tail at sequence end follows visual relative motion from the last supported fused pose. Startup
frames without metric support retain their initial pose estimates.

A.2.5
OPTIONAL OFFLINE HIERARCHICAL POSE REFINEMENT
After streaming and loop correction, we initialize hierarchical LiDAR bundle adjustment (Liu et al.,

across submaps, and the resulting pose increments are transferred back to camera timestamps.

A.4.2
CALIBRATION AND TIME SYNCHRONIZATION
We estimate the inter-LiDAR extrinsics using GICP (Chen et al., 2022). Initialized from the as-
sembly drawings, a shared rigid transform is jointly refined using point clouds collected in five
distinct scenes with the platform stationary. LiDAR–camera extrinsics are estimated using FAST-
Calib (Zheng & Zhang, 2026). We acquire paired images and LiDAR recordings of a calibration
target at multiple positions, orientations, and distances to provide complementary geometric con-
straints. We qualitatively validate the estimated extrinsics by projecting RGB colors onto the LiDAR
point cloud and visually inspecting the colorization for misalignment artifacts.
Time synchronization combines Precision Time Protocol (PTP) clock synchronization with external
camera triggering. A Xingqi XQ500 synchronization unit provides the common time reference and
camera trigger signals. The LiDAR clocks synchronize to the PTP reference over Ethernet. Each
image is timestamped with its corresponding camera trigger time. We verify image timestamps by
imaging a reference clock display and comparing the displayed times with the recorded timestamps,
with observed discrepancies below 10 ms.

A.5
IMPLEMENTATION AND EVALUATION PROTOCOLS
Implementation. All experiments use NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs. LiDAR–inertial bundle adjust-
ment uses a ten-scan sliding window. Gaussian refinement runs for 15,000 steps with camera poses
and exposure transforms fixed, following the repair schedule in Appendix A.3.2. Offline 3DGS
baselines are trained for 30,000 steps.
Evaluation protocols. Trajectory evaluation reports translational ATE RMSE after SE(3) alignment
for metric methods and Sim(3) alignment for vision-only methods. Each trajectory is evaluated at
timestamps with valid reference associations. Reported means weight sequences equally. Pose-stage
comparisons use matched timestamps within each run; offline HBA results are reported separately
rather than treated as a paired stage ablation.
Rendering evaluation holds out every eighth image from Gaussian optimization. On Oxford Spires,
M2DGR, and INS, offline 3DGS uses fixed MIRAGE online poses. MIRAGE rendering results
use the Gaussian maps after refinement, without HBA pose updates; Gaussian-LIC2 results use its
online maps. Comparisons exclude Observatory 02, Church 03, and indoor sq2 because Gaussian-
LIC2 did not complete reconstruction.
On the MIRAGE benchmark, visual baselines use one-in-twelve sampling of the same preprocessed
RGB sequences, without LiDAR, IMU, or reference-pose inputs. Each method supplies only its
predicted poses to downstream 3DGS. These poses remain fixed during training, and Gaussians
are initialized from training-view correspondences using a common triangulation procedure. The
common-frame evaluation uses shared training and test images. MASt3R-SLAM is evaluated on
registered keyframes, and its rendering results are excluded from common-frame rankings. Ours
(reference) uses the same 3DGS procedure with MIRAGE reference poses. Stream PSNR reports
the complete MIRAGE pipeline after refinement on its own held-out images, rather than the shared
stride-12 test set.
For the visual ablation, camera poses are obtained by interpolating LiDAR–inertial poses to image
timestamps and applying calibrated extrinsics; RGB supervision for Gaussian reconstruction is re-
tained. Photometric and geometry-aware refinement start from the same streaming map and share
the optimization budget, fixed poses and exposure, renderer, and test views. Photometric refinement
uses only L1 and SSIM losses with a fixed primitive count.

LIMITATIONS
While MIRAGE achieves accurate metric pose estimation and high-fidelity reconstruction, several
limitations remain. First, our benchmark focuses on static indoor scenes and does not assess the
impact of dynamic objects on localization and reconstruction. Second, our data acquisition and re-
construction pipelines lack dedicated treatment of transparent and semi-transparent objects, which
can compromise geometric accuracy and rendering quality. These limitations motivate future work
on extending the benchmark to dynamic scenes and improving reconstruction of challenging mate-
rials.
