# MeshBind: Point-to-Mesh Binding for Non-Rigid Scene Flow Automatic Annotation 

Yinqi Chen 1,∗, E Li 1, Shuaijun Wang 1, Weiyuan Peng 1, Meiying Zhang 1, and Qi Hao 1

Abstract —Scene flow estimation is essential for many au-tonomous driving (AD) and robotic applications, but obtaining ground-truth scene flow for non-rigid objects such as pedestrians is highly difficult. We present MeshBind , an automatic annotation method that uses mesh models to generate per-point scene flow for non-rigid objects. The novelty of this work is threefold: 1) developing a multi-view module that reconstructs an articulated mesh model from six surround cameras and registers it to the LiDAR frame; 2) developing a local-frame binding technique that attaches each point to the mesh via a per-triangle tangent-normal frame, invariant under rigid motion yet adaptive to articulated deformation; 3) developing a three-layer flow registration pipeline 

that combines ego-vehicle motion compensation, box binding, and mesh binding to produce full-scene per-point flow. We implement MeshBind in SF-Studio , an interactive annotation tool that visualises flow and allows human mesh corrections with automatic recomputation. We also release SFB (SUScape-Flow Benchmark), a large-scale benchmark built on the public SUScape dataset 1, covering 41k frames with per-point non-rigid pedestrian annotations and a Rigid/Semi-Rigid/Non-Rigid evaluation protocol. Experiments show that fine-tuning on SFB reduces three-way endpoint error (EPE) by 2–3 ×, and brings non-rigid EPE to the same level as rigid EPE. 

Index Terms —Scene Flow, Non-Rigid Motion, Pedestrian An-notation, LiDAR, Autonomous Driving. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Scene flow—the per-point 3D motion between consecutive LiDAR frames—is critical for motion segmentation [1], [2], odometry and SLAM [3], learning-based flow estimation [4]– [6], and navigation [7] in AD and robotic applications. How-ever, most real-world benchmarks assume a single 6-DoF rigid transform per dynamic object. This works for vehicles, but fails for walking pedestrians: leg swing, arm oscillation, and torso bending generate per-point motion that no rigid model can capture, especially at limbs (Fig. 1). Waymo [8] acknowl-edges this limitation, yet justifies it by its 10 Hz sampling rate. For other datasets, at the lower 2 Hz LiDAR sampling rate, the 0.5 s articulation makes the rigid approximation qualitatively wrong. Consequently, instance-level metrics hide systematic errors at joints, and supervision inherited from rigid assumptions structurally underrepresents true articulation. Generating per-point non-rigid flow for real LiDAR remains difficult, as manual labelling on sparse noisy point clouds is infeasible, and synthetic benchmarks [9] do not replicate real sensor noise. Recent foundation models for 3D human mesh construction [10], built on parametric body models [11], can predict detailed articulated meshes from images. Given

II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Scene Flow Datasets and Benchmarks 

Existing scene-flow datasets are categorized by their ground-truth generation methods: (i) Box-based rigid methods (e.g., Waymo [8], Argoverse 2 [13], nuScenes [14]) apply each 3D bounding box’s rigid transform to all interior points, an ap-proach that Waymo itself admits fails for non-rigid objects like pedestrians [15]. (ii) CAD fitting methods (e.g., KITTI [16]) use rigid 3D CAD models aligned with stereo and LiDAR data. (iii) Synthetic methods (e.g., Spring [17], SynFlow-4k [18]) render dense flow purely from simulation. (iv) Body-model fitting methods (e.g., HumanReg [9]) fit SMPL templates to dense indoor scans, but this does not translate well to sparse automotive LiDAR. Despite their varied approaches, none of these families provide scalable, real-world, per-point non-rigid pedestrian flow. Box-based methods, in particular, suppress limb-level articulation by enforcing rigid motion per box, causing existing estimators to miss substantial pedestrian motion on Argoverse 2 [19]. The evaluation metrics are equally coarse: Argoverse-style benchmarks typically report a three-way EPE (FD/BS/FS) that blends semi-rigid human– vehicle coupling with non-rigid limb motion into a single foreground value, obscuring the distinction. The proposed SFB 

fills this gap as a new category of real-world, mesh-based non-rigid annotation. To our knowledge, it is the first whole-scene LiDAR scene-flow benchmark to offer per-point non-rigid pedestrian annotations. It also introduces a Rigid / Semi-Rigid / Non-Rigid evaluation protocol that explicitly separates motion regimes previous benchmarks cannot isolate. 

B. Mesh Model Reconstruction and Point-to-Mesh Binding 

Human mesh reconstruction falls into two main categories. (i) Parametric body models like SMPL [11] and SMPL-X [20] represent the body as a differentiable mesh driven by pose and shape parameters. (ii) Image-to-mesh regressors use deep networks to predict these parameters from single images [21]– [23]; extensions such as HuMoR [24] and WHAM [25] add temporal and world-grounding, while foundation models like SAM 3D Body [10] deliver high-fidelity articulated predictions under occlusion and varying lighting. The proposed MeshBind 

builds on both approaches. Its multi-view module runs afoundation-model regressor on the six surround images to reconstruct a watertight articulated mesh for each pedestrian. However, because this mesh follows the camera convention, a straightforward extrinsic projection does not align with the LiDAR points. Standard point-to-mesh binding can associate points with triangulated surfaces using barycentric coordinates and spatial search [26], but it fails to preserve a fixed offset when the surface deforms. To address this, our local-frame binding technique stores a per-triangle local-frame residual, invariant to rigid motion yet adaptive to articulation, so that a single tuple can track each point through limb-level motion. 

C. Annotation Tools and Pipelines 

Annotation systems are defined by two criteria: label generation method and annotation scope. (i) Interactive labeling tools (LATTE [27], SAnE [28], 3D-BAT [29], SUSTech POINTS [12]) accelerate annotation via image fu-sion, one-click clustering, tracking, and smart evaluation, but output cuboids or segmentation masks, not per-point motion. (ii) Automatic pseudo-labeling (3DSFLabelling [30]) generates flow pseudo-labels by optimizing per-box motion parameters. The former is human-driven but not scalable for per-point motion annotation on sparse, noisy LiDAR; the latter is auto-matic yet rigid, unable to model limb-level articulation. Our developed SF-Studio combines both: MeshBind automatically produces per-point non-rigid flow for every pedestrian, while humans only refine the reconstructed meshes. Correcting a mesh automatically triggers re-binding and flow recomputation on the backend, enabling scalable semi-automatic annotation that remains reviewable and editable. III. (ii) Local-frame binding technique (Sec. IV-D) then loads the released flow for review, re-binds edited meshes, and writes the recomputed flow back, closing the annotation loop. 

Problem statement. We address the following problems, each of which motivates one stage of the proposed method: 1) How to reconstruct and align a per-pedestrian artic-ulated mesh model Mnt from multi-view images to the LiDAR frame, such that its deformation across consecutive frames serves as a geometric proxy for non-rigid motion? 2) How to bind each point pi,t ∈ P nt to the reconstructed mesh Mnt , yielding a tuple (j∗, λ 1.. 3, ri,t ) that remains invariant under rigid motion yet adapts to articulation, thus enabling the point to be tracked through limb-level motion across frames? 3) How to integrate ego-vehicle motion compensation, box binding, and mesh binding into a complete per-point flow pipeline for the whole scene, while ensuring that the process remains scalable and reviewable in practice? A. Dataset Construction 

We construct SFB on the public SUScape multi-sensor urban driving platform, which provides synchronized LiDAR point clouds, multi-view camera images, and calibration pa-rameters. The benchmark comprises 1,059 driving sequences totalling 41,196 frames at 2 Hz (train 741 / val 159 / test 159); each frame carries a 64-beam LiDAR point cloud of roughly 3.4 × 10 5 returns and six synchronised surround-view images. Consecutive-frame pairs at 2 Hz (∆t=0 .5 s) serve as scene-flow supervision, yielding 28,080 training and 6,026 test pairs (the 6,026 test pairs span 6,185 test frames used by the evaluation protocol below); val sequences are reserved for hyperparameter tuning. Following Sec. IV-D, the human categories sharing the SAM 3D Body mesh prior— Pedestrian, Child, BabyCart, BicycleRider, ScooterRider, Mo-torcycleRider, BicycleGroup, and RoadWorker—are annotated with per-point non-rigid flow and remain editable through the SF-Studio recompute loop; all other dynamic objects, vehicles, and background returns fall back to box-driven flow. 

B. Evaluation Protocol and Motion-Regime Metrics 

We report the standard Argoverse-style three-way EPE (foreground-dynamic EPE FD, background-static EPE BS, foreground-static EPE FS) together with motion-regime EPEs enabled by our per-point annotations. Using the fine-grained category indices in each H5 frame, every valid point is assigned to one of three regimes aligned with the SUScape taxonomy: Rigid (vehicles: car, van, truck, bus, long vehi-cle, concrete truck, crane, tour car), Semi-Rigid (wheeled human–vehicle coupling: bicycle, bicycle-rider, bicycle-group, scooter, scooter-rider, trimotorcycle, baby-cart), and Non-Rigid (pedestrian, child); the regime EPE is the mean end-point error over that regime’s points, exposing where rigid supervision fails. D. Benchmark Baselines: Zero-Shot Scene-Flow Estimation 

To establish reference performance, we evaluate eleven scene-flow estimators on the test split without any SUS-cape fine-tuning (eight Argoverse 2-pretrained networks and three learning-free baselines; Table I). Two observations stand out: the foreground-dynamic EPE FD (2.57–3.50 m feed-forward, 1.96–2.88 m learning-free) exceeds the background-static EPE BS by one to two orders of magnitude, confirming that dynamic objects remain the dominant error source on real driving LiDAR; and 2-frame models (DeFlow, VoteFlow) match or beat 3- and 5-frame variants (HiMo, Flow4D), the short ∆t=0 .5 s interval rendering long temporal context less informative than on Argoverse. The motion-regime EPEs expose an artefact of the rigid assumption: Non-Rigid is consistently the smallest regime (avg 0.400 m vs. 2.586 mRigid and 0.971 m Semi-Rigid, similarly in the learning-free group), not from capturing pedestrian articulation but because rigid-box supervision collapses every pedestrian point to a sin-gle 6-DoF box motion of small walking-speed displacement; combined with the scarcity of Non-Rigid points, this lets the rigid convention hide behind a small absolute error exactly where it is qualitatively wrong—ICP-Flow, enforcing a per-object rigid assumption, attains the lowest Non-Rigid EPE (0.316 m) precisely where its assumption is most violated.

We have presented MeshBind , a mesh-guided annotation method that converts articulated mesh deformation into per-point non-rigid scene-flow ground truth. Its core technique employs a per-triangle tangent-normal local frame, which remains invariant under rigid motion yet adapts to articulation. This method is implemented in our developed SF-Studio ,an interactive tool that incorporates human mesh corrections through a recomputation loop. The SF-Studio offers SFB 

(SUScape-Flow Benchmark), a whole-scene LiDAR scene-flow benchmark that provides per-point non-rigid pedestrian annotations and Rigid / Semi-Rigid / Non-Rigid flow evalu-ation protocols. Training nine estimators on SFB, either fine-tuned from Argoverse weights or trained from scratch, reduces the three-way EPE by a factor of 2–3 × and brings Non-Rigid EPE to the same level as Rigid EPE. These results show that the labels transfer effectively to conventional supervision and are sufficient as a primary training source.
