In outdoor environments, varying terrain and contact conditions can cause identical action commands to produce different motion
outcomes for quadruped robots. Predicting these outcomes is essential for reliable navigation and informed path selection. Existing
video world models can generate visually plausible futures, yet often struggle to capture robot dynamics and terrain interactions,
leading to overly optimistic predictions of action outcomes. We present DR-WM, a physically consistent world model that incor-
porates robot–terrain contact dynamics to predict the consequences of quadruped actions. Starting from an initial RGB image, our
method reconstructs scene geometry and integrates dense, dimensionless priors over terrain contact properties, calibrated using
real-world action–response data. The resulting terrain model supports the simulation of physics with spatially varying contact
properties such as friction. Given the initial state of the robot and candidate action sequences, we simulate its future motion and
use the resulting camera trajectories and rendered geometry to guide the training of a video world model. The generated future
observations are then used to update the terrain reconstruction, enabling iterative model rollouts without additional real-world
images. Extensive experiments demonstrate that DR-WM substantially outperforms geometry-only baselines and baselines with
uniform physical parameters on key physical metrics characterizing action outcomes, while achieving the best performance among
the evaluated baselines. These physically grounded predictions support more informed action selection: across 90 challenging
scenarios, an 8B vision–language model augmented with DR-WM outperforms an unassisted 32B vision–language model on five
of six evaluation metrics.

Introduction
Outdoor quadruped navigation depends not only on scene semantics and geometric traversability but also on how terrain
contact affects robot motion. As embodied navigation moves from structured laboratory settings to complex outdoor envi-
ronments, robots encounter spatially varying friction, support, and contact conditions. Under the same low-level controller,
an identical nominal motion command may produce steady progress, slipping, attitude disturbances, or even instability (Chen
et al., 2024). Reliable outdoor navigation therefore requires both understanding what is in the scene and predicting what will
happen when an action is executed on the current terrain.
A natural approach is to predict candidate action outcomes before execution, rule out high-risk behaviors, and decide whether
to proceed or adjust motion. However, real-world robot trials are costly and time-consuming. World models offer a promising
alternative, enabling policies or planners to compare candidate actions through visual predictions of their outcomes (Ha and
Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2019a;b).
However, a visually plausible future is not necessarily physically realizable. Existing video world models typically generate
future observations in two ways. One class conditions on predefined camera trajectories to synthesize viewpoint changes and
scene motion. This assumes the ground-truth camera trajectory and cannot determine whether it is physically feasible on the
current terrain. Another class predicts video directly from actions, but most current applications focus on game generation
and remain far removed from real-world settings. Scene geometry alone is also insufficient: geometrically similar surfaces
can produce markedly different robot responses due to different contact properties. Consequently, existing models may depict
smooth forward motion even when the same action would cause severe slipping or instability in reality.

FROM PIXELS TO EXECUTABLE PHYSICS
Figure 1: Overview of DR-WM. The model constructs an executable terrain representation from visual observations, then
simulates terrain dynamics to predict future observations and terrain-dependent action outcomes.
In this research we focus on physical consistency between robot motion and visual predictions: candidate actions and spatially
varying terrain contact jointly determine the robot state trajectory, from which camera motion is derived. This poses two key
challenges. First, a single RGB image provides only uncertain priors over terrain properties (Chen et al., 2024). These priors
require calibration using real robot action–response data to obtain contact parameters that reproduce robot motion. Second,
as the robot moves beyond the initial field of view, the model must complete unobserved regions and estimate their contact
properties. Although video models can complete visual content using scene priors and coarse 3D information (Team Wan et al.,
2025; Zhao et al., 2025), long-horizon motion predictions may still deviate from real outcomes without behavior-grounded
physical representations of these regions.
To address these challenges, we propose DR-WM, a physically consistent world model for robot–terrain interaction (Figure 1).
Given an initial RGB image, the robot’s initial state, and candidate action sequences, DR-WM jointly predicts future robot
motion and the corresponding visual observations. Our core idea is to model physical evolution and visual completion sepa-
rately while coupling them through a shared robot state trajectory: contact dynamics determine how the robot moves, and the
video model predicts what it will see as a result. Unlike methods that guide video generation with coarse simulation (Li et al.,
2025), DR-WM predicts motion using a terrain contact model calibrated against real robot behavior.
Specifically, DR-WM integrates terrain modeling, physics-guided video generation, and iterative scene updates. First, we
reconstruct scene geometry from visual observations and train PhyMap to predict dense, normalized priors for contact prop-
erties. We then map these priors to effective contact parameters using real-world robot action–response data. This calibration
mapping is learned exclusively from the training data and remains fixed at test time. Second, for each candidate action, we
simulate the resulting robot motion using the calibrated contact parameters and derive the corresponding camera trajectory
from the predicted base poses (Genesis Authors, 2024). Conditioned on this trajectory and coarse geometric renderings, the
video model generates future frames. Finally, we use the generated frames to estimate the geometry and contact properties
of previously unobserved regions. By propagating the robot state across chunks and incrementally extending the terrain rep-
resentation, DR-WM supports long-horizon rollouts without requiring additional real-world images. A decision model then
evaluates the candidate actions using these predicted observations and selects the action expected to produce more stable,
lower-risk motion (Figure 2). Experiments show that the behavior-calibrated, spatially varying contact model substantially
outperforms geometry-only and uniform-physics baselines on key motion-prediction metrics. DR-WM further outperforms

Related Work
2.1
Visual and Action-Conditioned World Models
Visual world models have evolved from latent prediction to controllable video generation. Early approaches predict future
observations for action evaluation (Finn and Levine, 2017; Hafner et al., 2019a). Interactive models extend this idea to
control-conditioned video (Yang et al., 2024; Bruce et al., 2024). Geometry-aware methods such as Spatia and WorldStereo
further improve spatial consistency and camera control through 3Dguidance (Zhao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). Yet these
systems emphasize camera-driven exploration, with limited modeling of physically grounded navigation actions. Our DR-WM
instead uses simulated robot actions to determine future motion and camera trajectories before visual prediction.
2.2
3D Reconstruction toward Physical Scene Understanding
Recent 3D reconstruction methods such as VGGT and MapAnything enable efficient scene reconstruction from images (Wang
et al., 2025; Keetha et al., 2026). Building on these geometric representations, IGGT and VGGT-Segmentor further incorporate
instance-level and semantic understanding (Li et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026). However, physical scene properties are still less
explored. Recent VLM-based methods such as SiPhy and PhysGS begin to address this direction by estimating physical
attributes from visual cues (Le et al., 2026; Chopra et al., 2026). These approaches often rely on multi-stage inference, making
efficient scene-wide physical prediction challenging. DR-WM targets this gap by extending feed-forward 3D reconstruction
with dense physical priors.
3


2.3
Robot Navigation Data
Navigation datasets cover diverse domains. NuScenes targets urban driving (Caesar et al., 2020), while RELLIS-3D and
GOOSE focus on unstructured outdoor environments (Jiang et al., 2022; Mortimer et al., 2024). ScanNet provides indoor
geometry (Dai et al., 2017), and SCAND records navigation behavior (Karnan et al., 2022). However, most datasets remain
domain-specific and capture limited physical variation across terrain surfaces. Terrain perception estimates traversability and
physical properties (Frey et al., 2023; Miki et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024), whereas real-to-sim methods infer simulator
parameters from observed responses (Chebotar et al., 2019; Ramos et al., 2019). Our work connects visual understanding with
executable physics for future prediction.

3.1
Physical–Visual Prediction Framework
DR-WM predicts robot motion and future observations under terrain-dependent contact dynamics. PhyMap reconstructs
geometry and dense physical priors from causal views. Calibrated using real robot action-response data, these priors define
executable terrain parameters. Genesis (Genesis Authors, 2024) then simulates robot motion from this terrain, the current
state, and commands. The resulting camera trajectories and rendered controls guide video generation. Future viewpoints thus
arise from robot–terrain interaction rather than prescribed trajectories.

To support PhyMap training, we construct Physcene–Image, a large-scale image dataset with dense physical-prior supervi-
sion, combining our real-world quadruped data, public indoor and off-road datasets, and web videos to cover diverse scenes.
CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) guides scene-based video filtering, followed by frame extraction and deduplication. We then use
ActiveSAM (Tien and Shen, 2026) to segment all images into a unified set of 32 semantic classes covering terrain, obstacles,
and traffic participants. The complete dataset contains 2.58M unique RGB-mask pairs: 1.5M on-robot, 690K indoor, 44.6K
off-road, and 350K web samples. For physical supervision, we construct a class-to-property lookup table from LLM priors.
The table converts segmentation masks into dense targets for three normalized physical properties. Appendix C provides the
detailed source breakdown.
The frozen backbone provides multi-scale DPT (Ranftl et al., 2021) and DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) features, along with
depth, normals and confidence. A trainable physical decoder predicts zv(u) at pixel u in view v. Supervision combines a
masked smooth-L1 loss on the physical targets with auxiliary terrain classification. Training follows a view-count curriculum
that progressively increases the number of causal input views. The predicted priors are lifted into 3D to form a physical field
aligned with the reconstructed point cloud.

3.4
Video Generation Model Training
Built on a Wan backbone (Team Wan et al., 2025), the generator integrates spatial and action control through two complemen-
tary branches. The spatial branch adopts coarse 3D conditioning (Li et al., 2025): RGB and confidence controls are rendered
from the causal point map, encoded by the frozen Wan VAE, and injected through VACE (Jiang et al., 2025). Spatial refer-
ences are selected from r < τk using visible-point overlap, as in Spatia (Zhao et al., 2025). In parallel, a dedicated action
ControlNet conditions generation on camera poses derived from robot motion. The dual-ControlNet design is introduced to
maintain reliable action following under complex off-road terrain while preserving spatial consistency. Detailed ablations are
provided in Appendix E.
Training proceeds in two stages. The first stage optimizes the spatial VACE branch, input projection, and LoRA adapters in
the attention and feed-forward projections (Hu et al., 2021). The second stage introduces the action ControlNet and jointly
optimizes it with the LoRA adapters, while preserving spatial control. Conditioned history and reference latents receive
timestep zero, while the loss is applied only to future latent tokens. Appendix B provides further training and conditioning
details.

4
Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Experiments evaluate executable physical priors, action conditioned motion, and future observation prediction. We further
examine their utility for VLM action selection. The appendices report additional ablations, physical prior remapping, long
horizon causal rollouts, and Genesis in the loop motion prediction. Appendix C describes the VLM segmentation and lookup
baseline.
Evaluation uses held-out scenes from Physcene–Robot, the robot-interaction subset of Physcene. Physcene–Robot contains
over 100K synchronized M20/Lite3 clips with RGB observations, robot states, and control commands. Scene-level partitions
are disjoint, and the behavior-calibrated PhyMap mapping remains fixed at test time. Tables 1, 2, 3, and 4 all use held-out
Physcene–Robot scenes. The executable-prior ablation in Table 4 varies only terrain contact parameters, keeping all other
settings fixed. Non-oracle rollouts exclude future real RGB frames from their inputs. Resampling applies only to evaluation
outputs. Reported confidence intervals are computed by bootstrapping scenes. Appendix A provides further evaluation details.
4.2
Comparison with Action-Conditioned World Models

image-to-video, camera-controlled, and action-conditioned methods. We compare CogVideoX1.5 (Yang et al., 2025), Wan2.2-

vertical motion, roll/pitch, and velocity. Vertical RMSE compares predicted and ground-truth vertical displacements in their

Conclusion
We presented DR-WM, a behavior-grounded world model for robot–terrain interaction that grounds video prediction in ex-
ecutable physics calibrated from real action–response data. By using physical simulation to guide video generation and
generated observations to update terrain reconstruction, DR-WM supports continued physical–visual rollout for comparing
candidate actions. Its spatially varying terrain physics improves held-out motion prediction over geometry-only and uniform-
physics alternatives, while DR-WM leads the reported automatic, reference-aligned, and human evaluations. Notably, its
predicted future observations enable an 8B VLM to achieve better mean results than an unassisted 32B model on five of six
action-selection metrics.
8


Despite these results, reliable prediction depends on the accuracy of reconstruction, contact modeling, and generated obser-
vations. Because generated observations update the terrain reconstruction, prediction errors may propagate across successive
rollouts. Improving geometric consistency and contact modeling over longer horizons is therefore an important direction for
future work. More broadly, our results highlight the value of grounding visual world models in measured robot responses.
Connecting commanded actions to terrain-dependent motion and its visual consequences offers a path toward more informed
action selection before real-world execution.

mask suppresses dynamic objects before mapping. Genesis resumes exactly from the simulator checkpoint at each chunk
boundary, and every new video chunk is appended without regenerating its prefix.

H
Strict Genesis-in-the-Loop Long-Horizon Rollout
We evaluate whether updating executable geometry and physical fields from generated observations improves motion predic-
tion over successive Genesis rollouts. Inference starts from the initial real RGB frame and causal robot state and follows a
recorded control sequence.
Protocol. For every non-oracle rollout, inference receives exactly the first real RGB frame I0, the latest causal robot state
s0 at the initial time, the raw recorded control sequence, and fixed camera, robot, controller, and calibration assets. Future real
RGB, LiDAR, robot state, and camera pose are isolated behind the evaluation interface and cannot affect mapping, reference
selection, simulation, video generation, or termination. When a two-view initialization is required, the first frame is duplicated
rather than reading a future observation.
Each rollout spans four consecutive 2.4 s chunks (9.6 s total). At chunk k, PhyMap reconstructs geometry and physical fields
from I0 and the method’s own generated history; the updated executable terrain is instantiated in Genesis; Genesis continues
the recorded controls from the previous predicted state; the fixed camera extrinsics convert simulated robot states into a camera
trajectory; and our trained video prediction model generates the next video chunk. The generated frames become the only new
visual observations available to chunk k + 1. Thus the endpoint follows three completed generated-observation map updates
followed by further Genesis simulation. Robot pose and velocity, joint state, controller memory and targets, camera follow
state, command-filter state, simulator time, and random seeds are serialized across chunk boundaries. Simulation resumes
from the predicted state without resets to future ground truth. Evaluation uses initial-frame alignment, with no trajectory-wide
alignment or fitted scale correction.
Data split. The evaluation uses 50 independent Physcene–Robot scenes with synchronized command and state streams
and three fixed generation seeds per scene. Scene identities, temporal windows, seeds, checkpoints, calibration, and failure
handling were frozen before inspecting model outcomes. Comparison of the frozen split manifests verified that the scenes
are disjoint from all training, calibration, controller-tuning, and hyperparameter-selection data. Table 13 reports the held-out
results.
Baselines. All variants share the initial observation, robot state, controls, controller, video model, sampling configuration,
valid-terrain mask, unknown region completion rule, and paired random seeds. Frozen Initial Map keeps the executable map
inferred from I0 fixed. Online Geometry + Frozen Physics updates geometry from generated history while retaining the initial
physical field. Online Geometry Only updates geometry but uses fixed uniform physical parameters. Online Uniform PhyMap
updates both geometry and physical predictions but broadcasts each channel’s valid-terrain mean. Full DR-WM retains the
complete updated spatial physical field. The primary controlled comparisons are Full DR-WM against Frozen Initial Map,
Online Geometry + Frozen Physics, and Online Uniform PhyMap.
Metrics. Base ATE is computed directly from the Genesis base trajectory, and camera rotation error is computed from
the camera pose induced by the simulated robot state and fixed rig extrinsics. We additionally report velocity RMSE. All
