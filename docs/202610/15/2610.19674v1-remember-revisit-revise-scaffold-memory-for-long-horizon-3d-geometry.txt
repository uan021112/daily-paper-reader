## ABSTRACT 

In 3D computer vision, geometry foundation models estimate camera poses and 3D scene structure from video sequences, but maintaining consistent reconstructions over long horizons remains challenging. Feed-forward models process long sequences as independent, fixed-length frame windows and subsequently align their predictions, causing pose and reconstruction errors to accumulate across windows. Streaming models retain information from previous frames to inform future predictions, but cannot revise earlier estimates when subsequent observations reveal errors. To address these limitations, we introduce Scaffold MemOry for Revision (SM ORE), a framework that equips existing geometry foundation models with a revisable external memory for long-horizon inference. Inspired by hippocampal associative memory in biological brains, which links spatial context with sensory experience, SM ORE associates where 

an observer is with what it observes, enabling past observations to be stored, retrieved, verified against new evidence, and revised when inconsistencies are detected. Crucially, SM ORE integrates with existing geometry foundation models while keeping them frozen, requiring no additional training or fine-tuning. We evaluate SM ORE with eight competitive geometry models on five datasets spanning camera pose estimation, multi-view depth estimation, 3D point-cloud reconstruction, simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), and novel view synthesis. Across all the evaluated geometry models, SM ORE consistently improves long-sequence camera pose estimation, reduces multi-view depth error by up to 58%, reduces Chamfer distance for 3D point-cloud reconstruction by up to 43%, and achieves average trajectory error as low as 2.9 cm in long-horizon SLAM. By retaining corrected camera poses and observations, SM ORE further improves the fidelity and spatial consistency of large-scale 3D scene reconstruction. These results establish revisable external memory as a flexible interface for extending pretrained geometry models beyond fixed-context prediction toward iterative, error-correcting inference over long visual sequences. Code, data, and models will be publicly available upon publication. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION AND RELATED WORK 

Recovering camera motion and 3D scene structure from visual observations is fundamental to 3D computer vision, underpinning robotic navigation, autonomous systems, augmented reality, and embodied AI Shen et al. (2026); Zhang et al. (2026c); Piriyajitakonkij et al. (2024); Zhang et al. (2018b;a); Khandelwal et al. (2023). Recent geometry foundation models have made substantial progress toward this goal. DUSt3R (Wang et al., 2024b) introduced point-map regression for unified geometric inference without known camera calibration or poses, followed by MASt3R (Leroy et al., 2024), Fast3R (Yang et al., 2025), VGGT (Wang et al., 2025a), VGGT-Ω (Wang et al., 2026a), and 

π3 (Wang et al., 2026b). These models estimate camera poses, depth, correspondences, and 3D scene structure directly from images. Yet, a fundamental challenge remains: how can they maintain accurate, spatially consistent reconstructions over long horizons? Existing geometry models largely approach long sequences in two ways, both with important limitations (Fig. 1). Long sequences are divided into fixed-length windows, reconstructed separately, and subsequently aligned into a common coordinate system. Small reconstruction or alignment errors can accumulate across windows, degrading camera poses and 3D geometry over long trajectories. TALO (Zhang et al., 2026a) and AMB3R (Wang & Agapito, 2026) address this through submap alignment and compact volumetric representations, respectively, while VGGSfM (Wang et al., 2024a) and GlueMap (Pan et al., 2026) combine learned geometry with structure-from-motion. Nevertheless, long-horizon reconstruction still requires maintaining geometric consistency across observations or submaps. Second, streaming geometry models such as CUT3R (Wang et al., 2025b), StreamVGGT (Zhuo et al., 2026), and STream3R (Lan et al., 2026) maintain information across successive observations through persistent states or causal caches. Such memory allows previous observations to inform future predictions, but retaining past context and explicitly revising previously estimated camera poses and scene geometry are different capabilities. Relatedly, EAGLE (Cao et al., 2026) combines visual and geometric memory for visual-query localization in egocentric video, but does not target retrospective correction of long-horizon 3D reconstruction. Later observations may reveal that an earlier estimate was incorrect, yet existing streaming memory does not explicitly retrieve, verify, and revise that estimate. Thus, remembering the past is not equivalent to revising it. The need for retrospective correction is well established in simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), where errors are corrected through loop closure and global optimization, as in graph-based SLAM (Grisetti et al., 2010; Strasdat et al., 2010), ElasticFusion (Whelan et al., 2015), and ORB-SLAM3 (Hu et al., 2022). Learned systems such as DROID-SLAM (Teed & Deng, 2021), NICER-SLAM (Zhu et al., 2024), and SLAMFormer (Yuan et al., 2026) exploit geometric constraints across observations to correct accumulated errors. More recent works, including MASt3R-SLAM (Murai et al., 2025), VGGT-SLAM (Maggio et al., 2025), VGGT-SLAM++ (Mandal et al., 2026), and ViSTA-SLAM (Zhang et al., 2026b), combine learned geometry with loop closure, while VGGT-SLAM 2.0 (Maggio & Carlone, 2026) uses model attention to verify retrieved images. However, these are dedicated SLAM systems built around specific representations and optimization pipelines. This motivates a general question: can existing 2geometry foundation models be equipped with a shared external memory to recall, verify, and revise past predictions while remaining frozen? Beyond computer vision, recurrent neural fields and continuous attractor networks (CANs) in neuroscience provide biologically inspired mechanisms for maintaining and representing continuous variables through persistent neural activity (Amari, 1977; Burak & Fiete, 2009). Vector-HaSH (Chandra et al., 2025) further combines structured spatial representations with associative memory, linking spatial states with sensory observations. This principle suggests a natural way to organize long-horizon visual memory: associate where an observer is with what 

it observes, so that past observations can be retrieved when related spatial states are revisited. Building on this principle, we introduce Scaffold MemOry for Revision (SM ORE), a revisable external memory for extending existing geometry foundation models to long-horizon inference. SM ORE goes beyond associative retrieval by supporting an explicit cycle of storage, retrieval, verification, and revision . As new observations arrive, SM ORE retrieves relevant past observations, compares them with current geometric evidence, verifies their consistency, and revises previously estimated poses and geometry when inconsistencies are detected. Corrected observations are retained in memory and can subsequently provide improved geometric context for later inference. Importantly, SM ORE is external to the geometry backbone. It therefore does not require a new geometry model to be trained for long-horizon processing. Instead, existing geometry foundation models can be integrated with SM ORE while their model weights remain frozen, requiring neither additional training nor fine-tuning. This design separates the problem of learning strong geometric priors from the problem of maintaining and correcting information over time: the foundation model provides geometric inference, while SM ORE provides persistent and revisable memory. Consequently, the same memory framework can be applied across different geometry backbones and tasks. We evaluate SM ORE with eight competitive geometry models on five datasets spanning camera pose estimation, multi-view depth estimation, 3D point-cloud reconstruction, simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), and novel view synthesis. Across the evaluated geometry models, incorporating SM ORE consistently improves long-sequence camera pose estimation. It reduces multi-view depth error by up to 58%, reduces Chamfer distance for 3D point-cloud reconstruction by up to 43%, and achieves average trajectory error as low as 2.9 cm in long-horizon SLAM. The corrected poses and observations retained by SM ORE further improve the fidelity and spatial consistency of large-scale 3D scene reconstruction. Our contributions are threefold: 

1. Revisable external memory for long-horizon 3D geometry. We introduce SM ORE, an external memory that associates spatial context with visual observations and supports explicit storage, retrieval, geometric verification, and revision of previously estimated poses and scene structure. 

2. Training-free integration with frozen geometry foundation models. SM ORE provides a model-agnostic interface for augmenting existing geometry foundation models while keeping their weights frozen, enabling long-horizon error correction without additional training or fine-tuning. 

3. Comprehensive evaluation across models and 3D tasks. We evaluate SM ORE with eight geometry models on five datasets across camera pose estimation, multi-view depth estimation, 3D point-cloud reconstruction, SLAM, and novel view synthesis, demonstrating consistent improvements in long-horizon geometric accuracy and spatial consistency. 

## 2 OUR PROPOSED SCAFFOLD MEM ORY FOR REVISION (SM ORE)

We introduce SM ORE, a revisable external memory for extending pretrained geometry foundation models to long-horizon inference (Fig. 1). SM ORE consists of a spatial scaffold and a content bank . The content bank stores past observations and their geometric predictions, while the scaffold associates where an observation was captured with what was observed through a shared memory representation. As new observations arrive, SM ORE stores their spatial and visual representations and uses visual content to retrieve candidate revisits. Because associative retrieval alone cannot determine whether a candidate is a genuine revisit, retrieved observations are verified against their historical poses before being used for correction. A verified revisit provides a global spatial anchor for revising accumulated placement errors. The geometry foundation model remains 3frozen throughout; only the external memory is updated online. Before describing these memory operations, we first define the overlapping frame windows used for local reconstruction and the local and global coordinate systems used to place and revise these reconstructions. Given RGB frames I1, . . . , I N , we process them in overlapping windows Wk. By default, each window contains 32 frames, with 16 shared between consecutive windows. See Table S4(Group G) for the effect of window size and overlap. Because a frame can appear in two overlapping windows, it is stored only once, using the first window in which it appears. We call this its source window ,denoted src( i) for frame Ii. For each window, the geometry foundation model predicts camera poses and 3D point maps in a local coordinate system defined by the camera pose of the first frame in that window. We define the global coordinate system as that of the first window and let Tk ∈ Sim(3) 

denote the transformation that places Wk in this global coordinate system. Consecutive windows are initially placed by aligning the predicted camera poses of their overlapping frames. Camera poses and point maps remain stored in their source window’s local coordinate system, while Tk

determines their global placement. The robust similarity alignment and the effect of changing Tk on stored geometry are detailed in Appendices B and C. 2.1 ASSOCIATING SPATIAL AND VISUAL MEMORY 

After processing each window, SM ORE stores its new frames in the content bank together with their predicted camera poses and point maps. Rather than storing global poses in a lookup table indexed by frame identity, SM ORE builds an associative memory : spatial states and visual observations are linked through a shared memory representation. This is important because, at retrieval time, the identity and location of a revisited frame are unknown. A new visual observation must therefore serve as a partial cue for recalling previously associated spatial information. 

Spatial scaffold. Following Vector-HaSH (Chandra et al., 2025), we represent each frame’s global camera pose using a structured spatial scaffold. Camera position is encoded by three periodic spatial modules operating at different spatial scales, while yaw, pitch, and roll are represented by three continuous attractor rings. The periodic representation preserves the continuous structure of position and orientation, while combining multiple spatial scales reduces the ambiguity introduced by any single periodic representation. Their concatenation forms the scaffold state gi.We map gi through a fixed random linear projection to a 1024-dimensional memory representation 

hi, retaining only the 64 largest activations followed by normalization. This sparse intermediate representation is a deliberate design choice: activating only a small subset of memory units reduces interference among stored associations, thereby increasing effective memory capacity and making recall more robust to partial or noisy cues. We systematically vary the sparsity level and scaffold dimension and study their effects in Table S4(Groups B and D). A reverse mapping, learned using ridge regression, reconstructs the spatial scaffold state from hi. Thus, unlike a lookup table that can only return an entry given its explicit index, the scaffold supports associative reconstruction of spatial information from a shared memory state (see more analysis in Appendix J.1 and Table S4(Group A)). See Appendix D for details of scaffold encoding and projection. 

Associating visual observations with the scaffold. To connect what is observed with where it was observed, we encode each frame Ii using a frozen DINOv2 ViT-S/14 encoder (Oquab et al., 2024), yielding a normalized 384-dimensional visual descriptor si. We learn bidirectional associations between si and the shared memory representation hi: visual content can cue its associated memory state, while the memory state can reconstruct its associated visual representation. Both mappings are updated incrementally using recursive least squares (RLS) whenever a new frame is stored. Together, these associations connect the spatial scaffold state gi, shared memory representation 

hi, and visual descriptor si. Consequently, a new visual observation can provide a partial cue for recalling memories associated with similar past observations, rather than requiring the identity or location of the corresponding frame to be known in advance. This associative recall is central to SM ORE: it enables what is currently observed to cue where related observations were previously encountered. Because such recall alone cannot establish that two observations correspond to the same physical location, we use it only to propose candidate revisits, which are subsequently verified geometrically (Sec. 2.2). All memory updates are external to the pretrained models; the geometry 4foundation model and DINOv2 encoder remain frozen. Instead of DINOv2, we evaluate alternative visual features in Appendix J.2 and Table S4(Group C). 2.2 RETRIEVING AND VERIFYING REVISITS FOR WINDOW REVISION 

Retrieving candidate revisits based on visual feature similarity. Given a current frame Iq ,we compute its visual descriptor sq and use the learned visual association to obtain a query memory representation ˜hq . Retrieval uses only visual information; the current camera pose and 3D geometry are not used. We do not directly trust the spatial state recalled from ˜hq , because a novel observation may still activate an existing memory. Instead, we compare ˜hq with all stored memory representations and select the five most similar frames. We then remove candidates that are within 64 frames of the current observation, originate from the immediately preceding window, or have DINOv2 cosine similarity below 0.5. The remaining frames are treated as candidate revisits and are geometrically verified next. Full retrieval details are provided in Appendix D. 

Verifying revisits by historical pose consistency. For each retrieved candidate, we select a second nearby frame ( ±3 frames) from the same source window to form a retrieved pair . We retain two pairs from different source windows, providing two independent historical references. If fewer than two valid pairs are available, no retrieval-based correction is applied. We use two pairs by default and study this choice in Table S4(Group E). When two pairs are available, the geometry foundation model jointly processes the 32 current-window frames and four retrieved frames, placing all 36 frames in a common local coordinate system. The current window retains this joint prediction unless it rotates the current frames by more than 5◦ relative to the prediction from the current window alone, in which case the current-window prediction is kept and the joint pass serves only to measure the revisit (Appendix M). Problem setting and datasets. We evaluate long-horizon camera pose estimation on CO3Dv2 (Reizenstein et al., 2021) and RealEstate10K (RE10K) (Zhou et al., 2018). CO3Dv2 contains object-centric videos spanning 8–68 s, with 200 annotated frames per sequence. RE10K contains predominantly forward-moving real-estate videos; we use 100 clips spanning 6.7–9.3 s. For both datasets, we uniformly sample N ∈ {10 , 50 , 100 , 200 } frames from each trajectory. 

Baselines. We evaluate six feed-forward geometry foundation models: DUSt3R (Wang et al., 2024b), Fast3R (Yang et al., 2025), MASt3R (Leroy et al., 2024), VGGT (Wang et al., 2025a), VGGT-Ω (Wang et al., 2026a), and π3 (Wang et al., 2026b), together with two streaming models: StreamVGGT (Zhuo et al., 2026) and STream3R (Lan et al., 2026). Streaming baselines use their native causal inference, while their SM ORE variants use the same 32-frame windowing policy. 

Metrics. We evaluate all pairs of sampled frames. For each pair, pose error is the larger of the rotation and translation-direction errors, so lower is better. AUC@30 instead measures the normalized area under the cumulative fraction of frame pairs whose pose error falls below thresholds up to 30 ◦; therefore, higher AUC@30 is better.

## 4 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

We introduced SM ORE, a revisable external memory that extends frozen geometry foundation models beyond fixed-context prediction through storage, retrieval, geometric verification, and revision. Across eight geometry models, five datasets, and five downstream tasks, our results show that SM ORE complements both feed-forward and streaming models, using verified revisits to correct accumulated errors over long visual sequences without fine-tuning the underlying geometry models. Several directions remain for future work. Retrieval verification introduces additional computation, motivating more efficient mechanisms for recalling reliable spatial information from the memory scaffold. Moreover, SM ORE currently focuses on revising global window placement; future work could extend revision to local geometric predictions when later observations provide stronger evidence.

## D SCAFFOLD CONSTRUCTION , S TORAGE , AND RETRIEVAL 

Sections 2.1 and 2.2 introduce the two memory operations used by SM ORE. When a frame is stored, its estimated global camera pose is converted into a compact memory address. Representing the global camera pose. When frame Ii is first stored, its source window has a current global placement Tsrc( i). ## E SCAFFOLD DYNAMICS AND CONTROLLED MEMORY DIAGNOSTICS 

The previous section described the scaffold as the mapping g ↔ h ↔ s used to store and retrieve frames. Here we briefly describe the continuous attractor structure underlying g and report controlled tests of its behavior. These tests characterize the memory representation itself; they are separate from the end-to-end trajectory experiments. 

Continuous attractor fields. Each spatial or orientation component of the scaffold is represented by a periodic neural field, following continuous-attractor models of spatial and directional coding (Burak & Fiete, 2009; Bicanski & Burgess, 2020). The field maintains a localized region of high activation, which we call an activity bump . The position of this bump around a ring or torus represents the encoded coordinate. For example, an orientation ring represents one angular variable such as yaw. Moving the bump around the ring changes the represented angle, while a full revolution returns to the same state. The spatial modules use the same principle on periodic two-dimensional grids and height rings. Thus, the scaffold stores pose through the location of the activity pattern rather than through its overall magnitude. 19 Figure S1: Controlled diagnostics of the scaffold representation. (a) A stationary orientation ring maintains a localized activity bump; the bump centre represents the encoded angle, while the orange dashed lines show the predicted bump boundaries. (b) When angular motion is supplied, the measured bump velocity closely follows the commanded velocity over the tested range. (c) The spatial phases during a closed two-dimensional path return close to their starting point, illustrating path integration in the periodic scaffold coordinates. (d) Associative recall error as the number of stored states increases. The scaffold configuration with the fitted content return map maintains low error through the largest tested load ( P = 160 ), whereas the Hopfield-style control accumulates larger error. These are controlled memory diagnostics and do not measure end-to-end reconstruction accuracy or establish an unrestricted memory-capacity limit. 

Maintaining and moving the represented pose. Local excitation together with broader inhibition allows an activity bump to remain stable once formed (Amari, 1977). A motion input can translate the bump across the periodic field, so changes in camera position or orientation correspond to changes in its phase. Observed camera poses provide anchors for the represented state; the scaffold therefore does not rely only on accumulating motion over time. The main SM ORE pipeline does not use these dynamics as a replacement for the geometry foundation model’s camera predictions. Instead, the predicted global pose provides the state that is encoded when a frame is stored, as described in Sec. 2.1. 

Returning from an address to a valid scaffold state. Appendix D introduced the return mapping 

Whg from the sparse address h to the scaffold state g. For a state g, one complete scaffold return is 

Φ( g) = ψ(Whg σ64 (Wgh g)) ,

where ψ normalizes the individual spatial and orientation fields. This return mapping is useful because an associative cue need not reproduce every component of the original scaffold state exactly. The return operation encourages the reconstructed representation to remain consistent with the learned scaffold structure. A perturbation that makes different modules mutually inconsistent can therefore be reduced by the return mapping. By contrast, a coherent displacement that moves all modules to another valid pose is itself a valid scaffold state; determining whether such a state corresponds to the correct physical location requires the geometric verification described in Sec. 2.2. 20 What these diagnostics establish. Fig. S1 checks three properties required by the memory design: a pose representation can remain stable, its phase can move continuously with motion, and a stored scaffold state can be recalled through the associative representation over the tested memory load. These experiments support the internal behavior of the scaffold, while the usefulness of scaffold retrieval for SM ORE is evaluated separately by the end-to-end comparison with flat descriptor retrieval in Table S4. 

## F GEOMETRIC VERIFICATION OF RETRIEVED REVISITS 

Section 2.2 describes how SM ORE verifies the frames returned by the scaffold before changing any window placement. Here we expand that procedure. The key idea is that a retrieved frame is not trusted from appearance alone: it must provide a geometric connection between the current window and a previously placed source window, and a second source window must support a compatible placement. 

Forming a retrieved pair. For each retrieved frame Ij , we select a nearby stored frame Ij−3 or 

Ij+3 . The partner must belong to the same source window as Ij and satisfy the sequential eligibility rule from Sec. 2.2. If neither partner frame is valid, the retrieved frame is discarded. The two frames form a retrieved pair . A pair is used instead of a single frame because two camera poses provide a geometric baseline for aligning the new prediction with the stored source window. We use an offset of three frames as a fixed design choice so that the second frame is nearby but distinct. 

Why use two different source windows? We retain two retrieved pairs, denoted a and b. The two frames within each pair come from the same source window, but the two pairs must come from different source windows. Because every stored frame records its source window, we enforce src( ja)̸ = src( jb).

This distinction is important. Each source window has its own global placement T . If both pairs came from the same source window, both placement proposals would depend on the same historical reference. Using two different source windows instead provides two separately placed references with which to check the current window. We use two pairs by default. One pair provides no second historical placement with which to test agreement, while a third pair requires two additional frames. Table S4 compares one, two, and three pairs and shows that two retain most of the measured benefit of three with fewer additional inputs. 

Joint prediction with the current window. If two valid pairs are available, the frozen geometry foundation model processes the 32 current-window frames together with the four retrieved frames, giving 36 input frames in total. The 32 current frames retain their sequential order, and the four retrieved frames are appended as the two pairs. Because all 36 frames are processed together, their camera poses are predicted in one common local coordinate system. This joint prediction creates a direct geometric relationship between the current window and the retrieved historical frames; the existing chain of window placements is not used to create that relationship.

## I NOVEL VIEW SYNTHESIS PROTOCOL AND DECODER 

We evaluate whether revising stored geometry improves novel view synthesis from long sequences. We use the same frozen geometry models, windowing scheme, and memory configuration as in the camera pose experiments. A Gaussian decoder converts the stored geometry into rendered images. The datasets and evaluation splits are described in Appendix H.5. I.1 SOURCE VIEWS AND GEOMETRY 

We render each held-out target from eight nearby input keyframes. Source views are selected using the sum of camera-centre distance and viewing-direction angle, computed from the ground-truth cameras. The target itself is excluded. Baseline and +SM ORE use identical source views. Each source keyframe retains the depth, confidence, and camera intrinsics predicted when it first enters a window. Its depth is unprojected into 3D points in that window’s local coordinate system (Appendix C). Baseline places these points in the global map using chained window transformations 

Tk. SM ORE uses the window transformations obtained after online revision. We fit a similarity transformation between the estimated and ground-truth input cameras. We use this alignment to express the target camera in each map’s coordinate system. Before decoding, we centre the source points at the centre of the source cameras and normalize their scale by the median source depth. We discard the lowest-confidence 30% of points in each frame. We also remove points at depth discontinuities, defined by a relative depth difference above 5% between neighbouring pixels. Both systems use the same filtering procedure. Images are rendered at 384 × 288 resolution with a 4:3 aspect ratio. Portrait CO3Dv2 sequences retain their original orientation. I.2 GAUSSIAN DECODER AND TRAINING

28 A four-level U-Net with approximately 7M parameters predicts a position offset, log-scales, rotation, opacity, and colour residual for each valid pixel. The position offset is bounded by two pixel footprints, and the scales are predicted relative to the footprint. A pixel’s footprint is its depth multiplied by the pixel size and divided by the focal length. This parameterization scales the Gaussians according to the input geometry. The Gaussians from all source views are rasterized into the target view using gsplat (Kerbl et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2025). An image-space U-Net with approximately 3M parameters then refines the rendering. It takes the rendered colour, alpha, and depth as input and predicts a bounded colour residual. The decoder is initialized to approximate splatting the coloured source points. 

Training data. For each geometry model, we train one decoder on the chained geometry and another on the geometry maintained by SM ORE. Both decoders use the same architecture, training data, schedule, and random seed. Training uses all 7-Scenes sequences except seq-01. These sequences are processed with the same windowing and memory configuration as the test sequences, and every keyframe is stored. Each training sample consists of one target keyframe and its eight nearest source keyframes. We exclude the target and its immediate neighbours from the source set, so the target image does not provide source pixels. 

Optimization. We train the decoders with an image reconstruction loss: 

L = L1 + 0 .5 LLPIPS + 0 .2 (1 − SSIM) .

We use AdamW for 12K steps with a learning rate of 2×10 −4, 500 warm-up steps, and cosine decay. Gradients are accumulated over two samples. Training takes approximately one hour per decoder on an RTX 6000 Ada GPU. Neither the geometry foundation model nor SM ORE is optimized by this loss. The comparison therefore evaluates each geometry representation with its own trained decoder under matched training conditions. I.3 EVALUATION AND ADDITIONAL RESULTS

J.3 HOW MUCH HISTORICAL EVIDENCE IS NEEDED ?Section 2.2 uses two retrieved pairs from different source windows to verify a revisit. In Table S4(Group E), we first vary the number of retrieved pairs while keeping the rest of the pipeline unchanged. We then disable the online placement update to distinguish verifying a revisit from actually using it to revise the trajectory. Using only one retrieved pair substantially degrades 7-Scenes ATE, whereas three pairs give similar online performance to two on both benchmarks. Three pairs keep the 7-Scenes ATE to 0.029. We therefore use two pairs as the default evidence. The online placement update is also important. When revisits are proposed and verified but not applied online, 7-Scenes ATE increases from 0.030 to 0.042 m and CO3Dv2 AUC@30 decreases from 93.4 to 91.3. The final global refinement can recover part of this loss, but those corrections are unavailable while later windows are being processed. The no-update row remains different from the raw trajectory because the default configuration retains the joint-pass local predictions; this effect is isolated in Appendix M. The additional rows test how accepted measurements are applied. The default online update re-solves the affected local pose graph using the accepted revisit measurement and the sequential constraints. The Distributed correction row instead applies the direct interpolation of the measured correction across the affected windows. Removing outlier rejection from the final global solve mainly reduces the final CO3Dv2 score, while separately re-measuring the historical frames changes the reported accuracy only modestly. J.4 SENSITIVITY TO SCAFFOLD AND WINDOW SIZE 

We next vary the dimensions of the scaffold representation and the window used by the geometry foundation model in Table S4(Group D). These experiments test sensitivity rather than removing a component: changing these quantities also changes the memory resolution or the input context available to the geometry foundation model. Performance is relatively stable across the tested scaffold dimensions. Increasing the address dimension from 1024 to 8192 does not consistently improve the final result, and increasing the torus resolution to 64 2 can reduce accuracy. Therefore, it shows that the memory structure itself is good at recall, and increasing the dimensionality of the address does not necessarily show us gains. On the other hand (Group B), optimal sparsity σ = 64 is set so that sparse addresses are near orthogonal to each other with minimum overlap, while too much sparsity results in loss of information. Window size has a stronger effect because it changes both the geometry predictions and the number of junctions between windows. We observe that the performance over both 7-scenes and CO3Dv2 improves with larger window size and moderate overlap ratio. As the overlap ratio increases, the number of windows increases, and so does the chaining error evident in Table S4. Also observe that a smaller window size increases the number of windows too, which is why we see degraded performance for W = 16 . Since the gains are so marginal, we select the best window size and overlap ratio with a simple grid search over these two parameters. 

Summary. Together, these ablations support the main design choices used by SM ORE:scaffold-based retrieval improves over direct descriptor matching, DINOv2 provides the strongest tested retrieval cue, two retrieved pairs provide an effective verification budget, and applying accepted revisit measurements online substantially improves the running trajectory. The scaffold 32 dimensions, window size, and activation sparsity of the scaffold address were all tuned to maximize performance across all tasks. 

## K COMPUTE AND MEMORY MEASUREMENTS 

We examine how SM ORE changes the computational and memory requirements of geometry foundation models as sequences become longer. For feed-forward models, long sequences are processed as overlapping windows whose local reconstructions must be connected across the trajectory, while streaming models retain causal context from earlier frames inside the model. In contrast, SM ORE keeps geometry-model inference bounded to the current window and stores long-horizon information in its external memory. Earlier observations are brought back into the geometry foundation model only when they are retrieved for revisit verification. All measurements use one RTX 6000 Ada GPU with a tested 46 GB GPU-memory budget, batch size one, and public model weights. We use the CO3Dv2 camera-pose experiment from Sec. 3.1. The default SM ORE configuration uses 32-frame windows; during revisit verification, up to four retrieved historical frames can be processed together with the current window, giving at most 36 frames in one geometry-model invocation. K.1 PEAK GPU M EMORY WITH INCREASING SEQUENCE LENGTH 

We first study peak GPU allocation as the number of input frames increases from N = 10 to N =200 . Table S5 shows how the bounded-window design of SM ORE limits the GPU working set over longer sequences. For feed-forward models, the sequence is reconstructed using overlapping windows and the resulting local predictions are chained through their shared frames. As the sequence grows, the baseline can retain increasing amounts of intermediate predictions and geometric information needed to connect these windows. With SM ORE, the geometry foundation model still operates on a bounded current window, while earlier observations are stored in the external scaffold and content bank and are selectively reintroduced only when needed. For example, VGGT grows from 9.1 GB at N = 10 to 23.9 GB at N = 200 in the baseline, whereas SM ORE remains between 8.5 and 9.6 GB. VGGT-Ω

similarly grows from 5.7 to 19.5 GB in the baseline but remains near 7 GB with SM ORE.Streaming models exhibit a different source of GPU-memory growth. Their native causal inference retains internal states or cached tokens from previous frames so that earlier observations can influence later predictions. These GPU-resident states grow with the sequence. StreamVGGT therefore increases from 8.6 GB at N = 10 to 22.7 GB at N = 100 and exceeds the tested 46 GB budget at N = 200 . STream3R exceeds the same budget from N = 50 onward. With SM ORE,long-range information is instead maintained externally and the geometry model receives bounded windows plus only a small number of retrieved frames. StreamVGGT therefore uses 11.4 GB at 

N = 200 , while STream3R remains feasible at 25.4 GB. The total memory used by SM ORE is not constant: the content bank grows as additional observations are stored. The important distinction is that this history does not remain part of the geometry foundation model’s active GPU context. The joint policy retains the current-window prediction produced by joint processing with the retrieved historical frames; it corresponds to the anchored configuration in the diagnostic logs. The ordinary policy retains the prediction of the current window alone. M.6 WHAT THESE DIAGNOSTICS ESTABLISH 

These experiments separate two decisions that are coupled in the default pipeline. The first is which local prediction to retain after the current and historical frames are processed together. The second is 

whether the verified historical measurement should revise the global trajectory . Revisit verification addresses the second decision but does not, by itself, protect the first. The joint policy used in the main experiments allows useful historical frames to improve the current prediction, as suggested by the CO3D comparison in Table S9. However, the failure-case experiments show that an incorrect historical proposal can occasionally distort the local prediction before verification. Retaining the ordinary prediction removes this direct dependence, while gating provides an intermediate option that accepts only sufficiently small changes. The gate used here measures rotational disagreement only. It does not guarantee small translation, scale, or dense-point error. We adopt the gated policy with τdist = 5 ◦ as the default: on the default proposals it retains every joint prediction (Table S9, where the largest measured distortion is 0.3◦

and the 5◦ gate matches the joint policy on both benchmarks), while the distortions in the failure cases of Table S10 are tens of degrees and are rejected. Finally, these results do not contradict the placement property in Appendix C. That property assumes that the local prediction has already been selected and then asks what happens when its global placement Tk changes. The present section instead studies the earlier question of whether joint processing changes the local prediction itself. 

## N MEMORY GROWTH AND PRUNING 

We additionally test retrieval as the memory bank grows using a separate retrieval-only diagnostic with up to 3,100 stored frames. A retrieved frame is considered a correct historical revisit when it comes from the same scene, lies within 10% of the scene’s spatial extent from the query camera, and 39 differs in orientation by at most 30 ◦. Recall@1 is the fraction of eligible queries for which such a revisit is ranked first, while Recall@5 measures whether one appears among the five highest-ranked retrievals. At 3,100 stored frames, scaffold retrieval achieves 0.36 Recall@1 compared with 0.25 for direct DINOv2 cosine retrieval, while Recall@5 is comparable at 0.42 versus 0.43. The stored bank also contains substantial redundancy. A simple pose-based insertion rule reduces the bank from 3,100 to 1,119 frames while preserving at least one valid historical revisit for 157 of the original 159 eligible queries. On these retained queries, scaffold Recall@5 increases from 0.42 to 0.54. This indicates that substantial redundancy can be removed without eliminating most useful historical observations, although we do not claim an optimal pruning strategy or unbounded memory capacity. 40
