Abstract—Robotic platforms combine multi-modal sensors to
operate across diverse conditions, but most dense correspondence
methods are limited to RGB-RGB or a small set of modality pairs.
Training a model for each modality pair scales poorly with the
number of sensors and forgoes potential positive transfer across
modality pairs. We introduce TartanMatch, a unified model for
dense correspondence across all 25 ordered pairs among RGB,
event, thermal, depth, and projected LiDAR. TartanMatch adapts
an RGB-pretrained correspondence model to all modality pairs
within one shared network using lightweight modality adaptation
and a mixture of real and synthetic data. On zero-shot real-world
benchmarks, TartanMatch reduces Endpoint Error (EPE) over the
strongest baselines by 61.9% for cross-modal matching and 49.6%
for same-modal matching. We further show that joint training
across all modality pairs improves average matching accuracy over
pair-specific training, especially on challenging cross-modal pairs.
These results demonstrate that a single model achieves broad
multi-modal coverage and state-of-the-art matching accuracy.

I. INTRODUCTION
Dense correspondence—estimating where each pixel in one
observation appears in another—is a core primitive for robotic
perception tasks such as visual odometry, place recognition, and
3D reconstruction. Although correspondence estimation has
advanced rapidly for RGB images, robots do not perceive
the world through RGB alone. Modern robotic platforms
combine multiple sensors with distinct yet complementary
sensing capabilities: event cameras capture rapid motion over
a wide dynamic range, thermal cameras remain informative in
darkness, smoke, and adverse weather, and depth or LiDAR
sensors provide geometry when appearance is ambiguous [1–4].
Robust robotic perception therefore calls for correspondence
between any sensors informative in the environment.
Existing correspondence methods cover only parts of this
multi-modal sensor space. Sensor-specific methods are designed
for a same-modal pair setting: RGB methods span optical
flow [5, 6] and wide-baseline matching [7, 8], with recent
models unifying both settings [9], yet operate only on RGB–
RGB pairs; likewise, event-based methods match only Event–
Event pairs [10, 11]. Cross-modal matchers extend matching to
pairs such as RGB–Depth or RGB–Thermal, but each typically
supports only one such modality pair [12–14]. Recent methods
broaden coverage to several cross-modal pairs by fine-tuning
RGB matchers on synthetic pairs that combine an RGB view

with a non-RGB view generated from RGB imagery [15, 16].
However, this training remains RGB-anchored—the model
never sees a pair in which both views are non-RGB—and
represents non-RGB inputs using fixed RGB-like renderings.
Such renderings can suppress sensor-specific structure useful
for accurate correspondence, such as event timing and depth
validity. Together, these limitations leave a central question: can
one unified model accurately estimate dense correspondence
for every ordered pairing among diverse sensor modalities?
This unification may also improve matching accuracy
through positive transfer across modality pairs. Although
different modality sensors measure distinct physical quantities,
every modality pair poses the same matching task: identifying
pixels in two views that correspond to the same physical point.
This common task motivates a second question: does joint
training across all modality pairs improve matching accuracy
over pair-specific training?
Motivated by these questions, we introduce TartanMatch, a
unified model for any-pair multi-modal dense correspondence
among RGB, event, thermal, depth, and projected LiDAR.
Given source and target observations drawn from these five
modalities, TartanMatch predicts dense flow and covisibility. A
single set of weights thus covers all 25 modality pairs, replacing
a collection of pair-specific models.
TartanMatch adapts UFM [9], an RGB-pretrained correspon-
dence model, to support dense matching across five modalities
and all 25 source–target pairs. Rather than converting non-RGB
modality into fixed RGB-like rendering, we represent each
modality in a form that retains its own measurement structure
and, where applicable, use lightweight learned projections to
map this representation to UFM’s three-channel input. These
inputs are then processed by the same UFM backbone, enabling
one shared model to handle every modality pair. To effectively
adapt the pretrained model to these new modalities, we train
TartanMatch in two stages: we first freeze the pretrained
encoder of UFM while optimizing the input projections and
downstream layers, then fine-tune the full network end to
end. We empirically observed that this schedule improves
performance over single-stage training.
Training a unified matcher is bottlenecked by data: no single
real-world dataset covers all five modalities with correspon-
dence or flow labels. We therefore assemble a training suite that
combines synthetic sources with real indoor, outdoor, aerial,
and driving scene captured from diverse datasets, rather than
solely relying on synthetic modality generation as in prior
works [12, 15, 16]. To construct supervision, we use provided
flow labels when available and otherwise derive flow from
depth and camera poses, refining inaccurately derived flow
with off-the-shelf matchers [17, 18]. Separately, we synthesize
thermal views to expand coverage of this underrepresented
modality [19].
On zero-shot real-world benchmarks covering a diverse
set of same-modal and cross-modal pairs across all five
modalities, TartanMatch reduces average EPE relative to the
strongest baseline for each benchmarked modality pair by
61.9% on cross-modal matching and 49.6% on same-modal
matching. Moreover, we show that joint multi-modal training
reduces average EPE relative to pair-specific training by 34.1%
across cross-modal pairs and 17.8% across same-modal pairs,
improving 11 of the 15 evaluated pairs, with the largest gains
on challenging cross-modal pairs.
In conclusion, our contributions are:
• Any-pair multi-modal dense correspondence. We in-
troduce a unified model that predicts dense flow and
covisibility for every ordered pair among RGB, event,
thermal, depth, and projected LiDAR. We further show that
joint training across all modality pairs improves average
per-pair performance relative to training a separate model
on each pair in isolation.
• Lightweight modality adaptation. We adapt an RGB-
pretrained correspondence model using modality-specific
representations and lightweight learned projections, which
outperform the fixed RGB-like rendering of prior works.
A two-stage training schedule, freezing the encoder
before end-to-end fine-tuning, further aids adaptation and
improves matching accuracy.
• Multi-modal training and evaluation suite. We build
a training data mixture that combines real and synthetic
sources across all 25 modality pairs, spanning indoor, out-
door, aerial, and driving scenes. The benchmark evaluation
suite covers all five modalities on real-world zero-shot
datasets.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Same-Modal Correspondence
Same-modal correspondence matches inputs from a single
sensor modality. Most work here concentrates heavily on
RGB–RGB, and methods for other modalities such as event
and thermal are developed independently rather than as
instances of a shared model. Within this setting, optical-flow
methods estimate dense motion between temporally adjacent
frames [5, 6], whereas wide-baseline matching methods target
larger viewpoint and appearance changes [7, 8]. Recent unified
RGB models, such as UFM, bridge these two regimes within
a shared architecture [9]. Beyond RGB, current methods still
remain sensor-specific such as E-RAFT and EMatch for event
streams [10, 11] and SuperThermal for thermal images [20].
TartanMatch extends dense correspondence across modalities,
treating same-modal matching as a subset of an any-pair
correspondence problem spanning RGB, event, thermal, depth,
and projected LiDAR.
B. Cross-Modal Correspondence
Cross-modal correspondence estimates matches between
inputs from different sensor modalities, but most existing
methods are limited to a few modality pairs. XoFTR and SC3EF
focus on RGB–Thermal matching [12, 13], while Walters
et al. [21] estimates dense multispectral correspondence for
RGB–FIR and evaluates transfer to RGB–NIR. MINIMA and
MatchAnything broaden this coverage by fine-tuning existing
RGB matchers on pairs whose non-RGB view is generated from
a real RGB image: MINIMA uses a diffusion model for IR and
Depth Anything V2 for depth [15], and MatchAnything uses
image-translation models for thermal and nighttime views [16].


3
Correspondence labels are therefore inherited from the RGB
source rather than measured, and no training pair contains two
non-RGB views. Moreover, their standard image backbones
encode non-RGB inputs through fixed RGB-like renderings,
which can collapse sensor-specific information such as event
timing or geometric depth. TartanMatch instead trains one
model on all 25 directed pairs among five modalities, including
pairs in which neither view is RGB. Each modality is encoded
in its own representation and mapped to the shared backbone
through lightweight learned projections.
C. Multi-modal Data for Supervision
Prior work has introduced a rich collection of multi-
modal sensory datasets, from synthetic engines that render
several modalities in perfect alignment [22] to real captures
spanning RGB, event, thermal, and depth or LiDAR sensors [1–
4, 23]. However, these resources are distributed across datasets
with different sensor subsets and rarely provide cross-modal
correspondence labels directly. Flow derived from depth and
camera geometry may also be inaccurate because of invalid
measurements or calibration errors. Constructing a broad
correspondence training set from these resources therefore
requires substantial data integration and label processing.
Consequently, prior methods have typically relied on RGB-
derived synthetic modality pairs [15, 16] or individual sensor
datasets with native supervision [10, 12]. To address this
limitation, we integrate diverse real and synthetic datasets
into a comprehensive training and evaluation suite, deriving
flow from geometry and refining unreliable labels.
III. METHOD
A. Overview
TartanMatch predicts dense correspondence between any
pair drawn from five modalities: RGB, thermal, event, depth,
and projected LiDAR, denoted M. Given a source input Xs of
modality ms ∈M and a target input Xt of modality mt ∈M,
the model predicts
(ϕs→t, Cs→t) = gθ(Πms(Xs), Πmt(Xt)) ,
(1)
where ϕs→t ∈R2×H×W is the source-to-target dense flow and
Cs→t ∈[0, 1]H×W is the per-pixel probability that a source
pixel is covisible in the target view. Each Πm is a modality-
specific adaptation that maps a sensor’s measurements to a
three-channel tensor (Sec. III-B), and gθ is a correspondence
backbone shared by all modality pairs (Sec. III-C).
In this work, we instantiate gθ with UFM [9], as shown in
Fig. 2. We retain UFM’s DINOv2 encoder, information-sharing
transformer, and DPT prediction heads without architectural
modification. The pretrained backbone is adapted to the five
modalities with a two-stage schedule on a multi-modal data
mixture (Secs. III-D and III-E).
B. Modality Adaptation
The modality adapter Πm maps an input observation X of
modality m to a three-channel tensor Z = Πm(X) ∈R3×H×W
that the pretrained backbone can consume. The central design
choice is how each sensor gets there: instead of hand-rendering
non-RGB sensors as fixed RGB-like visualizations, as in prior
cross-modal matching pipelines [15, 16], we keep each modality
in a representation suited to it and let a learned 1×1 projection
decide how to expose it to the backbone.
a) RGB and Thermal: RGB images are used as is, Zrgb =
Xrgb, and single-channel thermal intensity is replicated across
the three channels. Neither requires learned parameters.
b) Events: Events are represented as a voxel volume [24].

C. Shared Correspondence Backbone
The three-channel tensors Zs = Πms(Xs) and Zt =
Πmt(Xt) are processed by the same UFM backbone regardless
of the modality pair. Both are encoded into patch tokens by the
shared DINOv2 encoder; the two token sets are concatenated
with view-index positional encodings and processed by the
information-sharing transformer, which exchanges information
between the two views; and DPT heads decode the source-view
tokens into the flow field ϕs→t and covisibility logits ℓC, from
which Cs→t = σ(ℓC). All backbone components are initialized
from UFM’s RGB-pretrained weights. We initialize Pe to pass
the first three event bins through and Pg to replicate log-depth
across all three channels. We adopt UFM’s base configuration,
which excludes its optional local refinement module.

D. Two-Stage Training
We adapt the pretrained backbone to the multi-modal mixture
in two stages. Stage 1 freezes the encoder and trains the
projections Pe and Pg together with the information-sharing
transformer and DPT heads. Stage 2 unfreezes the encoder and
fine-tunes the full model end to end. The two stages address
the mismatch between the new modalities and the encoder’s
pretrained input distribution: Stage 1 brings each modality
closer to that distribution while the encoder is held fixed,
whereas Stage 2 absorbs the residual shift that the projections
cannot remove. Sec. IV-F shows that this schedule improves
over one-stage joint training.
E. Training and Evaluation Data
TartanMatch extends UFM to multiple modalities and is
trained on a multi-modal mixture combining synthetic imagery
with real sensor data, summarized in Table I. From a dataset
with k available modalities, all k2 ordered modality-pair types
are eligible for training. We construct each 650k-sample epoch
using fixed sampling rates at both the dataset and modality-pair
levels. Table I reports each dataset’s aggregate share of the
mixture, summed over its modality pairs.
Ground-truth flow is either provided by the dataset or
derived from depth, camera pose, and camera parameters.

that the derived flow is sometimes inaccurate, we refine it:

B. Evaluation protocol
We use five real-world datasets for zero-shot benchmark:
MTV [23], DSERT-RoLL [4], DTU [37], MVSEC [1], and
EDS [38]. Refer to Table I for the modality composition of
each dataset. On MTV, we use the race-track, country-road, and
park scenes. Similarly, we select an Outdoor Day1 sequence for
MVSEC, following E-RAFT. On DSERT-RoLL, we evaluate
same-modality and cross-modality matching on the normal
scenes and degradation robustness on the heavy-snow scene.
For EDS, we sample pairs with pose differences greater than
1◦, 5◦, 10◦, and 20◦.
For each modality pair, we compare against the strongest
available baseline with publicly released weights, ranging from
pair-specific models to cross-modal correspondence models.
UFM [9] is TartanMatch’s RGB-pretrained starting point and
therefore measures the benefit of multi-modal adaptation;
MINIMA [15] and MatchAnything [16] are the strongest
prior methods targeting broad cross-modal correspondence.
RoMa [8] provides the RGB-only counterpart to the RoMa-
based variants of MINIMA and MatchAnything, whereas
RoMa v2 [17] serves as a state-of-the-art RGB-only baseline.
Following MINIMA, we evaluate RGB-only baselines on
non-RGB modalities by rendering each sensor measurement
to a three-channel image with a fixed colormap (RGB-like
rendering). For event correspondence baselines, we include E-
RAFT [10] and EMatch [11], both are specialized in event and
trained on DSEC. For sparse correspondence, we additionally
evaluate XoFTR [12] and the LoFTR/ELoFTR variants of
MINIMA and MatchAnything. For comparisons with sparse

C. Cross-Modal Dense Correspondence
Table II reports zero-shot performance on RGB-anchored
(one view RGB) and non-RGB cross-modal (neither view
RGB) pairs against dense and sparse matching baselines.
Among RGB-anchored pairs, TartanMatch leads on most
settings, reducing EPE over the strongest baseline by 65% on
RGB→Depth and 43% on RGB→Event. The gains are larger
for projected-LiDAR pairs, which no baseline sees during
training: the best competitor records at least 30.48px EPE,
whereas TartanMatch remains below 12px. On Event→LiDAR
and Thermal→LiDAR, TartanMatch reduces EPE by 3.2×
and 3.7× and lowers the 5px outlier ratio by 31 and 16
points, respectively. TartanMatch also consistently outperforms
sparse matching baselines when evaluated only at their selected
correspondences. Fig. 4 further shows more complete warps and
coherent flow than MINIMA across representative same-modal
and cross-modal pairs.
Table III evaluates pose estimation on DTU and EDS
for RGB→depth and RGB→event, respectively. On DTU,
TartanMatch raises AUC@20◦by 42% over the strongest
baseline while matching it at AUC@5◦. On EDS, where all
baselines struggle, it improves AUC across thresholds by 2.1–
2.9× while running 7.5× faster than MatchAnything. Together,
these results demonstrate strong cross-modal correspondence
and pose estimation performance.

E. Degraded Scenarios
TartanMatch’s any-pair matching capability enables robust
correspondence estimation when individual sensors degrade.
Table IV evaluates TartanMatch on the heavy-snow sequences
of DSERT-RoLL, testing robustness under RGB degradation.
Snow renders RGB→RGB matching unusable: EPE grows from
2.40 to 30.34 and the 2px outlier ratio reaches 99%. The event-
based pairs stay accurate under the same conditions, with 0.79
EPE on Event→Event and 3.02 EPE on Event→LiDAR. An
any-pair mode lets a platform shift correspondence to sensors
that stay informative.
F. Ablations
Our ablation investigates three questions: (i) whether the
proposed sensor-specific input representations outperform fixed
RGB-like renderings; (ii) whether the two-stage training strat-
egy improves correspondence estimation over one-stage end-
to-end training; and (iii) whether jointly training on multiple
modality pairs benefits the performance of each individual
pair. Owing to compute and time constraints, the ablation
variants follow TartanMatch’s hyperparameters described in
Section IV-A, but train at smaller scale, specified in each
paragraph below. For ablation (a) and (c), we train the model
for 20 epochs, which we find sufficient for convergence. For
the ablation (b), we use the full training mixture and recipe.
We report mean EPE for cross-modality and same-modality
matching.
(a) Input representation. Each variant trains on pairs
from {RGB, Event} and {RGB, Depth} using every training
dataset that provides such modality pairs. (See Table I)
We evaluate the event variants on DSERT-RoLL, the only
benchmark that pairs event with non-event modalities and thus
measures both cross and same-modal performance, and likewise
the depth variants on DTU. In Table VI (a), the event voxel
volume reduces cross-modal (RGB↔Event) EPE from 10.56
to 7.46 over the fixed RGB-like rendering using event polarity
accumulation. In Table VI (b), log-normalized depth with a
validity mask improves over the color-mapped image on both
cross-modal (RGB↔Depth) and same-modal (Depth→Depth)
matching, 12.30 to 11.33 and 6.30 to 5.64, respectively. These
results validate the effectiveness of our modality-specific
input adaptation, showing that simply rendering non-RGB
modalities into RGB-like colormap images leads to suboptimal
correspondence estimation.
(b) One-stage vs. two-stage training. This ablation follows
the full TartanMatch setup: we compare the two-stage recipe
against training the full model end to end for 45 epochs on the
complete training mixture. Table VI (c) shows consistent gains:
cross-modal EPE decreases from 10.21 to 9.80 on DSERT-
RoLL and from 14.01 to 12.38 on DTU, with same-modal
EPE also improving. These results show that the two-stage
schedule enables more effective multi-modal adaptation than
one-stage training, improving both cross-modal and same-
modal matching.
(c) Does multi-modal training help? To limit training
cost without excluding any modality combination, we train 15
pair-specific models, one for each unordered pair of the five
modalities (5 same-modal and 10 cross-modal), and compare
them with a single model jointly trained on all 25 ordered
pairs in TartanAirV2. We evaluate them on the validation split
of TartanAirV2. As shown in Table VII, joint training reduces
mean EPE by 34.1% for cross-modal pairs and 17.8% for
same-modal pairs, improving 11 of the 15 evaluated pairs. The
largest gain is 61.5% on Event→LiDAR, which also has the
highest pair-specific EPE. All four regressions are at most
0.2 px. We hypothesize that joint multi-modal training favors
modality-invariant semantic cues over modality-specific low-
level cues, benefiting challenging pairs at a small cost in fine-
grained matching on those with sub-pixel accuracy. These


8
results demonstrate strong positive transfer where pair-specific
training struggles, with minimal cost on the remaining pairs,
supporting unified multi-modal training.
V. CONCLUSION
We presented TartanMatch, a unified dense correspondence
model that estimates dense flow and covisibility for any
pair between RGB, event, thermal, depth, and projected
LiDAR. A single backbone, inherited from a pretrained RGB
correspondence model, serves all five modalities; the only
per-modality parameters are a thin input projection over a
representation chosen for each sensor. Trained on a data suite
spanning 25 modality pairs, TartanMatch achieves state-of-the-
art zero-shot accuracy on same-modality and cross-modality
pairs, providing the first dense any-pair baseline. Because
each modality costs only an input representation and a thin
projection, future work can extend TartanMatch to new sensors,
such as radar, with few additional parameters. We further plan
to collect real data for underrepresented modalities, such as
thermal and LiDAR, and to apply any-pair correspondence to
downstream tasks such as cross-modal odometry, cross-sensor
calibration, and place recognition.
