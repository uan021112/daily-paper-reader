ABSTRACT
We present Unravel, a training-free framework that turns a single image into an
interactive 3D scene, where every object is an independent and movable asset. Ex-
isting generators either produce one entangled geometry in which no object can
be moved, or predict all objects in one feed-forward pass, where a wrong guess
cannot be detected or repaired. Unravel instead takes a different approach: it first
takes the scene apart, and then puts it back together. Starting from the input im-
age, an agentic harness that iteratively peels the image layer by layer, and adopts
point cloud as major modality for spatial relationship recovery. Specifically, a
vision-language model plans which objects are fully visible, an image editor re-
moves them and restores the occluded regions. Every edit is verified before being
incorporated into the reconstruction. This process gradually turns occluded parts
of the scene into direct observation, allowing objects to be reconstructed when
they are best exposed rather than hallucinated from the original image alone. The
scene is then rebuilt in reverse removal order, so that the supporting objects are
naturally placed before the objects they support. Since a single-view image only
reveals part of an object, we treat the observed points as a lower bound on its
size: an asset may grow to cover its points, but can not shrink away from them.
Finally, we physically settle the reconstructed scene to remove floating objects
and interpenetrations, followed by an agentic audit that corrects remaining place-
ment errors. Extensive experiments on diverse indoor scenes show that Unravel

INTRODUCTION
Embodied agents act on the world around them. They move objects, open containers, and rearrange
furniture. Training them in real spaces is too expensive, so they are trained in interactive 3D sim-
ulated scenes, where every object can be moved, replaced, or re-arranged (Yang et al., 2024c; Xia
et al., 2026). Building these simulated scenes by hand is costly as well, and recovering them from
single images offers an appealing alternative. A photograph is cheap to collect, but what we recover
from it should go beyond appearance. The scene must stay physically meaningful when its objects
are moved.
Many reconstruction methods recover scene geometry without explicit object decomposition (Wang
et al., 2024; 2025a; Kong et al., 2026). They reconstruct visible surfaces but leave occluded regions
incomplete and do not directly produce movable assets. Object-centric generators instead predict
separate meshes and poses from a cluttered image (Huang et al., 2025; Meng et al., 2026; Chen et al.,
2026). However, learning 3D priors requires costly data and training. Under heavy occlusion, errors
in shape completion can distort individual assets and propagate into the layout (Fig. 2). Because
object recovery and placement are coupled, these errors are difficult to isolate and correct.
We present Unravel, a training-free framework that deconstructs a scene before recomposing it
(Fig. 1). At each round, a vision-language model selects exposed objects for removal. An image
editor removes them and completes the freed regions, and a verifier checks the edit before it con-
tributes to reconstruction (Fig. 4). Occluded content is therefore completed in image space, where
intermediate predictions can be inspected and corrected. Each object is captured immediately before
removal and converted from its best-observed snapshot into an independent 3D asset.
Iterative removal can provide cleaner inputs for object reconstruction. Our key insight is to use the
deconstruction record as a geometric reference for recomposition. We align and fuse the per-round
point maps while retaining each point’s source round and pixel. Under a fixed camera, source rounds
distinguish successive surface observations along the same image ray. Instance masks associate
these observations with assets, producing point clusters that anchor their placement. Reversing the
removal order also guides assembly from supporting objects to the objects they carry.
Recomposition must respect the incompleteness of these clusters. Unravel registers each asset under
one-sided extent constraints on under-observed axes. The observed spans provide lower bounds

rather than forcing a complete asset to match a partial surface. Physical settling reduces floating
and interpenetration while limiting displacement from the geometric anchors. A final agentic audit
inspects scene renderings and refines remaining placement errors. Ablations show improved asset

show more faithful layouts. Our contributions are threefold.
• We introduce Unravel, a training-free framework for reconstructing interactive 3D scenes
from a single image. Verified layer-wise deconstruction generates independently movable
assets from their best-observed snapshots.
• We develop a recomposition strategy that reuses the deconstruction record. Round-and-
pixel provenance anchors each asset to its instance cluster, and reverse removal order guides
support-aware assembly. The strategy combines one-sided extent constraints with physical
settling.
• Extensive experiments across diverse indoor scenes demonstrate that Unravel can faithfully
reconstruct interactive 3D scenes from a single image.

RELATED WORK
2.1
FEED-FORWARD 3D RECONSTRUCTION
Feed-forward models offer an alternative to per-scene optimization, including in pose-free set-
tings. DUSt3R (Wang et al., 2024) regresses aligned pointmaps from uncalibrated image pairs.
Its successors support robust matching (MASt3R (Leroy et al., 2024)), streaming reconstruc-
tion (Spann3R (Wang & Agapito, 2025), CUT3R Wang et al. (2025b)), and thousand-view input
(Fast3R (Yang et al., 2025)). VGGT (Wang et al., 2025a) jointly predicts cameras, depth, pointmaps,
and tracks for hundreds of views in one forward pass. For monocular input, Metric3Dv2 (Hu et al.,
2024) estimates zero-shot metric depth and surface normals. Depth Anything V2 (Yang et al., 2024a)
provides relative depth. The MoGe series progresses from affine-invariant pointmaps (Wang et al.,
2025c) to detailed metric geometry (Wang et al., 2026; Kong et al., 2026). These methods recover
per-pixel geometry without explicit object decomposition, leaving occluded regions incomplete and
objects inseparable.
2.2
3D OBJECT ASSET GENERATION
Image-to-3D models generate textured meshes from clean object images. TripoSR (Tochilkin et al.,
2024) directly infers geometry in one forward pass. Multi-view methods (InstantMesh (Xu et al.,
2024), LGM (Tang et al., 2024b), Unique3D (Wu et al., 2024)) synthesize views before reconstruct-
ing geometry. Native 3D latent models (CLAY (Zhang et al., 2024), TRELLIS (Xiang et al., 2025),
Hunyuan3D-2 (Zhao et al., 2025), TripoSG (Li et al., 2025), Direct3D-S2 (Wu et al., 2026)) learn di-
rectly from asset corpora. TRELLIS.2 (Xiang et al., 2026) generates high-resolution textured assets,
while NI-Tex (Shan et al., 2026a) produces garment textures from non-isometric image inputs. Part-
aware models add component-level structure. PartCrafter (Lin et al., 2026) jointly generates part
meshes from a single image, and HoloPart (Yang et al., 2026) completes segmented parts. These
models provide strong priors for individual asset generation. Extending them to complete scene
generation remains challenging.
2.3
INTERACTIVE 3D SCENE GENERATION
Object-level scene generation produces independently editable assets rather than fused geom-
etry.
Text-driven methods arrange objects through layout diffusion (DiffuScene (Tang et al.,
2024a), InstructScene (Lin & Mu, 2024), PhyScene (Yang et al., 2024b)) or LLM-based planning
(Holodeck (Yang et al., 2024c)). Agentic systems (SAGE (Xia et al., 2026), SceneSmith (Pfaff et al.,
2026), SceneAssistant (Luo et al., 2026)) refine scenes through visual feedback. GPT-6 Astra (Ope-
nAI, 2026) also supports image understanding, editing, and 3D tool use. Yet semantic instructions
alone underspecify the geometry of a reference image. ControlRoom3D (Schult et al., 2024) and
WonderWorld (Yu et al., 2025) offer layout-conditioned and single-image scene synthesis, respec-
tively, without targeting independent object editing.

and-pixel provenance anchors each asset to its reconstructed geometry, and reverse removal order
guides support-aware assembly.

METHODS
Unravel deconstructs the scene before recomposing it (Fig. 3). The deconstruction stage (Sec-
tion 3.2) removes objects layer by layer and extracts independent assets from their best-observed
snapshots. Per-round reconstructions are aligned and fused into a point cloud that retains each ob-
ject’s location. Recomposition (Section 3.3) places assets in reverse removal order. Each asset is
registered to its point cluster, and physical settling refines the assembled scene.

3.2
AGENTIC LAYER-WISE DECONSTRUCTION
We use 2D foundation models to plan, edit, and verify layer-wise removals without task-specific
training. Occluded content is inferred through verified image completion before 3D reconstruction.
With the camera fixed, we align each reconstruction to the first round and fuse them into a point
cloud. This cloud provides the primary geometric reference for recovering spatial relationships.
The Deconstruction Loop
The loop uses a VLM planner and verifier with an image editor (Fig. 4,
upper block). The planner selects fully visible objects for removal and marks the rest for protection.
Their bounding boxes are drawn on a plan image. Walls and the floor are preserved, and contents
are removed before containers. Guided by the plan, the editor removes targets from Ik and inpaints
the freed regions to produce Ik+1. These edits provide more complete object views for later rounds.
The verifier checks for missed targets, unintended removals, and fabricated content before an edit
is committed. Changed pixels between Ik and the verified Ik+1 form the edit mask Ek. The loop
repeats until only the empty room remains.
We extract each asset immediately before removal from its best-observed snapshot. A segmentation
model (Carion et al., 2026) operates inside its bounding box to obtain the instance mask Si, improv-
ing precision over full-image segmentation. Appendix A.1 details box localization. We store the
snapshot and semantics. gpt-image-2 (OpenAI, 2026) converts the snapshot to a canonical image,
which an image-to-3D model (Xiang et al., 2026) turns into a textured mesh. The asset index records
the mesh, mask, semantics, box, and removal round for recomposition.

rebuilds the scene from a different edited image. Without explicit control, errors in scale and pose
can misalign surfaces across rounds and distort the fused geometry.

Point Attribution and Instance Clusters
Labeling a single-view cloud is normally ambiguous: a
pixel admits one label, while the surfaces stacked behind it belong to different objects. Deconstruc-
tion dissolves this ambiguity. The camera is locked, so a pixel p is one fixed ray, and every round
observes this ray at a different depth. We call the points added to the cloud at round k the layer of
round k, with the layer 1 the whole first reconstruction. A pixel now holds one point per layer, and
we label points rather than pixels.
For the point x of layer k at pixel p, the candidates are the objects ai whose instance masks Si cover
p and that are still present in round k, namely, whose removal round satisfies ri ≥k. They queue
along the ray in the order of removal, and we label x by the frontmost object. If nothing in the queue
survives to round k, the ray shows the room, and we label x by 0, the room. Because a label depends
only on its own layer and on the fixed masks, later removals never change labels already assigned.
Points labeled i form the cluster Ci of asset ai, and points labeled 0 form the room structure, from
which the environment shell E is fitted. The removal sequence provides the depth ordering along
each ray. Masks come from each object’s best-observed image and may include content completed
by earlier edits.
3.3
SUPPORT-AWARE SCENE RECOMPOSITION
Deconstruction records each object’s removal round and geometric observations. We use both to
recompose the scene (Fig. 3(b)). Removal rounds guide support-aware assembly. Point provenance
anchors registration and physical settling.
From Occlusion Order to Support Order
The deconstruction planner removes contents before
their containers, since a container becomes fully visible only after its contents leave. For a support
relation i →j, where object i rests on object j, the removal order therefore satisfies ri < rj. We
assemble the scene in descending removal round, starting from the empty shell. For every support
relation i →j, asset j is then placed before asset i. Every object arrives when its support is already
in place, so placement always has a physical reference. Large supports from later rounds enter first,
and small foreground objects from early rounds enter last.
The fused cloud provides the placement evidence. By the attribution rule of Section 3.2, every point
belongs to one asset, and the points of asset ai form its cluster Ci. The remaining points belong to
the room structure, and fitting them gives the environment shell E.

4.2
QUALITATIVE COMPARISONS
Figure 5 compares the four baselines on multiple scenes, each shown from two views. MIDI relies
on priors learned from limited 3D data, so heavily occluded objects often suffer severe generation er-
rors. SAM 3D produces reasonable assets in most cases, but its textures tend to drift. Its asset quality
falls short of TRELLIS.2, a dedicated image-to-3D model, and its training is expensive. Its place-
ment estimates are likewise imprecise, which easily introduces floating and interpenetration. The
direct Astra variant captures broad spatial relations, yet its self-generated meshes are visibly coarser.
Sharing our assets removes this gap, yet the red boxes still reveal placement discrepancies. Since the
meshes are shared, these errors come from placement rather than asset quality. Across these scenes,
Unravel preserves the input layout most closely. Appendix A.4 reports more reconstructions with
per-round edits, extracted assets, and multi-view renderings. Figure 11 shows reconstructions from
six real indoor images.

rounds. We compare the resulting 2D masks, 3D assets, and scene geometry in Table 1.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Unravel is limited by current image editing models. In highly cluttered scenes, edits may alter the
appearance or position of remaining objects. Such drift can accumulate across rounds and disrupt
point-cloud alignment and asset placement. Our verification improves editing reliability, but minor
errors may persist. Future work could combine stronger image editing models with geometric feed-
back to better preserve object identity and spatial relationships across rounds. Uncertainty-aware
checks could trigger targeted retries before small errors accumulate.

A.1
ACCURATE AND STABLE BOUNDING BOX LOCALIZATION
Every removal target needs an accurate box, since the editor removes exactly what the boxes mark.
Each target comes with a name and a short description. A vision-language model boxes all targets in
one call, one box per index, so every box stays bound to its target. The prompt requires tight boxes
that touch the outermost pixels of the object. Two boxes with IoU of at least τdup are treated as one
object boxed twice, and the extra one is detected again.
A global call can drift systematically in cluttered scenes, and it cannot notice its own drift. Each
global box is therefore probed with one independent local sample of the same target. If the probe
box overlaps the global box with IoU at least τp, the global box is confirmed. Otherwise the box
is considered drifted, and the target is re-detected by local detection. Local detection samples the
VLM n times independently for that target and keeps the samples that agree with at least one other
sample,
C = { bi : ∃j̸ = i, IoU(bi, bj) ≥τc }.
The element-wise median of these samples suppresses outliers and replaces the drifted box. When-
ever the probe or local detection fails to return a box, the global box is kept. The global call covers
all targets at once, while local detection stays stable. The cross-check keeps the strengths of both.
We use n = 3, τdup = 0.85, τc = 0.7, and τp = 0.5 in all experiments.
The verified target boxes are drawn in red with their indices, and the objects to protect in green, on
the plan image. The annotated image is fed back to the VLM once more, and any movable object
still without a box is added as an object to protect. A missed object is therefore protected rather than
deleted by mistake.
A.2

The registration transform combines a global scale with bounded per-axis adjustments in the object’s
frame. An under-observed axis may only grow, since its measured span underestimates the true one.

asset is too small, we apply a single uniform scale instead, so the asset keeps its proportions. Each

never shrink away from them. Image cues can refine orientation and translation, but only when this

A.3
ASSET SCORING PROTOCOL
The asset comparison in Section 4.3 is scored as follows. We evaluate all 160 objects from the five
test scenes. The human evaluation involved 15 participants. Each object contributes two meshes,
one from direct reconstruction and one from Unravel. Both meshes are rendered from the same four
viewpoints at 90◦intervals. The two renders appear side by side in random order, and the rater does
not know which mesh comes from which pipeline. Each pair is judged against the same reference,
a crop of the object’s region from the image of its best-observed round. The rater answers one
question, how faithfully each mesh reproduces the object in the reference.
Scores range from 1 to 10. A score of 1 to 3 means the shape is wrong or essential parts are
missing. A score of 4 to 6 means the object category is recognizable but the structure is deformed or
incomplete. A score of 7 or 8 means the overall shape is right with minor surface artifacts. A score
of 9 or 10 means the mesh is faithful in shape and structure, including the parts that were occluded
in the input image. We use GPT-5.5 (OpenAI, 2026) as the VLM rater. It receives the same renders,
reference, and rubric. The model and the human raters score independently, and neither sees the
other’s ratings. Table 1 reports the mean score of each side over all 160 objects, and Fig. 6 shows
representative examples of the quality gap.
