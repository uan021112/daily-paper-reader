Tactile-Guided Robot Learning: From Representation Learning to
Foundation Models
Kiyanoush Nazari*, Luchen Li, Haoshu Fang, Nima Fazeli, Zhibin Li,
Fumiya Iida, Thomas George Thuruthel
Abstract—Tactile sensing is becoming central to robust, adap-
tive, and dexterous robotic manipulation. Beyond its traditional
role in contact detection and reactive control, touch is in-
creasingly used to learn transferable representations, condition
manipulation policies, and support embodied physical reasoning.
Yet existing surveys largely emphasize tactile hardware and
perception, leaving the rapid shift toward learning-based tac-
tile manipulation insufficiently synthesized. This paper reviews
tactile-guided skill learning from a learning-centric perspective,
organized by the functional role of touch across the learning
and control pipeline. We examine tactile representation and
policy learning, tactile foundation models, predictive and adaptive
control, and generalization across objects, tasks, sensors, embod-
iments, and simulation-to-real transfer. Across these directions,
we identify common design principles and remaining challenges
in learning transferable physical representations, integrating
semantic reasoning with contact-level control, and scaling tactile
learning across heterogeneous data and hardware. Finally, we
outline emerging directions toward tactile foundation and world
models that can predict, reason about, and adapt physical
interactions. Together, this review characterizes the transition of
touch from a task-specific feedback signal toward a core modality
for general-purpose embodied intelligence. An accompanying
database of the reviewed literature and associated resources is
available at paper web page.
Index Terms—Tactile sensing, robot learning, robotic manipu-
lation, tactile world models.
I. INTRODUCTION
Dexterous manipulation remains a major challenge in de-
veloping robots capable of human-level adaptability in un-
structured environments (Lepora, 2024). Despite substantial
advances in perception, planning, control, locomotion, and
embodied AI, robots still struggle with everyday contact-rich
tasks such as precision insertion, deformable-object manipu-
lation, and assembly under uncertainty (Billard and Kragic,
2019). Unlike more structured robotic tasks, manipulation
requires continuous regulation of physical interactions among
the robot, objects, and environment (Cui and Trinkle, 2021).
Successful manipulation therefore depends not only on de-
tecting contact, but on estimating and regulating evolving
contact locations, forces, friction, compliance, and interaction
dynamics, often across multiple simultaneous contacts. These
quantities are difficult to infer reliably from vision alone,
particularly under occlusion and during fine manipulation,

Recent tactile sensors can capture high-resolution contact
geometry, force distributions, deformation, and slip (Team,
2026; Zhao et al., 2025b), while advances in imitation learning
(Tsuji et al., 2025), reinforcement learning (Kim et al., 2026),

learn complex manipulation skills from data (O’Neill et al.,
2024). Yet these developments remain only partially con-
nected, where modern policies are still predominantly vision-
centric and often treat touch as auxiliary information (Black
et al., 2024; Comanici et al., 2025). This limits their ability
to reason about physical interactions in tasks where contact
dynamics, rather than visual appearance alone, determine
successful execution.
The role of touch in robot learning is consequently shifting
from estimating low-level quantities such as contact, slip,
force, and object properties (Howe, 1993) toward providing
physically grounded information throughout the learning and
control pipeline. Tactile observations now support represen-
tation learning (Higuera et al., 2024), multimodal policy
learning, physical reasoning (Huang et al., 2025b; Morissette
et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2024), adaptive decision making,
and embodied world modeling (Cheng et al., 2026). This shift
reframes the central question from how robots can sense touch
to how robots can learn from and act through touch.
Despite rapidly growing work across tactile sensing, robot
learning, computer vision, and embodied AI, this literature
remains fragmented. Existing surveys largely emphasize sen-
sor hardware, sensing principles, classical tactile perception, or
earlier tactile-guided manipulation and control (Kroemer et al.,
2021; Li et al., 2020). Many also predate recent developments
in foundation models and VLA systems and therefore do not
capture the emerging role of touch within modern learning-
based manipulation (Cho et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2025; Hu
et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024b; Jahanshahi et al., 2025; Jin
et al., 2023; Kroemer et al., 2021; Lepora, 2024; Liu et al.,
2023; Luo et al., 2025; Man et al., 2022; Mandil et al., 2023;
Meribout et al., 2024; Xie and Correll, 2025; Xu et al., 2023;
Yao and Wang, 2024; Yu and Liu, 2025; Zhang et al., 2022;
Zhu et al., 2022). A unified learning-centric account of how
tactile information contributes across representation, policy
learning, embodied reasoning, and generalization is therefore
still lacking.

II. TACTILE REPRESENTATION LEARNING
Tactile sensing provides localized observations of physical
interaction shaped by contact dynamics, sensor mechanics,
and robot motion (Johansson and Flanagan, 2009). Raw
tactile measurements, however, are often high-dimensional
and sensor-dependent, with manipulation-relevant properties
such as contact geometry, loading, deformation, and slip only
implicitly encoded. Tactile representations bridge sensing and
action by extracting compact, task-relevant features while
preserving the spatial and temporal information required for
perception, prediction, and control. While early approaches
relied on physically motivated descriptors such as marker
displacement, pressure distributions, and vibration spectra,
recent work increasingly learns representations through super-
vised, self-supervised, multimodal, and multi-sensor training
(Cao et al., 2026), aiming for reusable features with reduced
dependence on task-specific labels and sensor characteristics.
This section examines four aspects of tactile representa-
tion learning, including learning informative features from
unlabeled interaction data (Sec. III-A); fusing touch with
complementary sensing modalities (Sec. III-B); aligning touch
with pre-trained vision-language embedding spaces to exploit
their semantic structure (Sec. III-C); and, finally, assessing
whether representations learned on particular sensors, tasks,
or embodiments transfer beyond their training conditions
(Sec. III-D).
A. Self-Supervised Tactile Representation Learning
Self-supervised learning enables tactile representations to
be learned from unlabeled interaction data (Gui et al., 2024;
Lee et al., 2018), avoiding physically motivated preprocess-
ing such as marker tracking (Li et al., 2023), finite-element
modeling (Sferrazza et al., 2019), or task-specific descriptors.
Unlike vision, where large-scale datasets and pretrained mod-
els are readily available (Russakovsky et al., 2015), tactile
data require physical interaction and remain strongly sensor-
dependent. Recent approaches therefore exploit data collected
through robotic exploration, demonstration, or in-the-wild

interaction (Yang et al., 2022), using reconstruction, con-
trastive learning, self-distillation, masked modeling, or action-
conditioned prediction (Mandil et al., 2022) to learn reusable
tactile features.

state to retain sufficient information for recovering the original
observation, or components removed from it. Given a tactile
observation x, an encoder fθ produces z = fθ(x), from which

Their central challenge remains defining appropriate invari-
ances, since variations in contact location or pressure may
represent either nuisance or task-relevant physical information.
T-DEX (Guzey et al., 2023) uses BYOL to pretrain a tactile
encoder on 2.5 hours of teleoperated play with distributed
uSkin sensors, enabling nearest-neighbor action retrieval from
a visuo-tactile latent space and data-efficient manipulation
from few demonstrations. exUMI (Xu et al., 2025a) instead
introduces Tactile Predictive Pretraining, using a Transformer
with latent diffusion to predict future tactile states conditioned
on context and actions; the frozen encoder is subsequently
transferred to Diffusion Policies, preserving deformation and
contact-transition information overlooked by static objectives.
Sparsh (Higuera et al., 2024) provides a large-scale compar-
ison of these paradigms, benchmarking masked autoencoding,
DINO self-distillation (Caron et al., 2021), and I-JEPA/V-
JEPA (Assran et al., 2023) on more than 460,000 unlabeled
tactile observations through a benchmark of six touch-centric
tasks. The stronger transfer of DINO and I-JEPA indicates
that reconstructing tactile appearance does not necessarily pre-
serve the mechanical information required downstream. More
broadly, reconstruction, contrastive, and predictive objectives
differ primarily in what they define as physically relevant
structure: force fields, cross-modal semantics, augmentation
invariance, or action-conditioned contact evolution. Their ef-
fectiveness therefore depends not simply on scale, but on
whether the learning objective preserves interaction variables
relevant to downstream manipulation. These methods never-
theless primarily learn representations from individual tactile
streams; we next consider representations learned jointly with
complementary sensory modalities.

Synchronized vision-touch pairs provide the most direct

masked current observation with the CLIP embedding of a
future unmasked frame, thereby relating contact to subsequent
outcomes. Tac-DINO (Li et al., 2026a) instead addresses spa-
tial granularity: rather than aligning local touch with an entire
image, it aligns touch with the corresponding visual patch,
outperforming image-level alignment on over 20,000 contacts
across 505 objects. Together, these approaches show that
both the temporal target and spatial granularity of alignment
determine what interaction structure is preserved.
UniTouch (Yang et al., 2024) aligns touch with frozen
image embeddings already connected to language and sound,
indirectly transferring this broader semantic structure and
enabling zero-shot material recognition, cross-modal retrieval,
and touch-conditioned generation. Octopi (Yu et al., 2024)
instead maps tactile embeddings into tokens consumed by
a frozen large language model, enabling open-vocabulary
reasoning about material and physical properties without ex-
plicit tactile-text alignment, although its reasoning remains
dependent on the backbone’s physical knowledge. Finally, FG-
CLTP (Ma et al., 2026a) complements semantic descriptors
with quantitative physical information by converting tactile
deformation into 3D point clouds, discretizing force and
contact geometry into numerical tokens, and jointly aligning
them with point clouds and language. Its sensor-independent
3D representation additionally supports sim-to-real and cross-
sensor transfer. Overall, these methods progressively extend
tactile alignment from global semantic correspondence toward
spatially localized, temporally predictive, multimodal, and
physically grounded representations.
D. Transferability and Generalization
Recent work increasingly targets general-purpose tactile
encoders rather than task-specific features (Feng et al., 2025;
Higuera et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024),
following the pre-training paradigm of vision and language,
z = fθ(T), where T ∼psrc(T). Such pre-training is valuable
only if representations remain informative under shifts from
psrc(T) to a target distribution ptgt(T). This is particularly
challenging for touch because measurements are tightly cou-
pled to sensor hardware, with transfer failures occurring even
between instances of the same sensor model due to manufac-
turing variation (Agarwal et al., 2025; Gupta et al., 2025).
Cross-sensor transfer follows three main strategies. First,
joint multi-sensor pre-training seeks sensor-invariant structure
from heterogeneous data. T3 (Zhao et al., 2024) combines
sensor-specific input encoders with a shared Transformer
trained across 13 sensors and 11 tasks, while AnyTouch (Feng
et al., 2025) learns cross-sensor correspondence in dynamic
interactions and ToucHD (Feng et al., 2026a) extends this
toward hierarchical contact dynamics. These approaches pro-
duce reusable encoders but require large jointly curated multi-
sensor datasets. Second, pairwise transfer learns mappings
between particular sensors, GenForce (Chen et al., 2026c)
translates heterogeneous observations into a shared marker
representation while preserving source force labels; CTTP
(Rodriguez et al., 2025b) aligns GelSlim and Soft Bubble
through InfoNCE; and Touch2Touch (Rodriguez et al., 2025a)
translates between them using diffusion. Such methods avoid
large joint corpora but must be retrained for new sensor pairs.
Third, SITR (Gupta et al., 2025) explicitly conditions a mod-
ified Vision Transformer on calibration images, disentangling
sensor-specific effects from contact geometry and thereby
extending transfer to new sensors given a small calibration
set.
These strategies trade data scale, transfer specificity, and
deployment assumptions, where joint pre-training scales across


6
sensors but requires heterogeneous corpora; pairwise methods
reduce this requirement but scale poorly with sensor diversity;
and disentanglement offers broader transfer at the cost of cal-
ibration. More importantly, transfer across tasks, objects, and
robot embodiments remains comparatively underexplored at
the representation level, and whether sensor-invariant features
ultimately yield transferable manipulation policies remains an
open question revisited in Section VII.
Across the paradigms reviewed above, no tactile repre-
sentation is universally optimal. Representations grounded in
physical quantities such as force, contact geometry, or action-
conditioned dynamics can improve invariance by abstracting
away sensor-specific measurements, but excessive abstraction
may discard fine texture, deformation, slip, or other task-
relevant cues. The central challenge is therefore to preserve
information required for downstream prediction and control
while suppressing nuisance variation. Evaluating this balance
remains difficult because benchmarks are fragmented across
sensors, tasks, and datasets, motivating standardized evaluation
of representation quality, transfer, and downstream manipula-
tion performance. The following section examines how these
representations are used for action generation.
III. TACTILE-CONDITIONED POLICY LEARNING
Given an informative tactile representation, the next chal-
lenge is to exploit touch for action generation. Unlike classical
tactile control (Howe, 1993), which typically reduces touch
to interpretable variables for hand-crafted controllers, tactile-
conditioned policies learn directly from rich tactile obser-
vations, allowing contact state, force distribution, slip, and
compliance to influence actions as physical interactions evolve.

contact locations and forces relative to scene geometry. These
approaches illustrate a progression from treating touch as an

interaction occurs.
Scaling IL requires reducing dependence on task-specific
robot teleoperation. T-DEX exploits task-agnostic robotic play
(Guzey et al., 2023), while exUMI (Xu et al., 2025a) uses
a handheld visuo-tactile interface to collect action-aligned
human interactions. DexVLA (Wen et al., 2025) combines
VR teleoperation for macro arm motion with autonomous
DexGrasp-VLA control using visual and tactile feedback
for fine hand manipulation, reducing operator burden while
collecting coordinated arm-hand demonstrations (Cui et al.,
2025). To address embodiment mismatch, TactAlign (Wi et al.,
2025) separately encodes human and robot tactile observations
and uses rectified flow (Liu et al., 2022) to align their latent
spaces from unpaired demonstrations, which subsequently
condition a shared ACT policy (Zhao et al., 2023). Together,
these approaches broaden tactile demonstrations through play,
portable human data collection, shared autonomy, and cross-
embodiment alignment.

Zhao et al., 2026a), while diffusion-based policies capture

distills task-specific simulated PPO (Schulman et al., 2017)

approaches separate learning the contact state from learning
how to act upon it.
Tactile RL faces a distinctive exploration challenge, where
informative tactile observations arise only through physical

learning signals.
To reduce costly real-world exploration, tactile RL increas-

ing. (Yin et al., 2025a) trains a PPO teacher using privi-

simulated touch. Text2Touch (Field et al., 2025) similarly
transfers behavior from privileged simulation to a tactile-
proprioceptive student. This formulation is particularly suited
to tactile manipulation, simulation exposes physical variables
unavailable at deployment, while touch provides an observable
interface through which the student can infer their action-
relevant effects, including changes in friction, inertia, contact
geometry, and disturbances.
Simulation improves RL scalability and safety but introduces
a substantial tactile domain gap because sensor observations
depend on deformation, contact mechanics, material prop-
erties, and transduction. One strategy adapts observations,
(Church et al., 2022) trains PPO policies in simulation for
edge/surface following, rolling, pushing, and balancing, then
uses Pix2Pix (Isola et al., 2017) to translate real TacTip (Ward-

representation itself. (Yin et al., 2025a) uses binary normal
and ternary shear signals, enabling scalable simulation and
zero-shot deployment on a ReSkin-equipped (Bhirangi et al.,
2021) Allegro hand, while Text2Touch (Field et al., 2025)
similarly combines simulation and distillation for a TacTip-
equipped dexterous hand. These approaches expose a fun-
damental trade-off where high-fidelity touch preserves richer
interaction information but is harder to simulate, whereas
abstract contact representations simplify transfer at the risk
of discarding control-relevant cues. Tactile RL methods are

Overall, touch contributes to RL by revealing latent inter-
action states, shaping exploration and rewards, and enabling
deployable policies to infer physical properties represented ex-
plicitly during privileged training. Yet direct real-world tactile
RL remains costly and potentially unsafe. Consequently, recent
systems combine RL with pretrained representations (Tang
et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2026), privileged distillation (Field et al.,
2025; Yin et al., 2025a), structured exploration and rewards
(Field et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025), and tactile sim-to-real

fore increasingly serves as the adaptive policy-optimization
component within a broader tactile learning pipeline rather
than as unconstrained end-to-end learning from raw physical
interaction.
C. Diffusion and Generative Policies
Diffusion policies have rapidly become a prominent ap-
proach to learning manipulation behaviors from demonstra-
tions, particularly for tasks requiring coordinated and tem-
porally extended actions (Chi et al., 2025; Gu et al., 2026;
Nguyen et al., 2026). Their key distinction from conven-
tional behavioral cloning lies in replacing point-wise action
regression with conditional generative modeling where rather
than committing to a single action for a given observation,
the policy learns a distribution over plausible future action
sequences. This property is particularly relevant to contact-rich
manipulation, where demonstrations can be inherently multi-
modal. Variations in object pose, contact geometry, friction, or
deformation may admit several successful interaction strate-
gies, while averaging these behaviors through deterministic
regression can produce physically ineffective actions. Diffu-
sion policies instead preserve this diversity while generating
temporally coherent action chunks, making them an attractive
policy class for tactile-guided manipulation.

Diffusion can operate at different levels of the manipulation
hierarchy. PP-Tac (Lin et al., 2025) learns coordinated arm-
hand trajectories for thin deformable-object manipulation from
500,000 optimized trajectories, conditioning a Transformer
on proprioception and tactile deformation with an auxiliary
forward-kinematics loss. TacDiffusion (Wu et al., 2025b)
instead generates 6D wrench commands from force/torque
history and end-effector velocity using a DDPM (Ho et al.,

behavior-tree tactile controller; the generated wrench feeds an

insertion configurations. At a higher level of abstraction,
DexterityGen (DexGen) (Yin et al., 2025b) trains diffusion
on approximately 10 billion transitions generated by PPO
manipulation primitives to produce feasible finger-keypoint
trajectories, subsequently converted to joint commands. Al-
though DexGen uses proprioceptive rather than explicit tactile
feedback, it illustrates diffusion as a reusable physical motion
prior that could be further constrained by tactile interaction
state.
Diffusion’s iterative sampling and extended action horizons
create a temporal mismatch with fast contact dynamics. Re-
active Diffusion Policy (RDP) (Xue et al., 2025) addresses
this through a slow-fast architecture: a 1-2 Hz latent diffusion
policy generates long-horizon action chunks from visual, tac-
tile, and proprioceptive observations using FiLM conditioning
(Perez et al., 2018), while a 20-30 Hz CNN-GRU continuously
refines them using high-frequency tactile/force feedback. PP-
Tac (Lin et al., 2025) similarly augments generated motions
with tactile slip correction, while TacDiffusion (Wu et al.,
2025b) uses a second-order filter to interface diffusion infer-
ence with a 1-kHz impedance loop. These architectures reveal
a recurring hierarchy in which generative models provide
slower coordinated behavior, while high-bandwidth tactile
feedback stabilizes contact-level execution.
Generation quality also depends on how contact state is repre-
sented. Compact wrench or deformation signals can condition
diffusion directly (Lin et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025b), whereas
high-dimensional touch benefits from separate representation
learning. Touch in the Wild (Zhu et al., 2026b) pretrains
visual and tactile encoders on more than 2.6 million syn-
chronized observations using masked tactile reconstruction
and bidirectional cross-attention between CNN tactile and
CLIP-ViT (Radford et al., 2021) visual features. The resulting
contact-aware representation, combined with proprioception,
conditions a U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015) diffusion policy
for fine-grained manipulation. Separating representation and
policy learning exposes the tactile encoder to substantially
greater contact diversity than task demonstrations alone, while
leaving diffusion to model how these states map to future
behaviors.
Overall, diffusion policies extend tactile-conditioned con-
trol beyond deterministic action regression by representing
multiple feasible behaviors over extended temporal horizons.
Existing methods span motion generation (Lin et al., 2025),
force-domain policies (Wu et al., 2025b), slow-fast reac-
tive architectures (Xue et al., 2025), reusable motion priors
(Yin et al., 2025b), and large-scale visuo-tactile pretraining
(Zhu et al., 2026b). Their central challenge is the trade-off
between temporal abstraction and contact reactivity, longer
horizons support coherent manipulation strategies, whereas
rapidly changing contacts require high-frequency correction,
motivating the hierarchical and hybrid architectures increas-
ingly seen in tactile diffusion policies.
Across these learning paradigms, an orthogonal design
choice is how tactile information is fused with vision and
proprioception. The simplest approach concatenates indepen-
dently encoded features before action prediction, enabling pre-
trained representations to be readily integrated into policies, as
in T-DEX (Guzey et al., 2023), exUMI (Xu et al., 2025a), and
deformable-object tracing (Zhao et al., 2026a). More coupled
architectures learn cross-modal interactions through fusion
tokens (Ye et al., 2026) or bidirectional attention (Zhu et al.,
2026b), while 3D-ViTac (Huang et al., 2024a) and VT-Refine
(Huang et al., 2025a) geometrically align vision and touch in
a common 3D frame to explicitly ground contact within the
scene. Fusion can also be temporal, Reactive Diffusion Policy
(Xue et al., 2025) separates slower multimodal action genera-
tion from high-frequency tactile correction. Thus, policy-level
fusion spans feature, attention-based, geometric, and temporal
integration, depending on whether touch complements visual
context, spatially grounds contact, or provides rapid feedback
during interaction.
IV. TACTILE FOUNDATION MODELS AND EMBODIED
INTELLIGENCE
Recent Vision-Language Models (VLMs) (Bordes et al.,
2024) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (Kawa-
harazuka et al., 2025) are shifting robotic manipulation from
task-specific policies toward general-purpose embodied mod-
els. Tactile foundation models extend this paradigm by jointly
representing vision, touch, language, and robot state, aiming
to transfer physical knowledge across tasks, objects, embodi-
ments, and sensing configurations.
Formally, let ot = {It, Tt, pt, l}, where It, Tt, pt, and l de-

multimodal model learns

Architecturally, these models generally combine modality-
specific tokenization, transformer-based multimodal fusion,
and downstream adaptation for control, reasoning, or world
modeling. Recent extensions incorporate force grounding, se-
mantic tactile pretraining, memory, predictive world models,
and explicit reasoning, progressively positioning touch as a
modality for embodied physical intelligence rather than solely
reactive feedback.
A central challenge is grounding tactile observations in
semantic spaces inherited from vision-language pretraining.
Octopi-1.5 (Yu et al., 2025), built on Qwen2-VL (Wang
et al., 2024a), projects GelSight observations through a fine-
tuned CLIP encoder (Radford et al., 2021) and learns tactile
attributes such as hardness and roughness through contrastive
objectives. Visual Prompt Tuning (Jia et al., 2022) adapts
the model for tactile description and ranking, while retrieval-
augmented generation (RAG) (Gao et al., 2023) retrieves


10
related tactile experiences as contextual memory. Touch thus
becomes both a semantic observation and a query into prior
physical experience.
OmniVTLA (Cheng et al., 2026) similarly grounds touch se-
mantically before action learning. Its dual-path tactile encoder
is pretrained on ObjTac and integrated into a π0-based policy
(Black et al., 2024), SigLIP (Zhai et al., 2023) transfers visual
semantic priors to tactile images, while a Semantic-Aligned
Vision Transformer contrastively aligns touch with vision and
language. The resulting embeddings encode reusable prop-
erties such as material, hardness, and roughness, illustrating
a broader foundation-model principle where tactile features
become more transferable when grounded in semantic or
physical concepts rather than optimized solely for a single
control objective.
The next step is to make these representations directly
condition action generation. TLA (Hao et al., 2026) extends
Qwen2-VL (Wang et al., 2024a) toward tactile-language-
action modeling by encoding sequential GelStereo observa-
tions (Zhang et al., 2023) with a pretrained ViT (Dosovitskiy
et al., 2020), fusing them with language tokens, and adapting
the language model through LoRA (Hu et al., 2024) to au-
toregressively generate discretized actions. This demonstrates
tactile control through a pretrained multimodal language back-
bone and transfer to unseen assembly geometries.
VTLA (Zhang et al., 2026a) further addresses temporal
touch and the mismatch between autoregressive token pre-
diction and continuous control. Vision-Guided Temporally
Enhanced Tokens encode tactile sequences, while visual-
token ordering mitigates autoregressive recency bias. After
supervised next-token training, Direct Preference Optimization
(DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023) ranks sampled actions by devia-
tion from demonstrations, providing an additional continuous-
control signal. Domain-randomized demonstrations generated
in Isaac Gym (Makoviychuk et al., 2021) further support zero-
shot sim-to-real insertion.
A complementary family extends pretrained continuous-
action VLAs such as π0 and π0.5 rather than discretizing
actions as language tokens. Tactile-VLA (Huang et al., 2025b),
built on π0 (Black et al., 2024) and Gemma (Team et al.,
2024), jointly encodes vision, language, proprioception, and
temporally aggregated touch before a tactile-aware action
expert generates continuous motion and desired contact force
through Conditional Flow Matching (Lipman et al., 2022).
This allows linguistic concepts such as gently or firmly to
directly modulate interaction through hybrid position-force
control. TacVLA (Zhang et al., 2026b) instead extends π0.5
(Intelligence et al., 2025) with contact-aware gating, activating
tactile tokens only after contact to avoid interfering with pre-
trained visual-language reasoning during free-space motion.
However, compatibility with a VLA token space does not
ensure that touch encodes the physical variables required
for manipulation. This is particularly important for vision-
based tactile sensors, whose image-like observations represent
deformation, pressure, shear, and contact geometry rather
than conventional visual appearance. The resulting question
is therefore not only how to align touch, but what latent space
it should be aligned with.
TaF-VLA (Huang et al., 2026a) addresses this through
physical grounding. Its TaF-Adapter is pretrained on over
10 million synchronized tactile images, force/torque measure-
ments, and pressure maps. Temporal tactile sequences are
encoded by a ViT and causal Transformer, while force and
pressure are discretized through VQ-VAE (Van Den Oord
et al., 2017); InfoNCE alignment (Oord et al., 2018) then pro-
duces history-aware, force-grounded tactile embeddings with-
out direct force regression. The frozen adapter subsequently
conditions π0.5 for flow-matching control across contact-rich
tasks and sensors. OmniVTLA (Cheng et al., 2026), by con-
trast, emphasizes semantic grounding by aligning touch with
vision and language. These approaches reveal complementary
requirements, semantic alignment supports abstraction and
transfer across objects and tasks, whereas physical alignment
preserves quantities such as force and pressure required for
precise interaction. General tactile foundation representations
will likely need both.
A further requirement is transfer across heterogeneous tac-
tile hardware. FTP-1 (Yuan et al., 2026) aggregates 26 datasets
spanning 21 tactile sensors to pretrain a generalist tactile
policy prior on π0.5. Sensor-specific observations are mapped
into a unified representation shared with vision, language,
and proprioception, followed by lightweight adaptation to
new sensors, tasks, and embodiments. Evaluation across five
robotic platforms, including unseen tactile sensors, indicates
transfer beyond individual sensing configurations. Conceptu-
ally, FTP-1 moves from tactile-enhanced foundation policies,
where touch augments a pretrained VLA for particular tasks,
toward foundation tactile policies, where heterogeneous tactile
experience itself contributes to the reusable prior.
Scaling such models therefore requires diversity as much
as volume. Internet video can provide broad priors for gen-
eralist robot learning, but lacks aligned actions and contact
information needed for tactile interaction (McCarthy et al.,
2025), while tactile datasets remain fragmented across sen-
sors, embodiments, sampling rates, and interaction protocols.
ObjTac-based semantic pretraining (Cheng et al., 2026), force-
grounded pretraining in TaF-VLA (Huang et al., 2026a), and
heterogeneous multi-dataset pretraining in FTP-1 (Yuan et al.,
2026) address complementary dimensions of this problem.

Reactive tactile control. The most direct strategy is to modify

avoidance with higher-priority tactile force minimization. To-

local contact regulation, implemented either around learned

a Gaussian Density Network (Bishop, 1994). A Bayesian

a feedforward-feedback controller performs surface following,
tracking, and pushing. Such methods combine learned tactile

Physically structured MPC explicitly encodes interaction

matrices and optimizes over these data, although its approxi-
mately linear assumptions constrain highly nonlinear contacts.
These approaches trade physical structure and interpretability
against assumptions that become increasingly restrictive for
complex manipulation.
Learning predictive interaction models. Learned dynamics
relax these modeling assumptions while retaining explicit
online optimization. Oller et al. (Oller et al., 2023) predict
future tactile depth maps, wrenches, and grasp poses from
current touch and actions, incorporating these predictions into
MPPI (Williams et al., 2015); PointNet (Qi et al., 2017) object
embeddings condition the model across geometries, although
known 3D models are required. For deformable manipula-
tion, (Zhang and Demiris, 2023) learns visuo-tactile latent
garment dynamics through a contrastive Conditional VAE and
evaluates candidate actions using complementary visual and
tactile costs, with vision measuring global task progress and
touch maintaining local edge contact. LeTac-MPC (Xu and
She, 2024) maps tactile images into a learned latent state and
embeds differentiable MPC within a 25 Hz grasp controller,
optimizing gripper width online under disturbances. These
methods illustrate a hybrid paradigm in which representations
and dynamics are learned while action selection remains
explicitly optimized.
Online adaptation of interaction dynamics. Even learned dy-
namics may fail when physical properties depart from the
training distribution. RoboPack (Ai et al., 2024) therefore
infers latent object physics from sequential visuo-tactile in-
teraction, a recurrent GNN predicts particle-level dynamics
while an LSTM integrates tactile history to infer stiffness
and deformability, enabling MPPI-based planning for occluded
packing and non-prehensile manipulation. Adaptive contact-
implicit MPC (Huang et al., 2024c) instead combines an
explicit Linear Complementarity System with a residual model
updated online from prediction errors, adapting contact dy-
namics to unknown geometry and friction while preserving
a physics prior. Here, touch identifies not only the current
state but also the latent physical properties governing future
interaction.
Overall, predictive and adaptive tactile control spans im-
mediate feedback correction (Caroleo et al., 2024; Chen
et al., 2025; He et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2025; Zhao et al.,
2025c, 2026b), probabilistic tactile servoing (Lloyd and Lep-
ora, 2024), physically structured and data-driven MPC (Oller
et al., 2024; Shirai et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024b), and
learned or adaptive predictive models (Ai et al., 2024; Huang
et al., 2024c; Oller et al., 2023; Xu and She, 2024; Zhang
and Demiris, 2023). Their key distinction is where learning
enters the feedback loop: perception, state estimation, dynam-
ics prediction, model adaptation, or direct action generation.
Compared with tactile-conditioned policies, these methods
additionally exploit discrepancies between predicted and ob-
served interaction during execution. As tactile world mod-
els become more expressive, this boundary will increasingly
blur: MPC uses interaction models for online task-specific
optimization, whereas foundation world models seek reusable
predictive knowledge across broader tasks and distributions.
Combining such general predictive models with MPC’s con-
straint handling, receding-horizon adaptation, and closed-loop
robustness represents a promising direction for tactile-guided
manipulation.
VI. GENERALIZATION OF TACTILE-CONDITIONED
POLICIES
Generalization is essential for tactile-guided manipulation,
yet is inherently multidimensional as a policy may transfer
across objects or materials while remaining specific to a
particular manipulation task, tactile sensor, or environment.
This challenge is especially pronounced for touch because
observations depend strongly on contact geometry, sensor
morphology, deformation, and interaction dynamics, such that
even small changes in the physical interface can substan-
tially shift the tactile distribution. Robust transfer therefore
requires representations and policies that preserve task-relevant
physical structure while suppressing sensor- and embodiment-
specific variation. Existing approaches pursue this through
transferable representations (Higuera et al., 2025), multimodal
learning (Tang et al., 2025), simulation (Yin et al., 2025b),
policy fine-tuning (Huang et al., 2025a), domain adaptation
(Jing and Qian, 2025), and cross-embodiment alignment (Zhu
et al., 2026a). However, evidence remains fragmented, with
most studies demonstrating transfer along only one or two di-
mensions. We therefore assess generalization separately across


14
objects, tasks, sensors, embodiments, and environments to
determine how far current tactile-conditioned policies extend
beyond their training conditions.
A. Object and Task Generalization
Tactile-conditioned policies must transfer when object prop-
erties or manipulation objectives differ from training. Touch
can support such transfer because local interaction mechanics
may remain informative despite changes in visual appearance.
However, an important distinction exists between object gen-
eralization, where the underlying skill remains similar, and the
more demanding task generalization, where physical interac-
tion knowledge must transfer across manipulation objectives.
a) Object generalization. Most studies evaluate geometric
transfer to unseen shapes, sizes, or contact configurations
while preserving the manipulation objective. TacDiffusion (Wu
et al., 2025b), visuo-tactile insertion (Azulay et al., 2025),
RoboPack (Ai et al., 2024), TLA (Hao et al., 2026), Tactile-
VLA (Huang et al., 2025b), and AnySkin (Bhirangi et al.,
2025) demonstrate varying transfer across novel geometries,
where touch provides recurring physical cues such as contact
alignment, force evolution, and slip. Transfer to unseen object
instances is similarly demonstrated by Online Multitask Learn-
ing (Ye et al., 2026), Touch in the Wild (Zhu et al., 2026b),
PP-Tac (Lin et al., 2025), and TactAlign (Wi et al., 2025),
using diverse interaction data, pretrained representations, or
cross-embodiment alignment to compensate for uncertainty in
object identity, configuration, or appearance.
More challenging transfer involves changes in kinematic
structure or functional affordance. Tac-Man (Zhao et al.,
2025c) and TacMan-Turbo (Zhao et al., 2026b) manipulate
unseen articulated objects without predefined kinematic mod-
els, while TEXterity (Bronars et al., 2024), Tactile Tool
Manipulation (Shirai et al., 2023), and Manipulation via
Membranes (Oller et al., 2023) accommodate variation in tools
and interactions through tactile feedback or predictive models.
Such results increasingly rely on shared interaction mechanics
rather than object similarity.
Nevertheless, current evaluations predominantly vary geom-
etry or object identity in isolation; coupled changes in friction,
compliance, mass distribution, surface properties, and con-
tact dynamics remain uncommon. Existing evidence therefore
demonstrates generalization mainly within structured object
families rather than unrestricted out-of-distribution physical
transfer.
b) Task generalization. Task generalization requires knowledge
learned from one set of manipulation objectives to support dif-
ferent contact sequences, action strategies, or success criteria.
This demands reusable physical representations or interaction
priors rather than task-specific tactile-action correlations.
One route is task-independent tactile pretraining. Transfer-
able Tactile Transformers (T3) (Zhao et al., 2024) and Binding
Touch to Everything (Yang et al., 2024) learn representations
from diverse interactions that transfer across downstream
tasks, while large-scale visuo-tactile pretraining in Touch in
the Wild (Zhu et al., 2026b) follows a similar principle.
These methods primarily demonstrate improved downstream
adaptation rather than zero-shot execution, suggesting that
tactile representations currently transfer across tasks more
readily than complete observation-to-action mappings.
Foundation policies offer a complementary route by inte-
grating touch with broad semantic and action priors. Tactile-
VLA (Huang et al., 2025b), TLA (Hao et al., 2026), and
tactile-force-aligned VLAs (Huang et al., 2026a) combine
pretrained vision-language-action knowledge with tactile ob-
servations that resolve task-specific physical states. Language
can specify new goals, while tactile or force-grounded repre-
sentations provide interaction information required to execute
them, moving toward reusable semantic and physical priors
rather than independent task-specific policies.
Nevertheless, task generalization must be distinguished from
transfer to unseen objects, instructions, configurations, or force
requirements. Most reported evaluations remain within related
manipulation families or recombine familiar contact primitives
such as grasping, insertion, and contact regulation; substan-
tially fewer demonstrate fundamentally new skills absent from
training. Evidence is therefore considerably stronger for object
generalization and cross-task representation transfer than for
unrestricted zero-shot task generalization.
B. Sensor and Embodiment Generalization
Tactile learning faces a modality-specific transfer problem
because observations are intrinsically coupled to sensing hard-
ware. Differences in transduction, resolution, compliance, and
placement can produce different measurements for the same
interaction (Gupta et al., 2025; Jing and Qian, 2025); embod-
iment changes additionally alter kinematics, morphology, and
action spaces.
a) Cross-sensor generalization. Cross-sensor transfer primarily
seeks representations that suppress sensor-specific variation
while preserving physical interaction state. Sparsh (Higuera
et al., 2024) and T3 (Zhao et al., 2024) pursue this through
self-supervised or heterogeneous multi-sensor pretraining,
whereas UniForce (Chen et al., 2026b) explicitly maps hetero-
geneous tactile observations into a force-grounded latent space
using image-force mappings constrained by force equilibrium
and reconstruction. This enables zero-shot transfer across
GelSight, TacTip, and uSkin and cross-sensor coordination
in downstream VTLA manipulation. ReactiveDiffusion (Xue
et al., 2025) evaluates control across tactile configurations,
while Touch in the Wild (Zhu et al., 2026b) exploits large-scale
portable data spanning diverse interactions. These approaches
suggest two main routes, learning invariance from sufficiently
diverse tactile data or grounding heterogeneous observations
in shared physical quantities such as force.
b) Cross-embodiment generalization. Human-to-robot transfer
is currently the most developed setting. TactAlign (Wi et al.,
2025) learns correspondence between human and robot tactile
latent spaces, whereas ViTaMIn (Liu et al., 2025) and exUMI
(Xu et al., 2025a) reduce the domain gap using portable
visuo-tactile interfaces shared between human data collection
and robot deployment. Visuo-tactile cortical alignment (Zhu
et al., 2026a) similarly seeks a common human-robot latent
space. Thus, embodiment mismatch can be addressed either

C. Sim-to-Real Generalization
Simulation enables scalable interaction, privileged supervi-
sion, and safe exploration for robot learning (Akinola et al.,
2025; Chen et al., 2026a; Li et al., 2026b; Makoviychuk et al.,
2021; Tao et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2026), but tactile sim-to-real
is particularly challenging because touch depends directly on
contact mechanics and sensor transduction. Simulators must
approximate geometry, friction, deformation, force transmis-
sion, and sensor response, where even small modeling errors
can alter both tactile observations and closed-loop behavior
(Sferrazza et al., 2019).
Tactile simulators therefore trade physical fidelity against
computational efficiency. Physics-based methods explicitly
model contact and sensor deformation, whereas graphical
methods approximate sensor outputs such as depth, marker
displacement, or tactile images and scale efficiently with
simulators such as Isaac Gym (Makoviychuk et al., 2021) and
MuJoCo (Todorov et al., 2012); reduced-order representations
based on forces, contact points, or pressure provide an in-
termediate abstraction. Importantly, transfer does not neces-
sarily require pixel-level realism if the policy representation
suppresses sensor-specific appearance. This motivates force-
grounded (Huang et al., 2026a), geometry-based (Ma et al.,
2026a), and deliberately abstract tactile representations (Field
et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2025a) that seek to preserve action-
relevant physical state across domains.
The sim-to-real gap can consequently arise at multiple
levels: a physics gap in contact dynamics, a sensor gap in
deformation and transduction, an observation gap between
synthetic and physical measurements, and a resulting pol-
icy gap when these discrepancies shift closed-loop behavior.
Transfer strategies target different levels of this hierarchy.
Domain randomization varies geometry, sensor parameters,
and initial conditions during training (Lin et al., 2025; Zhang
et al., 2026a); privileged teacher-student learning deploys
simplified binary or quantized tactile representations while
exploiting richer simulated state during training (Field et al.,
2025; Yin et al., 2025a); and (Church et al., 2022) instead
learns real-to-sim tactile translation, allowing policies trained
on synthetic observations to operate with physical sensors.
Simulation can also complement rather than replace real
data. VT-Refine (Huang et al., 2025a) initializes a diffusion


16
policy from real demonstrations and uses GPU-parallel tactile
simulation and reinforcement learning to discover corrective
contact behaviors underrepresented in those demonstrations.
Large-scale tactile pretraining (Higuera et al., 2024; Yuan
et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026) can further reduce sensitivity
to exact simulated observations by learning interaction reg-
ularities across broader distributions. Overall, scalable tactile
learning is unlikely to rely on a single high-fidelity simulator;
more promising is to combine simulation, physical data,
transferable representations, and adaptation according to which
aspects of contact can be modeled reliably.
VII. OPEN CHALLENGES AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS
The literature reviewed in Sections III–VII reveals a broader
transition, touch is evolving from local feedback into a
modality for representation, reasoning, and prediction in em-
bodied intelligence. Yet manipulation cannot be reduced to
sequence prediction because actions actively generate the
observations from which contact state is inferred, and their
consequences unfold through coupled interaction dynamics.
Tactile observations are therefore inherently action-dependent
and contact intrinsically dynamical (Johansson and Flanagan,
2009). General tactile intelligence must consequently represent
interaction state, predict its evolution, actively acquire missing
physical information, and regulate contact across multiple
timescales. This motivates a transition from tactile-augmented
policies toward tactile-native embodied intelligence, with four
directions particularly important.
A. From Tactile Features to Interaction Representations
Future tactile representations must abstract away sensor-
specific form factors while retaining the physical information
required for manipulation. Existing approaches span self-
supervised features, semantic alignment, geometric grounding,
and force-aligned latent spaces (Higuera et al., 2024; Huang
et al., 2026a; Ma et al., 2026a; Yang et al., 2024), while cross-
sensor models such as TactX and generalist policies such as
FTP-1 indicate that interaction knowledge can transfer beyond
individual sensing configurations (Park et al., 2026; Yuan et al.,
2026). However, no representation yet provides a common
interface that is simultaneously transferable, predictive, seman-
tically meaningful, and sufficiently precise for contact control.
A promising target is an interaction-centric latent state
organized around the physics of contact rather than its sensor-
specific rendering. Such a representation should preserve con-
tinuous variables-geometry, loading, deformation, slip, and
compliance-while supporting semantic abstractions such as
stable contact, slippage, or jamming. Bridging these levels,
potentially through physical inductive biases such as fric-
tion, deformability, and history dependence, could provide a
common representation for control, prediction, memory, and
reasoning.
B. From Tactile-Augmented Models to Hierarchical Physical
Intelligence
Foundation and generative policies expose a fundamental
temporal mismatch between long-horizon reasoning and rapid
contact regulation. Reactive Diffusion Policy, TacDiffusion,
and TouchWorld address this through hierarchical architectures
that separate slower planning or generation from faster tactile
correction (Wu et al., 2025b; Xue et al., 2025; Zhou et al.,
2026). The broader principle is that foundation models should
complement rather than replace feedback control: high-level
models specify intent and subgoals, intermediate models pre-
dict interactions and generate motion or force references, and
low-level loops stabilize contact and reject disturbances. A
central open problem is designing the bidirectional interface-
what physical information should flow downward to control,
and which tactile events should trigger upward replanning.
Such hierarchies could also translate abstract task goals into
explicit interaction specifications. Language describes what to
achieve but rarely specifies acceptable forces, contact modes,
deformation, or termination conditions. Tactile-VLA begins to
connect linguistic concepts with desired force (Huang et al.,
2025b); more generally, foundation models could generate
pose tolerances, contact modes, force/deformation limits, and
terminal conditions that feedback controllers or MPC can
enforce (Lloyd and Lepora, 2024; Xu and She, 2024). This
would combine semantic flexibility with precise physical con-
trol without requiring either level to subsume the other.
C. From Reactive Feedback to Predictive and Active Touch
The next major transition is from reacting to contact toward
reasoning about its consequences before acting. Emerging
tactile world models predict future visuo-tactile or latent states
(Huang et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2026d; Zhou et al., 2026),
but their value ultimately lies in decision making: predicting
whether pressing, sliding, or regrasping will maintain stability,
induce slip or jamming, exceed force limits, or deform an
object. Evaluation should therefore extend beyond forecasting
accuracy to whether predictions improve action selection,
failure avoidance, and recovery.
Predictive models must also update when reality contradicts
their forecasts. RoboPack demonstrates inference of latent
physical properties from tactile history, while ReTouch adapts
predictions online (Ai et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026c).
Future world models should maintain calibrated uncertainty
and revise beliefs as new contact evidence arrives, particularly
when prediction reliability affects safety.
Beyond prediction, tactile perception should become active.
Because touch is action-generated, robots can deliberately
probe compliance, friction, grasp stability, or other uncertain
properties before committing to task actions. Such epistemic
actions trade immediate task progress for information gain.
Integrating active touch with predictive world models would
therefore shift manipulation from passive contact estimation
toward purposeful physical experimentation.
D. From Dataset Scaling to Lifelong Tactile Intelligence
Scaling touch differs fundamentally from scaling vision
because tactile observations vary not only with objects but also
with sensor mechanics, calibration, contact pose and velocity,
robot dynamics, and interaction strategy. Consequently, sheer
sample count is less important than interaction diversity.

support this direction (Park et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026;
Yuan et al., 2026), while HapTile and FreeTacMan provide
complementary mechanisms for scaling haptic-informed and
robot-free demonstrations (Alian et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026).

interfaces are non-stationary, sensors drift or are replaced,
surfaces wear, materials age, and contamination changes fric-

and consolidate experience without catastrophic forgetting.

ual tactile policies. A tactile-native embodied agent should
identify what it does not know, act to acquire that infor-
mation, predict the consequences of alternative interactions,
and regulate contact robustly across timescales, sensors, and
embodiments. In this view, touch is not auxiliary to vision
but a computational interface through which embodied models
reason about and act upon the causal dynamics of the physical
world.
VIII. CONCLUSION
This paper reviewed the transition of tactile-guided robotic
manipulation from perception and reactive feedback toward
learning-based physical interaction. We examined tactile rep-
resentation and policy learning, foundation models, predictive
and adaptive control, and generalization, highlighting how
touch increasingly contributes not only to contact estimation
but also to action generation, physical reasoning, and interac-
tion prediction.
Despite rapid progress, scalable tactile intelligence remains
constrained by heterogeneous sensing hardware, limited in-
teraction data, and transfer across tasks, sensors, and em-
bodiments. Future progress will require transferable physical
representations, scalable multimodal pretraining, predictive
tactile world models, and architectures that connect high-
level reasoning with fast contact-level control. Together, these
advances could establish touch as a core modality for general-
purpose embodied intelligence, enabling robots to reason
about, predict, and adapt their physical interactions in the real
world.
