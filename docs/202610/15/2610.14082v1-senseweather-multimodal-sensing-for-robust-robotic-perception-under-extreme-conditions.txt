# SenseWeather: Multimodal Sensing for Robust Robotic Perception under Extreme Conditions 

Fucai Ke 1, Sukai Huang 1, Junyi Hao 1, Kal Backman 1, Naim Rastgoo 1, Gholamreza Haffari 1, Hamid Rezatofighi 1

> 1

Faculty of Information Technology, Monash University, Clayton, VIC 3800, Australia 

Abstract — Robotic perception must remain reliable when extreme environmental conditions severely degrade the primary RGB observation. Although robotic platforms commonly carry complementary sensors such as depth and infrared, much of the modern perception stack remains RGB-centric, creating a mismatch between multimodal onboard sensing and pre-trained downstream models. We study whether complementary sensing can be used upstream to recover a task-compatible RGB observation that preserves existing perception capabilities without modifying or retraining the downstream stack. To this end, we introduce SenseWeather , a controlled multimodal benchmark for robotic perception under severe rain, snow, dust, fog, and darkness, with registered RGB, depth, and infrared observations, clean references, and grounded high-level perception tasks. We further develop Fusion-to-RGB ,with two parameter-efficient sensor-fusion realizations based on token-space fusion for a diffusion transformer and residual latent conditioning for an inpainting UNet. Across frozen open-vocabulary grounding and vision-language models, RGB+D+IR fusion improves all paired downstream comparisons over the corresponding RGB-only restorers, including AP 50 gains across all ten detector comparisons. Importantly, these perception gains do not consistently follow conventional image-fidelity met-rics, and increasing weather severity does not induce monotonic downstream degradation. These results show that complemen-tary sensing can preserve reusable robotic perception under extreme conditions, while highlighting task-level perception robustness as an objective distinct from visual restoration quality. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Modern robotic systems increasingly depend on RGB-centric perception as the front end of their perception–action pipeline. Advances in object detection, vision-language mod-els, and embodied reasoning have enabled powerful pre-trained models to support scene understanding, target local-ization, decision making, and interaction across a wide range of robotic platforms [1]–[8]. Reliable operation, however, requires these perception capabilities to remain functional when robots encounter extreme environmental conditions. Severe rain, fog, dust, snow, and poor illumination can corrupt the RGB stream, obscure scene structure, and remove task-relevant visual evidence [9]–[11]. Such degradation is therefore not merely an image-quality problem: it can prop-agate through the robotic perception stack, causing failures in localization, scene understanding, and subsequent decision making. Robotic platforms, however, typically operate with sensing redundancy rather than RGB alone. Depth cameras, infrared sensors, and other onboard modalities provide complemen-tary geometric, spectral, and structural cues that can remain reliable when visual appearance is degraded [12]–[15]. Depth can preserve scene geometry and object boundaries under poor visibility, while infrared can retain discriminative cues under challenging illumination. In principle, such redun-dancy should allow a robot to maintain perception when its RGB stream becomes unreliable. In practice, however, much of the modern perception stack is built around pretrained RGB models that cannot directly consume these auxiliary observations. This creates a deployment-level interface mis-match between the multimodal sensors available on the robot and the RGB-centric models used for perception, reasoning, and action [16]–[18]. Existing approaches offer two incomplete solutions to this deployment problem. RGB restoration preserves the standard visual interface, but typically relies on the corrupted RGB ob-servation alone [19]. When adverse conditions remove task-relevant evidence, such methods must reconstruct it without access to the complementary measurements already available on the robot [20]. Task-specific multimodal perception takes the opposite route: it directly fuses RGB with depth, infrared, or other sensors [14], [15], but usually requires dedicated architectures, supervision, or retraining for each downstream capability [21], [22]. This limits reuse of the large ecosystem of pretrained RGB perception and reasoning models already available for robotic systems. We therefore ask whether multimodal sensing can instead be used upstream to recover a standard RGB observation that preserves complementary sensor evidence while remaining directly consumable by existing RGB models without downstream modification or retraining. Realizing this goal presents several challenges. First, learning sensor-conditioned restoration requires registered degraded RGB, auxiliary sensor observations, and clear RGB references from the same robotic viewpoint and scene state, which are difficult to acquire systematically across controlled extreme conditions. Second, onboard sensors pro-vide heterogeneous evidence, with depth capturing geomet-ric structure and infrared providing complementary spectral cues, which must be effectively integrated into a common RGB observation. Third, preserving visual appearance alone is insufficient: the recovered observation must retain the task-relevant evidence required by the robot’s downstream perception stack. Improvements in conventional restoration metrics therefore need not translate into better localization, recognition, counting, or visual reasoning [21], [23]. To study robotic perception under extreme environmen-tal conditions, we construct SenseWeather , a multimodal benchmark spanning five controlled yet realistic simulated robotic environments. It provides registered adverse RGB, clear RGB, depth, and infrared observations under severe rain, snow, dust, fog, and darkness, including unseen environ-ments and held-out viewpoints. SenseWeather further eval-uates high-level perception capabilities commonly required by robotic systems through open-vocabulary grounding and vision-language reasoning, covering localization, counting, presence recognition, and localized object identification. The benchmark therefore measures whether task-relevant scene understanding can be preserved when the robot’s primary RGB observation is severely compromised. Building on SenseWeather, we introduce Fusion-to-RGB ,which uses complementary onboard sensing to recover a standard RGB observation for existing robotic perception models. We develop two architecturally distinct realizations: token-space sensor fusion for a diffusion transformer and residual latent conditioning for an inpainting UNet. Both preserve the pretrained generative prior and require only parameter-efficient adaptation, while producing RGB obser-vations that can be directly consumed by unchanged down-stream models. This allows robotic systems to exploit multi-modal sensing under extreme conditions without constructing a task-specific fusion model for each perception capabil-ity. Across open-vocabulary grounding and vision-language reasoning, Fusion-to-RGB improves all paired downstream comparisons over the corresponding RGB-only restorers, including cases where conventional image-fidelity metrics do not improve. a) Adverse-Weather Image Restoration.: Image restora-tion has been extensively studied for adverse conditions such as rain [9], [24] and haze [10], [25], with recent all-in-one methods targeting multiple degradations within a unified framework [19]. Generative priors have further improved realistic restoration through diffusion-based re-construction [20], [26], [27], task-oriented objectives [21], and pretrained image generation or completion models [23], [28], [29]. These methods, however, remain predominantly RGB-centric and must infer information lost under severe degradation from the corrupted image itself. We instead use complementary sensing to recover the RGB observation. 

b) Multimodal Sensor Fusion.: Robotic platforms com-monly combine RGB cameras with depth, infrared, or Li-DAR sensors that provide complementary geometric and spectral cues [5]–[7], [12], [30]. Such information has been exploited through task-specific multimodal fusion for 3D detection and robust perception [13], [16], [18], [31], as well as through multimodal image fusion [15], [22], [32]. Most existing approaches produce task-specific predictions or fused representations. In contrast, we fuse sensing up-stream to restore a standard RGB image, allowing heteroge-neous pretrained RGB models to operate unchanged. 

c) Restoration for Machine Perception.: Restoration is traditionally evaluated using full-reference fidelity metrics such as PSNR, SSIM [33], and LPIPS [34]. These metrics measure similarity to a clean reference but do not nec-essarily reflect the usefulness of restored images to ma-chine perception. Recent work has begun incorporating task-oriented restoration objectives [21], while adverse-weather benchmarks expose persistent degradation in modern vision and vision-language models [11]. Our evaluation instead keeps heterogeneous downstream models frozen and mea-sures whether one restored RGB image transfers across de-tection and vision–language tasks. We therefore treat visual fidelity and machine readability as complementary, rather than interchangeable, objectives. III. S ENSE WEATHER BENCHMARK

B. Extreme-Condition Environments and Multimodal Cap-ture 

Collecting registered multimodal observations with paired clear references and controlled adverse conditions is difficult to achieve systematically in the real world. We therefore construct SenseWeather in AirSim [35] across five opera-tionally distinct robotic environments: an island base, three offshore docks, and an oil refinery. These environments cover dense container terminals, industrial facilities, coastal terrain, vehicles, material-handling equipment, and maritime assets, supporting both UAV and ground-robot perception scenarios. Each environment is equipped with a curated semantic catalog that groups task-level objects into coarse and fine-grained categories. Across all five environments, the catalogs contain 22,495 object instances spanning 175 coarse and 247 fine-grained categories (Table I). Observations are captured from UAV- and robot-centric viewpoints around annotated scene objects. Each accepted viewpoint provides registered 512 ×512 clear RGB, degraded RGB, depth, infrared, and instance annotations. We consider severe rain, snow, dust, and fog together with a separate darkness condition. Visibility and framing checks remove views with poorly observed or clipped targets, while paired capture preserves the same camera pose and scene state across sensing conditions. This produces spatially aligned multimodal observations suitable for both restoration and downstream robotic perception evaluation. Detailed sampling and capture procedures are provided in the supplementary material. We use approximately 10k paired observations for restora-tion training and construct a manually curated Primary Extreme-Condition Test Set of 625 observations for down-stream evaluation. It contains 500 observations under severe rain, snow, dust, and fog at intensities 0.8, 0.9, and 1.0,together with 125 registered darkness observations from non-reused viewpoints. The test set covers four environments un-seen during training and held-out viewpoints from Offshore Dock 1, providing both cross-environment and cross-view evaluation. In total, it supports 625 localization queries with 825 target boxes and 939 vision-language questions. We additionally construct a Controlled Intensity Study Set 

with 400 manually reviewed observations from 10 fixed viewpoints. For each viewpoint, scene state and sensing configuration are held fixed while rain, snow, dust, and fog intensity varies from 0.1 to 1.0. This controlled split isolates the effect of weather severity on robotic perception from changes in scene composition; darkness is evaluated separately as a fixed extreme illumination condition. 

C. Grounded Robotic Perception Tasks and Evaluation 

SenseWeather evaluates whether observations under ex-treme conditions retain the spatial and semantic evidence required for high-level robotic scene understanding. We con-sider open-vocabulary localization together with three vision-language capabilities: category counting, object presence reasoning, and localized object identification. All tasks are instantiated directly from the recorded simulator state and subsequently audited, ensuring that target labels, questions, answers, and spatial groundings remain consistent with the observed scene. Localization prompts cover diverse object categories, while the vision–language tasks probe comple-mentary aspects of scene understanding beyond object de-tection alone. Our primary evaluation measures task-level perception us-ing frozen pretrained models. Our experiments address three questions. First, can com-plementary sensing preserve high-level robotic perception under extreme conditions while keeping the downstream per-ception stack unchanged? Second, how do depth and infrared contribute across restoration backbones, perception tasks, and environmental conditions? Third, do conventional image-fidelity metrics and commanded weather severity provide reliable proxies for downstream perception robustness? We answer these questions through controlled image substitution with frozen downstream models. 

A. Experimental Setup a) Restoration models.: We compare four RGB-only restoration baselines with six sensor-conditioned variants of Fusion-to-RGB. The RGB-only baselines include MAE [36], Pix2Pix-Turbo [23], Qwen-Image-Edit [29], and Real-Fill [28]. For Qwen and RealFill, we further evaluate RGB+D, RGB+IR, and RGB+D+IR variants to isolate the contribution of complementary sensing. All learned models are trained on the same approximately 10k paired obser-vations from Offshore Dock 1, with degraded RGB as input, optional registered depth and infrared as auxiliary observations, and clear RGB as supervision. The training data are disjoint from all evaluation sets, and model selection and inference settings are fixed before evaluation. 

b) Frozen downstream perception.: We evaluate re-stored observations with five open-vocabulary grounding models, Grounding DINO, Florence-2, Falcon-Perception-300M, OmDet-Turbo, and YOLO-World [37]–[41], together with Qwen3-VL-2B and Qwen3-VL-4B [42]. All down-stream models remain frozen. For each paired compari-son, the target annotation, prompt, scoring protocol, model weights, and inference settings are held fixed; only the RGB observation supplied to the model is changed. This controlled image-substitution protocol directly tests whether complementary sensing recovers task-relevant evidence that can be reused by an unchanged robotic perception stack. 

c) Evaluation splits.: Unless otherwise stated, down-stream perception is evaluated on the primary extreme-condition test set described in Sec. III. Full-reference metrics are reported separately on observations with paired clear RGB and are used only to analyze visual fidelity rather than as a proxy for perception performance. The Controlled Intensity Study Set is reserved for the severity analysis in Sec. V-F.

C. How Do Complementary Sensors Contribute? 

The benefit of auxiliary sensing is not attributable to a single dominant modality. In open-vocabulary localization, the relative value of depth and infrared depends strongly on the generative backbone. For Qwen, RGB+IR outperforms RGB+D across all five detectors, whereas RealFill benefits more from depth in four of the five comparisons (Table III). Despite this backbone dependence, combining both modali-ties is consistently strongest: RGB+D+IR achieves the best localization result for every backbone–detector pair. A similar pattern appears in vision–language reasoning (Table V). Neither depth nor infrared alone dominates across model scales and capabilities, while RGB+D+IR achieves the best result in ten of the twelve comparisons and improves over the RGB-only parent in all twelve. These results suggest that depth and infrared provide complementary evidence whose usefulness varies with the restoration architecture and downstream task. Joint conditioning therefore offers a more robust strategy than relying on either auxiliary modality alone.

For each con-dition, we average four RGB+D+IR-minus-RGB gains: Grounding DINO AP 50 and the mean VQA gain for each of the Qwen and RealFill backbones. Fusion yields the largest improvements under fog ( +7 .11 pp), dust ( +6 .43 pp), and darkness ( +4 .33 pp), while gains are modest under rain (+0 .85 pp) and negligible under snow ( −0.10 pp). We use this average only to summarize the condition-level trend. The smaller gains under rain and snow are consistent with their weaker effective RGB degradation in our simulator: the controlled intensity sweep produces substantially smaller weather-to-clear MAE changes for these conditions than for fog and dust. We therefore attribute the limited gains primarily to the current degradation regime rather than to an inherent limitation of multimodal sensing. Darkness is excluded from this comparison because it has no controlled intensity sweep. 

F. Weather Severity Is Not a Proxy for Perception Difficulty 

Commanded weather intensity is a reliable control variable for visual corruption, but not for robotic perception difficulty. Within each weather type, increasing intensity produces an almost monotonic increase in RGB degradation (Fig. 4, left). Yet frozen detection performance shows only weak and often non-monotonic dependence on the same intervention (Fig. 4). This decoupling shows that physical corruption and robotic perception difficulty are distinct. The latter depends jointly on degradation type, scene content, restoration be-havior, and downstream model response. Weather intensity should therefore be treated as a controlled intervention, not a universal difficulty scale, motivating direct task-level eval-uation of perception robustness under extreme conditions. VI. C ONCLUSION 

This work studies how complementary onboard sensing can preserve robotic perception when extreme environmental conditions severely degrade the primary RGB observation. We introduce SenseWeather, a controlled multimodal bench-mark spanning diverse operational environments, extreme weather and illumination conditions, and grounded high-level perception tasks. We further develop Fusion-to-RGB through two parameter-efficient sensor-fusion designs that recover a conventional RGB observation for unchanged pretrained perception models. Across open-vocabulary grounding and vision-language reasoning, multimodal sensing consistently improves downstream perception over RGB-only restoration. Our analysis further shows that neither conventional image fidelity nor nominal weather severity reliably predicts per-ception robustness, highlighting the importance of evaluating extreme-condition robotic perception directly at the task level.
