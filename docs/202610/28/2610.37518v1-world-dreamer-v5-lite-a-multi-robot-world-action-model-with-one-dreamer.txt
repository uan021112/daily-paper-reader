Abstract
Multi-robot control requires integrating distributed camera
views while predicting separate action sequences.
Most
vision–language–action and world-action models target
one robot. Combining multiple views alone does not estab-
lish spatial correspondence between them. World Dreamer
v5-lite is a multi-robot world-action model built around a
shared world state ϕ for robot-specific action prediction.
Calibrated features from a pretrained video encoder pop-
ulate ϕ in workspace coordinates; its spatial size remains
fixed as views are added. A vision–language model pro-
vides scene context and one query per robot. The queries
read ϕ and exchange pose-aware information, while their
action streams remain separate. Masked-view cooperative
training withholds selected camera views from both visual
branches while retaining all action targets. This encourages
the use of observations shared by other robots. Pretraining
uses more than 10,000 hours of trajectories from 20 simu-
lated robot types, RoboFactory, and real robots. On Robo-
Factory, World Dreamer v5-lite achieves 74.0% mean suc-
cess, 23.7 percentage points above the strongest evaluated
baseline. Across four real-robot tasks, it reaches 78.8%
mean success, a margin of 13.8 points.
It also reaches
58.08% overall on RoboCasa365.
1. Introduction
Vision–language–action (VLA) models adapt pretrained
multimodal backbones to robot control [2, 3, 11, 16, 31],
and world-action models further couple action prediction
with predicted visual dynamics [7, 12, 30]. These models
inherit semantic knowledge from vision–language pretrain-
ing and, in the case of world-action models, knowledge of
how scenes evolve from video pretraining. Most of these
models predict actions for a single robot.
Many manipulation tasks instead require several robots

to work in a shared workspace, for example, when one robot
holds an object while another operates on it [18]. This set-
ting differs from single-robot control in three respects. First,
observations are distributed: each robot typically carries its
own cameras, which move with it and capture only part of
the workspace, and image patches from different cameras
do not correspond to the same physical locations. Second,
control is separate while decisions are coupled: the robots
may differ in embodiment and action space, yet the action of
each robot depends on where the other robots are and what
they are doing. Third, observations are often incomplete:
the camera of one robot may be occluded by another robot
or by the manipulated object, so a robot must sometimes act
on what other cameras observe.
Extending single-robot models to this setting is not
straightforward.
Concatenating camera tokens does not,
by itself,
establish correspondence between views in
workspace coordinates. Separate policies preserve robot-
specific controls, but a policy restricted to its own cameras
cannot use observations from teammates. Existing multi-
agent policies can share information across robots [17, 23].
Even with this capacity, training with complete observations
gives a robot little incentive to use another view when its
own cameras already suffice.
World Dreamer v5-lite addresses these requirements
with a shared world state ϕ built from calibrated video fea-
tures in workspace coordinates. A vision–language model
conditions one query per robot on the current images and
instruction; the queries read ϕ and exchange pose-aware in-
formation while their action streams remain separate. Only
observed video features enter the state used for action pre-
diction, while an auxiliary future-video latent objective
trains the video encoder. During training, selected cam-
era views are withheld from both visual branches, but every
robot retains its action target, exposing the model to incom-
plete visual coverage.

2. Related Work
Vision–language–action
models.
Vision–language–
action (VLA) policies map language and images to robot
actions with pretrained multimodal backbones.
RT-2
co-fine-tunes
web-pretrained
vision–language
models
(VLMs) on robot trajectories and represents actions as text
tokens [31]. OpenVLA builds on the Prismatic VLM and
predicts discretized action tokens after training on about
970k demonstrations from Open X-Embodiment [11], and
Octo is a transformer policy pretrained on 800k Open X-
Embodiment trajectories and instructed by language or
goal images [9]. π0 attaches a flow-matching action expert
to a PaliGemma backbone [3], and π0.5 extends its training
to heterogeneous data sources for open-world mobile ma-
nipulation [2]. GR00T N1 pairs a vision–language module
with a diffusion transformer that generates continuous
actions [16].
These policies process the observations of
one robot as a sequence of image tokens. World Dreamer
v5-lite retains the VLM as a source of semantic context and
agent queries, but additionally aggregates the views of all
robots in a calibrated shared world state.
World-action models.
World-action models couple ac-
tion prediction with predicted visual dynamics. UniPi gen-


erates a future video from an image and a language goal
and extracts actions with an inverse-dynamics model [7].
UWM integrates action diffusion and video diffusion in one
transformer with an independent diffusion timestep for each
modality [30].
Several recent models use video predic-
tion mainly as a training signal: UVA decodes video and
actions from a joint latent with separate diffusion heads
and bypasses video generation at inference [12], and Fast-
WAM [25] and GigaWorld-Policy [24] likewise retain video
co-training while skipping future-frame generation when
acting. World Dreamer v5-lite also uses future-frame pre-
diction only during training, but it lifts the observed features
of the video encoder into a shared world state in workspace
coordinates instead of using them as image-indexed tokens
of a single robot.
Multi-robot policies.
RoboFactory provides composi-
tional multi-agent manipulation tasks for studying embod-
ied collaboration [18]. On this benchmark, GauDP recon-
structs a 3D Gaussian field from the RGB observations of
all agents and redistributes it to each agent within a dif-
fusion policy [23]. CLS-DP instead targets decentralized
execution without communication: privileged multi-agent
dynamics are distilled into a latent space during training,
and each agent infers this latent from its own camera at de-
ployment [17]. World Dreamer v5-lite differs from these
methods in three respects: it builds on pretrained vision–
language and video models, it lets robots exchange infor-
mation explicitly through pose-aware agent queries, and its
masked-view training encourages robots to act on shared
observations when their own views are withheld.
3D visuomotor policies.
Explicit 3D representations pro-
vide spatial structure for visuomotor policies. DP3 con-
ditions a diffusion policy on a compact representation ex-
tracted from sparse point clouds [26]. PDFactor factorizes
a point cloud into three orthogonal feature planes [21], a
plane factorization also used for 3D-aware generation in
EG3D [4]. The shared world state ϕ adopts a similar plane
layout but is built from the features of a pretrained video
encoder across multiple moving cameras, handles patches
without depth through candidate points along their view-
ing rays, and is accumulated over observed frames with a
visibility-gated update.
3. Method
In this section, we present World Dreamer v5-lite, a frame-
work for language-conditioned control of multiple robots
that share a workspace.
At each decision step, given a
language instruction, the current and recent RGB obser-
vations of calibrated cameras, and the proprioception of
each robot, World Dreamer v5-lite predicts an action chunk
for every robot. As illustrated in Fig. 1, World Dreamer
v5-lite draws shared context from two sources. A vision–
language model (VLM) processes the instruction and the
current images together with one learnable query per robot
(Sec. 3.1). In parallel, a video encoder processes the recent
observations of all cameras (Sec. 3.2), and its features are
placed in workspace coordinates to build the shared world
state ϕ (Sec. 3.3). The Action Decoder combines these sig-
nals with the proprioception of each robot and the relative
poses between robots to predict the controls of every robot
(Sec. 3.4). Finally, Sec. 3.5 introduces masked-view coop-
erative training, which encourages the robots to act on each
other’s observations.
3.1. Vision–language conditioning
The instruction and the current images of the available
views form a shared prefix of language and image tokens
for the VLM (Fig. 1). A fixed layout reserves N robot slots,
and an agent mask magent ∈{0, 1}N selects the robots
present at the decision step. One learnable agent query per
robot slot follows the prefix. The input embedding of the
query for slot i ∈{1, . . . , N} is
ri = qshared + ei,
(1)
where qshared is a learnable embedding common to all
robots and ei is a learnable embedding of slot i. Because
all queries follow the same prefix, one VLM pass condi-
tions the robot queries on the same scene without rerunning
the backbone separately for each robot.
The VLM provides two outputs to the Action Decoder.
First, the VLM processes the input query embedding ri with
the shared scene prefix and produces a contextualized hid-
den state at robot query position i. This hidden state is
projected and normalized to form the initial decoder query
q0

the layer-wise keys and values of the prefix tokens form
a shared scene-prefix cache, which supplies the instruction
and image context to every robot without another VLM pass
(Sec. 3.4). Outputs of unused slots are zero-padded.
3.2. Video encoder
While the VLM provides semantic context from the cur-
rent images, the video encoder supplies the spatial evidence
from which ϕ is built. Recent RGB histories occupy V cam-
era slots, which are separate from the N robot slots: a robot
may provide several views, and a workspace camera may
provide another. A view mask mview ∈{0, 1}V selects the
available cameras; the same mask determines which current
images enter the VLM.
A causal video variational autoencoder (VAE) converts
each RGB history into latents, and the encoder of a pre-
trained video model processes To=3 observed latent steps.

3.3. Shared world state ϕ
The video encoder produces a feature for each observed
image patch. Patch indices from different cameras do not
identify the same physical location, so features from differ-
ent views cannot be pooled directly, and concatenating them
would yield a token set that grows with the number of cam-
eras. The shared world state ϕ instead organizes features
by their locations in a common workspace. As shown in
Fig. 2, camera geometry places each token in 3D, the token
is fused with its geometric embedding, and the result is writ-
ten to three axis-aligned planes (XY, XZ, and YZ) [4, 21],

state has a fixed size regardless of the number of cameras
and is shared by all robots.

3.4. Action Decoder
The Action Decoder must generate controls for robots that
may differ in embodiment while accounting for how they in-
teract. Concatenating the action tokens of all robots into one
sequence entangles heterogeneous action spaces and length-
ens the sequence with every added robot, whereas decod-
ing each robot independently ignores the others. We there-
fore keep a separate action stream for each robot and let the
robots communicate only through their queries qi, initial-
ized from the VLM as q0
i (Sec. 3.1).
For robot i, the current proprioception is si ∈RDs and
the target action chunk is ai ∈RH×Da, where H=30 is
the number of future control steps. Proprioception and ac-
tions are padded to common widths Ds=20 and Da=18,
which lets one decoder handle embodiments with different
numbers of control coordinates. The action mask mact ∈
{0, 1}N×H×Da identifies the valid control coordinates and
recorded future steps of each robot.

First, the Field Injection Block lets each query qi read
ϕ through cross-attention, retrieving the spatial context
pooled from all cameras. Because all robots read the same
state, the attention is biased toward the location of the cam-
era assigned to robot i, so that each robot reads ϕ from its
own perspective without excluding distant regions.
Second, the Scene Decoder Block conditions the action
stream of robot i on the scene-prefix cache. Each token at-
tends to the cache of the matching VLM layer and, causally,
to the preceding tokens of its own stream. An action token
can therefore use the language–image context, the propri-
oception of its robot, the field-updated query, and the ear-
lier action tokens, while remaining isolated from the action
streams of the other robots.

3.5. Masked-view cooperative training
The architecture above allows the robots to share informa-
tion, but it does not by itself ensure that this information is
used. If every robot always observes its own cameras, the
decoder can rely mainly on those views and ignore both ϕ
and the other robots. To encourage cooperation under in-
complete visual coverage, training uses mview to withhold
either all cameras of one randomly selected robot or a ran-
dom subset of views. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the same cam-
eras are masked in both visual paths, so a masked camera
contributes neither its current image to the VLM scene pre-
fix nor its history to ϕ. Camera calibration remains available
to define spatial references and the relative-pose descriptors
Pij.
Since view masking is applied only to observations, the
recorded action chunks remain valid targets for all robots,
including those whose cameras are hidden. The remaining
views can contribute through ϕ and the scene-prefix cache,
while query interaction provides another route for sharing
context.

4. Experiments
The evaluation covers multi-robot collaboration on Robo-
Factory [18], single-robot household manipulation on
RoboCasa365 [15], and real-robot manipulation with ho-
mogeneous and heterogeneous robot teams.
4.1. Datasets and protocols
Pretraining corpus.
The pretraining corpus combines
manipulation trajectories generated in simulation for 20
robot types, RoboFactory trajectories, and real-robot trajec-
tories collected with LeKiwi and OpenArm. These sources
cover single-arm and dual-arm configurations, more than 60
task types, and more than 10,000 hours of interaction. Dif-
ferences in action dimensionality are handled by the action
mask, and missing cameras are handled by the view mask.
The pretrained model is fine-tuned separately for each eval-
uation setting, and evaluation episodes are not included in
the training data.
RoboFactory.
RoboFactory
provides
compositional
multi-agent manipulation tasks built on ManiSkill [18].
Evaluation uses three tasks with increasing numbers of
agents: Place Food (two agents), Camera Alignment (three
agents), and Take Photo (four agents).
These tasks are
also evaluated in prior work on this benchmark [17, 23],
and they test how performance changes as the number of
collaborating robots grows. The original tasks are retained,


but the camera configuration is modified so that each agent
receives observations from its own cameras. All methods
in Tab. 1 are trained and evaluated under this configuration.
Following the RoboFactory protocol, each task is evaluated
over 100 scene configurations.
RoboCasa365.
RoboCasa365 provides 365 household
tasks across 2,500 kitchen scenes, 220 of which require
mobile manipulation [15].
Following the official leader-
board [19], World Dreamer v5-lite is fine-tuned on the of-
ficial Human300 pretraining dataset, which contains 300
tasks across 2,500 kitchen scenes; no other RoboCasa data
are used. Evaluation uses 50 tasks divided into three splits:
18 Atomic-Seen, 16 Composite-Seen, and 16 Composite-
Unseen tasks.
The Composite-Unseen tasks are not in-
cluded in Human300 and are therefore evaluated zero-shot.
Following the leaderboard protocol, each task is evaluated
over 50 environments.
Real-robot tasks.
Four real-robot tasks are evaluated:
two are performed by two LeKiwi robots, and the other
two by a heterogeneous team of two LeKiwi robots and
one OpenArm. Each trajectory records synchronized multi-
view RGB images, proprioception, and camera calibration.
Each task is evaluated over 100 trials.
4.2. Baselines and metrics
On RoboFactory and the real-robot tasks, World Dreamer
v5-lite is compared with six baselines: Diffusion Policy [6],
ACT [28], π0.5 [2], Fast-WAM [25], and two variants of
GauDP [23].
Global GauDP predicts the actions of all
agents with a single joint policy, whereas Local GauDP
controls each agent with an independent policy. The re-
maining baselines are single-robot policies; one model is
shared by all agents and is run once per agent at each de-
cision step. On RoboCasa365, World Dreamer v5-lite is
compared with recent advanced methods: PatchWAM [22],
ABot-M0.5 [5], PRTS [27], RLDX-1 [10], GigaWorld-
Policy 0.1 [24], π0.5 [2], and Diffusion Policy [6]. Their
results are taken from the official leaderboard [19].
The metric is the task success rate. For RoboFactory and
the real-robot tasks, the mean is the unweighted average
over tasks. For RoboCasa365, the overall score is the av-
erage over all 50 tasks, so the three splits are weighted by
their numbers of tasks.
4.3. Implementation details
The vision–language backbone is initialized from Qwen3-
VL [1]. Its vision encoder and language model are trained,
while the input embeddings and the language-modeling
head are frozen. Training uses bfloat16 precision and Deep-
Speed ZeRO Stage 2. Each vision–language forward pass
is reused for four flow-matching samples of the action de-
coder.

5. Conclusion
World Dreamer v5-lite places a shared world state ϕ at
the center of multi-robot action prediction. Calibrated ob-
servations from available cameras are aggregated into this
state. Each robot query reads the same state, forming an
all-to-one, one-to-all flow of visual information. Vision–
language context and relative-pose-aware query exchange
support coordination, while the flow-based decoder retains
a separate action stream for each robot. Masked-view co-
operative training withholds selected cameras from both vi-
sual branches while preserving action supervision for ev-
ery robot, encouraging the use of observations shared by
teammates. World Dreamer v5-lite achieves 74.0% mean
success on RoboFactory and 78.8% across four real-robot
tasks, and reaches 58.08% overall on RoboCasa365. The
shared-state design thus provides a common spatial refer-
ence for the team while preserving robot-specific control.
