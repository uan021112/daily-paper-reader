†Project leader: Chang Zhou. arXiv:2610.37509v1 [cs.RO] 27 Oct 2026 Abstract 

As egocentric video becomes increasingly available for embodied learning, an im-portant research question is how to turn this low-cost, scalable source of data into robot manipulation capabilities. Egocentric videos naturally provide rich context for high-level task understanding, while real-robot demonstrations with wrist obser-vations and action annotations provide supervision for fine-grained action alignment. We present Ego-CEVR, a data-native framework for hierarchical visual represen-tation learning that assigns distinct learning objectives to global and interaction representations. The global module learns object relations, task state, and temporal progress through future prediction on egocentric video. The interaction module interprets wrist observations in global context and learns local alignment and contact cues through robot action supervision. Separate residual paths expose both repre-sentations to the action policy, preserving direct access to task context alongside fine-grained execution information. Ego-CEVR supports independent pretraining of its global video module on large-scale egocentric videos and videos from diverse robot embodiments, without requiring robot action labels or paired wrist observations. 

## 1 Introduction 

In recent years, a series of works [1–4] and their applications have marked the rapid development of embodied manipulation. More and more embodied data from various sources such as egocentric video (Ego), Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) and cross embodiments data have been applied to the training of models.[5–7] As embodied data scale up, different types of data are expected to play distinct roles in robot learning.[8] An important challenge is to systematically use data from diverse sources with varying forms of supervision. [9–11]. However, how can we design data-native models that are specifically tailored to these various data sources in order to make better use of the data from these different sources? This issue has not yet been fully discussed. VLA policies transfer visual and linguistic priors to robot control, yet semantic generalization does not always translate into faithful execution on unseen tasks [12–14]. Predictive world models learn how scenes evolve during manipulation, but using this knowledge for precise local control remains challenging [15–17]. Integrating semantic understanding, world modeling, and action generation is therefore a promising direction [18, 19]. To benefit from heterogeneous video and robot data, such integration must also specify which representations can be learned without actions and how robot supervision should ground them in local control. Reusing manipulation experience from humans or other robots requires handling differences in morphology, action space, and local interaction geometry [20, 21]. Despite differences in embodiment, similar manipulation tasks often involve similar changes in the spatial and functional relationships between objects. Egocentric videos and robot head-camera videos can therefore both provide supervision for learning task-level visual representations [5, 22]. Wrist observations provide a closer view of the end effector and its target, revealing local cues for action alignment and contact. Video prediction alone may not sufficiently ground these cues in robot actions. We therefore learn global task context through video prediction and local interaction representations through robot action supervision. Following this principle, we introduce Ego-CEVR , a data-native framework for hierarchical visual represen-tation learning (Figure 2). It learns global task context from human egocentric and robot head-camera videos through future prediction, and learns local interaction representations from wrist observations under robot action supervision. ## 2 Related Work 

Vision-language-action policies. Vision-language-action (VLA) models transfer pretrained visual and linguistic knowledge to robot control, achieving substantial progress in semantic generalization, multitask manipulation, and deployment across environments [1–4, 26, 27]. However, object recognition and semantic understanding acquired from image-text data do not directly specify how object states should evolve during manipulation or how actions should realize an instruction. On unseen tasks, policies may reproduce familiar training behaviors, causing their execution to deviate from the intended instruction [21]. These limitations motivate incorporating future visual prediction into policy learning, providing temporal supervision through state transitions and enabling learning from video experience beyond action-labeled robot demonstrations [28, 29]. 

Predictive world models for robot control. Video foundation models provide rich spatiotemporal priors for learning manipulation processes and their visual consequences [30, 31]. Predictive policies guide action generation using future visual states or internal video-model representations, while world-action models further couple visual state evolution with robot actions [15, 16, 21, 32]. These approaches expand the use of video knowledge for control, but visual generation quality does not necessarily ensure the spatial precision required 3for fine manipulation. Visually plausible predictions may fail to preserve the relative positions, alignment, and contact relationships needed for accurate execution, and prediction errors can propagate into action errors on unseen tasks and scenes. Consequently, recent work more directly addresses the control relevance of predictive representations. GE-Act 2.0 develops a control-oriented latent space, while VPP2 improves predictive and action generalization through manipulation-video pretraining and subsequent adaptation [17, 33, 34]. Fast-WAM further demonstrates that predictive supervision can shape control representations during training without requiring explicit future-video generation at deployment [35]. These advances raise a central question: how should manipulation knowledge acquired from video be organized into policy conditions that support precise execution? 

Integrating vision-language understanding and world modeling. The complementary requirements of task semantics, manipulation dynamics, and local control motivate integrating vision-language understanding, world prediction, and action generation. One line of work jointly models video and actions or coordinates semantic understanding, visual prediction, and control through multiple experts [19, 36–38]. Another incorporates predictive information into VLA policies through future-latent alignment or attention-based conditioning [18, 39, 40]. These studies show that temporal knowledge learned through video prediction can complement the visual-language context of a base policy. For manipulation policies with both Head and Wrist observations, this integration also requires determining the learning roles of different views: how to acquire transferable task knowledge, how embodiment-dependent local interaction should use that knowledge, and how both should support action generation. Ego-CEVR addresses these questions through a representational hierarchy with an explicit conditional dependency. Head video prediction learns global representations, which condition the learning of execution-relevant interaction representations. The action expert accesses both through separate conditioning paths alongside the base-policy context. 

Cross-embodiment learning from heterogeneous egocentric data. Human egocentric videos capture diverse object interactions and task variations, providing coverage that is difficult to obtain through robot teleoperation alone. Early work uses such videos to learn transferable visual representations and rewards [41, 42]. Subsequent methods transfer human manipulation experience to robot policies through human-robot data alignment, hand-motion estimation, or action retargeting [5, 43, 44]. These approaches demonstrate the value of human experience, while their motion-estimation and alignment requirements also affect the range of usable data and the quality of available supervision. EgoWAM and Ego4WAM further investigate how prediction targets, data alignment, and supervision influence transfer, showing that video-only supervision can provide an effective foundation for subsequent robot learning [22, 45]. These findings highlight the need to match model learning objectives to the observations and supervision available across data sources. Ego-CEVR incorporates this requirement into a shared Head/Ego video learning interface spanning humans and heterogeneous robot embodiments. The global module supports independent learning without a common action space or paired Wrist observations, while robot demonstrations ground the learned knowledge in local interaction and the target robot’s action mapping. 

## 3 Method 

3.1 Overview and Data Formulation 

We use egocentric data to refer to first-person videos from either humans or robots. When the source needs to be specified, we use human egocentric videos and robot head-camera videos, respectively. Both are captured with non-fisheye RGB cameras. For egocentric data, only the first-person camera view is used, without paired wrist observations or robot action annotations. Real-robot demonstrations, in contrast, include observations from a head camera and two wrist cameras, together with robot action annotations. We denote the egocentric observation at time t by IHt and the pair of wrist observations by IWt . The action sequence used for policy training is denoted by At. We use ℓ for the language instruction when describing the language-conditioned modules below. As illustrated in Figure 2, Ego-CEVR consists of a global video representation module, an interaction representation module, and a representation-augmented action expert. The global module learns from 4egocentric video through future prediction and produces a representation Gt. The interaction module uses wrist observations and Gt to produce an interaction representation It under robot action supervision. The global module can therefore be pretrained on egocentric data independently of wrist observations and action annotations. We use π0.5 [4] as the base policy and retain its original encoding of observations, language instructions, and robot state. Two additional attention branches provide Gt and It to the action expert. The following subsections describe the representation learning objectives and action conditioning in detail. 

3.2 Predictive Global Representation Learning 

We use the diffusion transformer (DiT) [46] of the pretrained Wan2.2 video model [30, 47] as our visual backbone. Given the current egocentric observation IHt and language instruction ℓ, a frozen VAE encoder E

maps the observation to a latent zt = E(IHt ). The language-conditioned video DiT processes zt at a zero noise timestep. We extract attention value tokens from selected intermediate layers and concatenate them along the token dimension to form the global representation Gt. The resulting tokens retain spatial information from these layers for interaction modeling and action prediction. At inference, extracting Gt does not require generating future video. We supervise the video backbone through conditional flow matching [48] over future video latents. The frozen VAE encoder E maps the current egocentric frame to zt and the subsequent video frames to future latents 

Zt+1: t+h, where h denotes the prediction horizon in latent timesteps. During training, we keep zt clean and construct noisy future latents through linear interpolation: 

Zτt+1: t+h = (1 − τ )Zt+1: t+h + τ ϵ, ϵ ∼ N (0 , I ), τ ∈ [0 , 1] . (1) Given the noisy future latents Zτt+1: t+h and noise timestep τ , the video DiT predicts the vector field uv ,conditioned on zt and the language instruction ℓ. The prediction objective is 

Lpred = Eτ,ϵ [w(τ ) MSE ( uv , ϵ − Zt+1: t+h)] , (2) where w(τ ) is the timestep-dependent loss weight, and the mean squared error is computed only over valid future latent positions. By modeling visual changes during manipulation, Lpred trains the video backbone to produce global representations from current egocentric observations. The video backbone can be pretrained independently of the interaction module and action policy using human egocentric videos and robot head-camera videos. This stage requires video and corresponding text conditions, but no shared robot action space. During pretraining, only the video backbone is updated, while the VAE remains frozen. Downstream robot learning can initialize the video DiT either from this stage or directly from the pretrained Wan2.2 weights. 

3.3 Global-Conditioned Interaction and Action 

Given the current wrist observations IWt , we obtain wrist features Wt using the frozen VAE encoder and shared video DiT introduced in the previous section. The wrist observations are processed in a separate forward pass using the same cross-layer feature readout. The interaction network FI then attends to the global representation Gt to incorporate scene and task context into the wrist features, yielding an interaction representation: 

It = FI (Wt, G t). (3) The global representation Gt is computed from the egocentric observation and language instruction without using wrist features. The interaction representation It combines wrist features Wt with Gt, allowing local cues for approach, alignment, and contact to be interpreted in the context of the current task and scene. For action generation, we retain the original conditioning pathway of π0.5 for current egocentric and wrist observations, the language instruction, and robot state. Let Pt denote the resulting base-policy context. Conditioned on Pt, the action expert processes the noisy action sequence and produces final-layer hidden 5states ht. We augment these states with two independent cross-attention branches that read the global and interaction representations: 

˜ht = ht + α CrossAttn G(ht, G t) + β CrossAttn I (ht, I t). (4) Both branches use ht as queries. Their keys and values come from Gt and It, respectively. The learnable scaling parameters α and β are initialized to zero. The fused states ˜ht are mapped through the original output projection to the action vector field ua, and action sequences are generated using the flow-matching sampling procedure of π0.5.Following the flow-matching action formulation of π0.5 [4], we construct the noisy action sequence as 

Aτt = (1 − τ )At + τ ϵ a, ϵa ∼ N (0 , I ), τ ∈ [0 , 1] . (5) Here, At is the ground-truth action sequence. The action prediction objective is 

Lact = Eτ,ϵ a [MSE ( ua(Aτt , τ | Pt, G t, I t) , ϵ a − At)] . (6) This loss trains the action expert and the two attention branches to use the global and interaction representa-tions for action prediction. It also provides action supervision for the interaction network FI , encouraging It

to retain information relevant to local execution. During training on robot demonstrations, we combine action supervision with egocentric video prediction using the overall objective: 

Ltotal = Lact + λpred Lpred , (7) where λpred controls the relative contribution of the video prediction loss. The action-conditioning representa-tions Pt, Gt, and It are constructed from current observations and task conditions, while future egocentric frames are used only to compute Lpred .

## 4 Experiments 

We evaluate our method in simulation and on real world robots. Our simulation experiments use LIBERO [23], LIBERO-Plus [24], and RoboTwin 2.0 [25] to assess manipulation performance, robustness to distribution shifts, and bimanual manipulation, respectively. Controlled ablations examine the contributions of hierarchical representations and view-specific learning objectives. Real-robot experiments use the AgiBot G1 and G2 platforms to evaluate video-guided task transfer and few-shot cross-embodiment adaptation. We further investigate the benefits of human egocentric and robot head-camera videos on Plug Insertion, Water Pouring, and Package Sorting. 

4.1 Evaluation Setup 

Benchmarks. We evaluate manipulation performance on LIBERO, robustness to perturbations on LIBERO-Plus, and bimanual manipulation under the Easy and Hard settings of RoboTwin 2.0. All evaluations follow the standard protocols of the respective benchmarks, with task success rate (%) as the evaluation metric. For LIBERO-Plus, we evaluate our policy trained on LIBERO. 

Comparisons and experimental controls. We compare our method with representative vision-language-action policies and world-model-based manipulation approaches. In controlled ablations, we keep robot demonstrations, camera inputs, base-policy initialization, and evaluation settings consistent to assess the contribution of each design choice. Detailed ablation configurations and transfer settings are described in the corresponding subsections. Hierarchical representation structure. Compared with flat feature fusion, Ego-CEVR improves the average success rate from 96.9% to 97.7% on LIBERO and from 73.8% to 90.2% on LIBERO-Plus, with an improvement of approximately 16.4 percentage points on the latter. Under camera viewpoint perturbations, success increases from 66.7% to 91.1%, a gain of 24.4 percentage points. The larger gain on LIBERO-Plus suggests that hierarchical representations contribute to robustness under perturbations. We attribute this advantage to hierarchical representation learning: the global representation provides task goals and scene context, while the interaction representation learns local execution cues conditioned on this context. These results are consistent with our design of organizing global manipulation knowledge and local interaction information through hierarchical representation learning.

## 5 Discussion and Limitations 

The need for data-native models. Our experiments show that different embodied data sources provide different kinds of information for robot learning. This suggests that how a model organizes data and assigns learning objectives may set a limit on what it can learn as data scale grows. The embodied AI industry has accumulated substantial amounts of data, and making effective use of these diverse sources will remain an important challenge. We argue that embodied algorithms should be reconsidered from the perspective of data. Diverse data sources require data-native models designed to learn from different observations and forms of supervision. 

Scaling and hierarchical training. As embodied data scale up, larger models are expected to learn from increasingly diverse experience. Yet effective scaling across these data sources remains unresolved. This raises a further question of whether action-aligned supervision alone can lead to emergent capabilities, as observed in LLMs. If video generation scales successfully, transferring knowledge from egocentric video to fine-grained robot actions will still require a distinct stage of representation learning for action alignment. We therefore believe that multistage hierarchical training will become an important direction for embodied learning. 

Limitations and future work. Due to resource constraints, we did not pretrain on large-scale human egocentric video or conduct large-scale experiments with different data and model sizes. Whether the proposed framework continues to benefit from substantially larger video collections and exhibits scaling behavior remains an open question. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We presented Ego-CEVR, a data-native framework that learns complementary global and interaction repre-sentations from video prediction and robot action supervision. Across simulation and real-robot experiments, Ego-CEVR achieves competitive manipulation performance and improved robustness under visual perturba-tions. Ablations show the benefit of hierarchical representations and of assigning different learning objectives to head and wrist observations. On real robots, additional human egocentric videos improve manipulation performance and support transfer to a new task, while a few-shot demonstrations enable video-learned knowledge to be aligned with fine-grained actions on a new robot.
