# Ground Sample Distance Constrained k-Coverage Viewpoint Planning via Set Multi-Cover Formulation 

Waleed Bin Khalid and Chul Min Yeum 

Abstract — Drone-based 3D reconstruction and inspection re-quire viewpoints that satisfy image-quality constraints while ob-serving each surface region multiple times, which overlap-based planning heuristics do not guarantee. We study ground sample distance (GSD)-constrained k-coverage viewpoint selection for structures represented by surface voxels. Candidate viewpoints are generated along outward surface directions, screened with distance, viewing-angle, field-of-view, and occlusion tests, and augmented for voxels with insufficient visibility. Selection is then posed as set multi-cover with per-voxel coverage demands. We propose a dynamically weighted greedy heuristic that prioritizes remaining coverage deficits and scarce viewing alternatives, and compare it with sequential overlap-based pruning and an integer linear programming (ILP) formulation on the same can-didate pools. Across two structures, four voxel resolutions, and three coverage requirements, the proposed approach achieves full or near-full k-coverage with compact viewpoint sets, using only 3.00–16.39% more viewpoints than ILP while offering an interpretable selection process based on coverage deficits and viewpoint availability. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Autonomous aerial inspection and 3D reconstruction re-quire a set of camera viewpoints that observe all surface regions and satisfy image-quality constraints governing re-construction fidelity. In such applications, image resolution is commonly expressed through ground sampling distance (GSD), which directly determines the recoverable surface detail in the reconstructed model [1], [2], [3]. Photogrammetric flight planning commonly recommends 60–80% image overlap to support reliable reconstruction [1], [4]. Under idealized strip-imaging assumptions, a forward overlap ρ implies that a surface point appears in approxi-mately 1 /(1 − ρ) consecutive images, motivating multi-view redundancy. However, overlap is a global acquisition heuris-tic and does not directly enforce that each surface region is observed a prescribed number of times under sensing constraints, particularly in the presence of self-occlusions and complex geometry. In this study, we address the viewpoint generation and selection problem for aerial inspection of structures with known geometry (in a world coordinate frame). Specifically, we aim to generate a compact set of camera viewpoints, each defined by a waypoint and a camera orientation, that satisfies prescribed GSD and k-coverage requirements. The GSD constraint enforces a spatial-resolution bound under the adopted sensing model, while the k-coverage requirement

Surface voxels represent discrete observable regions, and candidate viewpoints are generated to satisfy geometric constraints derived from a prescribed GSD bound and maximum oblique viewing angle. Each candidate view-point observes a subset of surface voxels, yielding a set multi-cover formulation that seeks at least k observations per voxel, subject to the visibility available in the candidate set. We construct a sensing-compliant candidate set and select a smaller subset of viewpoints using a globally informed greedy k-coverage heuristic, evaluated against an integer lin-ear programming (ILP) reference formulation over the same candidate set. Viewpoint selection is explicitly decoupled from downstream tour generation, allowing discrete coverage requirements to be evaluated independently of trajectory optimization. Coverage path planning (CPP) ensures a robot observes or traverses all regions of an environment. Early work estab-lished complete coverage through geometric decomposition and topological reasoning [5], with surveys formalizing CPP taxonomies and coverage–efficiency trade-offs [6], [7], [8]. These approaches provide spatial coverage guarantees but primarily reason about traversal completeness rather than image (sensing) quality or multi-view observations. Modern inspection planners extend CPP to sensing-driven tasks by jointly selecting viewpoints and motion plans un-der geometric and robotic constraints [9]. Aerial inspection planners generate candidates through iterative or adaptive sampling to reconstruct complex structures [10], [11], while recent works incorporate sensing constraints such as field-of-view limits, incidence angle, and overlap heuristics [12], [13]. Reconstruction-oriented planners further improve imag-ing quality through continuous trajectory optimization or topology-aware planning [14], [15]. Several systems have been proposed that couple view-point generation with trajectory optimization [16], [17], [18]. While such integrated approaches consider both sensing and motion objectives, our work focuses specifically on viewpoint selection, allowing per-surface-region coverage requirements to be examined independently of trajectory optimization. arXiv:2610.36450v1 [cs.RO] 27 Oct 2026 Fig. 1: Overview of the proposed GSD-constrained k-coverage pipeline, from voxelization and sensing-compliant candidate generation to visibility assignment, augmentation, and final viewpoint selection. 

Sequential redundancy reduction has been used in view-point planning, where candidate viewpoints are discarded if their visibility regions overlap substantially with previously selected views [10], [11]. However, overlap-based pruning does not, by itself, enforce a prescribed number of observa-tions for each surface region, and its outcome can depend on viewpoint ordering and overlap thresholds. Recent work has formulated viewpoint planning as acombinatorial set-cover problem. Adaptive spatial subdivi-sion combined with ILP optimization has been used to obtain minimum-cardinality viewpoint sets with complete feature coverage [19], [20]. Related multi-cover formulations have also been studied in geometric coverage problems, demonstrating the use of explicit redundancy constraints in combinatorial optimization. These formulations establish mathematical approaches to coverage selection. Our work builds on this perspective by incorporating GSD-constrained candidate generation, coverage-driven viewpoint augmenta-tion, and per-surface-region multi-cover requirements. Voxel representations are widely used to simplify visibil-ity reasoning in complex environments. Voxelized planners generate viewpoints around clustered surface regions prior to trajectory optimization [21], and enriched voxel maps support planning in dynamic environments [22]. Existing work has addressed geometric coverage, sensing-constrained viewpoint generation, and combinatorial view-point optimization through different formulations. Building on these approaches, this work considers their integration for GSD-constrained k-coverage over voxelized surfaces. Specif-ically, we combine sensing-aware candidate generation, tar-geted augmentation of undercovered surface regions, and set multi-cover optimization to select compact viewpoint sets satisfying prescribed coverage requirements when feasible. III. V IEWPOINT GENERATION 

A. Ground Sample Distance (GSD) Formulation 

In the general case where the sensor images the surface at an oblique angle, the GSD is given by GSD = Rs × 2 tan (0.5θFOV )

w × cos (α) (1) where Rs = √d2 + h2 is the slant distance between the sensor and the surface, d is the lateral offset relative to the surface normal, h is the perpendicular distance from the sensor to the surface, w is the horizontal number of pixels, θFOV is the horizontal field of view of the camera, and α is the oblique viewing angle between the camera viewing direction and the surface normal. For our algorithm, we require that the GSD evaluated at each visible voxel center does not exceed a prescribed GSD max under the adopted geometric sensing model. To enforce this constraint, we fix a maximum α value and compute the corresponding slant range Rs using GSD max 

and α. Viewpoints are then placed at the perpendicular component of this slant range, h = Rs cos (α), along the outward surface normal. Under this placement, the nominal nadir GSD becomes GSD set = GSD max cos 2(α).

B. Voxelization 

Given a point cloud or mesh, we select a voxel size s

and construct a uniform 3D grid. Each voxel v has a grid index (i, j, k), and its center is computed as ci jk = ow + s ·

(i + 0.5, j + 0.5, k + 0.5), where ow is the origin. 

C. Labeling Exterior and Interior Space 

Some empty cells lie within sealed cavities unreachable from outside. To distinguish exterior space from interior cavities, the grid is padded with a layer of empty cells on all sides, and a flood-fill is performed from a corner of this boundary through empty cells only. Reached cells are labeled 

exterior ; unreached cells are classified as interior cavities .

D. Computing Normals 

Each surface voxel requires an outward-pointing normal to place a camera at a physically meaningful viewing direction. A direct option is to average the normals of mesh or point-cloud samples within the voxel, but this is input-dependent and sensitive to within-voxel sampling density, where sparse or uneven samples can yield unstable normals. We therefore compute voxel normals from exterior-facing faces only, using the exterior labeling from the previous step. For each occupied voxel, we examine its 6-connected neighbors. A face is exterior-facing if the neighbor cell is empty and labeled exterior. If a voxel has no exterior-facing face, it is classified as internal (e.g., fully surrounded by occupied cells or facing only cavities) and is not assigned a normal. For all others, the voxel normal is the normalized sum of the corresponding face normals: 

nvoxel = ∑exterior-facing faces nface 

∑exterior-facing faces nface 

If this sum is negligible due to opposing exterior faces on a thin wall or edge, the voxel generates multiple viewing directions. In such cases, each exterior-facing face normal is stored as a separate multi-normal record, generating one candidate viewpoint per direction. Because only exterior-facing faces are considered, internal voxels and inward-pointing normals are not produced, resulting in outward-facing directions for surface voxels. 

E. Generating Initial Viewpoints 

For each surface voxel, an initial candidate viewpoint is placed at the GSD-derived distance h along the normal direction using p = cvoxel + h · nvoxel . The viewpoint direction vector is defined as o = −nvoxel . The camera orientation at each viewpoint is defined by aligning its optical axis with 

o, so that the sensor directly faces the generating surface element. For multi-normal voxels, one viewpoint is generated per normal direction. 

F. Removing Blocked Viewpoints 

Some viewpoints may have an occluded line of sight to their generating voxel. We apply a ray pre-filter in which for each viewpoint at p, a ray is cast along its direction vector, 

o, towards the target voxel and discarded if the first occupied intersection lies closer than the expected distance. A toler-ance τ = s√3/2 compensates for voxel surface intersections. 

G. Assigning Visible Voxels to a Viewpoint and Identifying Undercovered Voxels 

The GSD-derived viewpoint placement establishes sens-ing constraints under the adopted geometric model. Since cos 2(α) ≤ 1, the nominal GSD setpoint satisfies GSD set ≤

GSD max . Candidate visibility is subsequently evaluated using distance and viewing-angle thresholds, defined as Tdist = Rs

and Tang = α, respectively. These constraints are applied to voxel-center observations and provide a discrete approxima-tion of sensing compliance rather than a guarantee over the entire voxel surface. Each viewpoint, initially generated from a single voxel, may observe multiple voxels simultaneously. A voxel with center cv and normal vector nv is considered visible from viewpoint p if it satisfies distance, deviation, FOV, and occlu-sion constraints [12]. The distance constraint requires ∥cv −

p∥ ≤ Tdist and the deviation constraint requires arccos (−o ·

nv) ≤ Tang . The FOV constraint requires arccos (ˆo · ˆq) ≤

0.5θFOV , where q = cv − p. Finally, occlusions are checked using the same ray test employed for blocked-viewpoint re-moval, retaining only unobstructed visibility relations under the adopted model. Each surface voxel is assigned a seen-by-waypoints (SBW) count, defined as SBW (v) = ∑p j ∈P I[v ∈ Vj], where P denotes the generated candidate viewpoint set, Vj is the set of surface voxels visible from viewpoint p j, and I[·] is the indicator function. Voxels with SBW (v) ≥ k have sufficient candidate visibility to satisfy the requested k-coverage, while 0 <

SBW (v) < k indicates insufficient candidate visibility and SBW (v) = 0 indicates no candidate visibility. Voxels with SBW (v) < k are therefore identified for targeted viewpoint augmentation. 

H. Viewpoint Augmentation 

For voxels with SBW (v) < k, additional candidate view-points are generated to address coverage deficiencies. New viewing directions are sampled within a cone centered on the voxel’s outward normal, introducing alternative oblique viewpoints that may improve candidate visibility in hard-to-observe regions. Given a voxel normal n, a local tangent frame (t1, t2) is constructed. Directions are sampled by tilting the normal by angle β and rotating around it by angle 

φ , yielding dtilt = cos (β )n + sin (β ) ( cos (φ )t1 + sin (φ )t2). A viewpoint is placed along each sampled direction as p =

cvoxel +h dtilt , where h is the same GSD-derived distance used for initial viewpoint generation. Candidates are validated using the same ray pre-filter, then merged into the viewpoint pool and incorporated into visibility and SBW updates. IV. S ELECTING A SUBSET OF VIEWPOINTS

We compare our method with a locally guided overlap-pruning heuristic and an ILP formulation that provides minimum-cardinality solutions to the discrete multi-cover problem when optimality is certified. As discussed in Sec-tion II, overlap-based pruning is widely used in coverage planning. We formalize this as Sequential Overlap-Based Viewpoint Pruning , in which candidate viewpoints are pro-cessed sequentially and accepted only if their visible region contributes sufficient new coverage relative to previously selected viewpoints [10], [12]. While such heuristics promote redundancy, they do not explicitly enforce per-voxel multi-cover constraints. We therefore include this method as a representative redundancy-reduction baseline to assess how explicit k-coverage reasoning alters coverage behavior.

A viewpoint is selected only if its overlap with G is strictly less than τ. This ensures that views that mostly see already-covered voxels are pruned. We consider two sorting strategies as well. In vertical ordering, viewpoints are processed from bottom to top, and in visibility-based ordering, viewpoints are processed in descending order of the number of voxels they can observe. This strategy exhibits several structural limitations. First, it introduces a starting-point bias , as the first candidate is always accepted and early decisions disproportionately influence the final solution. This leads to order dependence ,where different candidate orderings can produce substan-tially different viewpoint sets. More fundamentally, overlap pruning provides no explicit coverage guarantee : unfavorable thresholds or orderings may leave voxels uncovered even when feasible solutions exist. The selection process is also local in nature, as each candidate is evaluated only with respect to previously selected viewpoints rather than the global coverage state. Decisions are therefore made sequen-tially based on current overlap information without explicitly reasoning about unmet coverage requirements across the entire voxel set. As a global baseline, we formulate the k-coverage require-ment as an ILP, yielding an exact combinatorial model of the set multi-cover problem that minimizes the number of selected viewpoints subject to explicit per-voxel coverage constraints. The optimization is performed over the gener-ated candidate set, and a minimum-cardinality solution is established when optimality is certified.

The ILP is solved using a branch-and-bound procedure, in which integrality constraints are relaxed to obtain linear-programming lower bounds, and subproblems are pruned when they cannot improve the best feasible integer solution. If optimality is certified within the imposed time limit, the solution is provably optimal over the generated candidate set; otherwise, the best feasible solution obtained is returned. Although the ILP yields a minimum-cardinality solution when optimality is certified, its worst-case complexity is exponential due to the NP-hardness of set multi-cover. The formulation introduces one binary decision variable per can-didate viewpoint and one coverage constraint per voxel, so the instance size grows quickly with finer voxelizations and richer augmentation. In practice, solve time depends not only on the number of variables/constraints but also on the struc-ture of the visibility matrix (e.g., redundancy and overlap patterns), and some large instances may not certify optimality within a fixed time budget. We therefore use the ILP as a reference for comparing viewpoint-set cardinality, with certified optimal solutions providing a minimum-cardinality benchmark over the generated candidate set. VI. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

This work formulates aerial inspection viewpoint selec-tion as a GSD-constrained k-coverage problem over vox-elized surface representations. Candidate viewpoints are con-structed and filtered under a geometric sensing model, while targeted augmentation increases the availability of candi-date observations for undercovered voxels. Set multi-cover optimization then enables explicit evaluation of per-voxel coverage requirements over the resulting discrete candidate pool. The proposed Globally Informed Greedy k-Coverage heuristic prioritizes remaining coverage deficits and scarce viewing alternatives through an interpretable, dynamically weighted selection rule. Across the evaluated structures, voxel sizes, and coverage levels, it achieves full or near-full requested k-coverage with compact viewpoint sets, requiring 3.00–16.39% more viewpoints than the corresponding ILP solutions. The ILP comparison provides a discrete minimum-cardinality reference where optimality is certified; neither global camera-pose optimality nor a theoretical approxima-tion guarantee is claimed. The present study assumes known, static structure ge-ometry and evaluates coverage using modeled voxel-center sensing and visibility constraints. It does not assess mini-mum viewpoint separation, UAV reachability or dynamics, collision-safe trajectories, pipeline runtime, or downstream reconstruction quality; the evaluation is also limited to two structures. Future work may incorporate execution feasibility and reconstruction-based assessment while retaining explicit coverage reasoning.
