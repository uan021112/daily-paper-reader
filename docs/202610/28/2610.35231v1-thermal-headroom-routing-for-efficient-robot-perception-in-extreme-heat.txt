Abstract —A robot working in a Phoenix summer runs its detector on a small embedded processor that heats itself. Once the chip reaches its thermal limit, the governor throttles it and every detector configuration slows down together. Adaptive inference routers pick a model size and input resolution per frame and react to latency, which in hot weather arrives after the router’s own earlier choices have already heated the chip. We model this with an RC thermal plant wired in closed loop to a deadline-constrained router over four YOLO11 configurations, and propose Thermal-Headroom Routing (THR), which forecasts the chip temperature in closed form and skips configurations that would cross the throttle threshold. Heating measured on an Apple M5 under nonstop YOLO11 inference fits the one-node model within 1 °C, with a time constant and thermal resistance close to the values we assumed. We test THR on seven real traces with camera degradation, eight ambient temperatures and 20 seeds, with Holm correction across traces, against a latency-belief router, a fixed-threshold thermostat, duty caps tuned offline, and a control given THR’s own heavy-model budget. With the deadline at the large model’s median latency and the air between 45 and 55 °C, THR never throttles and beats the belief router by 3.9 to 7.5 percentage points of deadline misses and 12 to 15% of energy per on-time frame on every trace. All temperature-aware policies, THR included, lie on one accuracy-timeliness curve set by the share of heavy-model frames, so at one ambient on a known chip a well-tuned thermostat matches THR. The forecast pays off when conditions move away from the ones a rule was tuned for. A duty cap tuned at 45 °C throttles 16% of the time at 50 °C and 31% at 55 °C, and on a chip 20% hotter than assumed an 88 °C thermostat throttles up to 29% of the time, while THR, given that chip’s measured heating parameters, stays at zero. These results hold, with larger effects, when every decision is given one deadline period of wall-clock time. With 25% deadline slack, throttling costs little and the belief router wins on on-time utility. Code and outputs are public. 1

## 1. Introduction 

Consider a Jetson-class module in a sealed enclosure on a robot in the Sonoran Desert, with the air at 45 °C. If the perception stack runs its largest detector on every frame, the chip reaches its throttle limit in about a minute. The governor then down-clocks it, and the small models slow down along with the large one until the chip cools [1], [2]. In our simulations a policy that always runs the large model goes 12.5 s without an on-time detection at 45 °C and 91 s at 55 °C. Fig. 1 shows one two-minute mission at 45 °C: a router that reacts to latency drives the chip into repeated throttle cycles, and THR keeps it just under the line. Adaptive inference is the usual answer to a tight latency budget. Early-exit networks and routers choose cheaper computation per input or per frame [3]–[5], and contention-aware detectors such as ApproxDet [6] also watch the load from co-located applications. These systems react to latency once it has risen. On a hot chip that signal arrives late, arXiv:2610.35231v1 [cs.RO] 26 Oct 2026 because the slowdown was caused by earlier choices and lasts tens of seconds. Thermal-aware DVFS [7] and thermal-aware multi-DNN scheduling on phones [8] address heat by controlling frequency or placement. We ask a narrower question that matters for robots with a fixed set of detector configurations: how should the router itself use temperature, and how much does a temperature forecast add over simpler rules? Belief-space perception routing [9], which this work extends, left thermal effects out of its contention model. Our contributions: (i) a closed-loop model of thermal contention for adaptive inference, in which the router’s configuration choice sets the power input to an RC thermal node with a hysteretic throttle (§3); (ii) THR, a closed-form rule that keeps the forecast temperature under the throttle limit at about 7 μs per frame (§4); (iii) an evaluation against baselines chosen to beat it: a thermostat, duty caps tuned offline, a matched-budget control in the spirit of the matched-input controls of [10], a deadline sweep, and five variations of the true chip, with 20 seeds and Holm correction (§6); and (iv) an account of where THR helps and where it does not. Its gain over latency-only routing comes from rationing the heavy model. The forecast itself pays off when the chip heats differently from what a fixed rule assumed, and with deadline slack, throttling costs less than rationing does. 

## 2. Related Work 

Adaptive and efficient inference. Early-exit networks [3], [4], dynamic transformers [5] and early exiting for driving [11]–[13] vary computation with the input. Modality gating [14]–[16] decides which sensors to process. ApproxDet [6] selects detection branches under a latency SLA using measured contention from co-located apps. In all of these the compute budget is fixed or observed. None treats it as a state the policy’s own choices drive. 

Thermal behaviour of edge inference. On a Raspberry Pi, long CNN runs throttle within seconds without active cooling, and outdoor ambients between 22 and 36 °C change throughput by up to 27.7% [2]. A Jetson AGX Orin in an outdoor enclosure throttled under a synthetic stress test at a +40 °C setpoint (GPU 95.6 °C, CPU 99.0 °C), while a YOLOv8 workload drew about half the power and stayed below the threshold [17]. Throttling therefore depends on the model, the cooling and the duty cycle, which is why we sweep the plant parameters below. Measurements on phone SoCs show throttling dominating sustained performance [1]. 

Thermal management. HotSpot-style RC models [18] and temperature-aware MPSoC scheduling [19] predict hot spots and schedule around them. zTT [7] learns CPU/GPU frequency settings with reinforcement learning so that phones avoid throttling. Phoenix [8] combines processor placement with multi-exit networks to delay throttling for multi-DNN video apps. THR works one level up: it leaves the governor alone and chooses among detector configurations using a temperature forecast. It could run on top of a DVFS policy like zTT.

## 3. System Model 

Frontier and deadline. There are four configurations, from most to least expensive: C1 is YOLO11x at 1280 px, C2 is YOLO11x at 640, C3 is YOLO11n at 1280 and C4 is YOLO11n at 640, with per-frame latencies profiled under normal and contended compute [9]. The base deadline D is C1’s median latency under normal compute, between 211 and 341 ms depending on the trace, so C1 meets D about half the time on a cool chip and C2 to C4 always meet it. Section 6.6 relaxes D to 1.25 × and 1.5×. Accuracy is the IoU-matched F1 agreement between a configuration’s detections and C1’s on the same frame. This pseudo-ground truth ranks configurations differently from real annotations [9], so accuracy and utility numbers are internal consistency checks. Miss, throttling and energy numbers do not depend on it.

Closed loop. Whether the chip is throttled at frame t

depends on what the router chose before t, so each policy runs in closed loop with the plant. For a given seed, all policies see the same pre-drawn latency samples (normal and contended), the same camera measurement noise and the same temperature-sensor noise. 

Frame timing. In the main experiments the traces play at their 10 Hz recording rate and each recorded frame is one routing decision. The chosen configuration runs once on that frame, its latency is drawn from the profiled distribution, and the result counts as a miss if it exceeds D. Late results are dropped, so there is no queue and a late frame never delays the next one. Power is charged through the duty u(c), and the thermal model advances ∆t = 0 .1 s per decision. Because C1 takes about D (211 to 341 ms) to run, this compresses wall-clock time by a factor of 2.1 to 3.4. Section 6.5 repeats the heat experiment with physically consistent timing, where every decision lasts exactly D, and the conclusions hold. 

## 4. Policies 

All policies use the objective from [9]. They predict a compute state, keep the configurations whose median latency in that state meets D, and serve the one with the highest expected accuracy given the camera-fault belief bf . A 10-frame dwell keeps them from switching back and forth.

## 5. Experimental Setup 

Traces. Seven real traces with the frontiers and fault labels of [9]: the full TartanDrive drive, which has no camera faults and is our negative control, a 309-frame stretch of rough terrain, RADIATE rain, snow, fog and night, and WoodScape fisheye video with real soiling masks. Each trace is repeated to fill a 1200-decision mission (2 minutes at the 10 Hz recording rate). 

Experiments. E1 re-scores the original fault-coupled contention schedule for bursts of misses. E2 sweeps ambient temperature from 20 to 55 °C with thermal contention only. E3 adds fault-coupled contention on top of throttling. E4 gives THR a wrong Rth or τ . E5 relaxes the deadline. E6 changes the true chip. E7 and E8 vary THR’s H and m and the thermostat threshold, as sensitivity analyses. E9 sweeps fixed C1 caps between 0.2 and 1.0. E11 repeats E2 with physically consistent frame timing (§6.5).

## 6. Results 

6.1. Cool air 

At 35 °C and below no policy brings the chip to Thi . THR, Belief, Reactive, Thermostat and Matched budget make the same choice on every frame and their miss rates differ by exactly 0.0 percentage points on all seven traces (Fig. 2). At 40 °C THR starts holding C1 back a little early (C1 on 93 to 94% of frames, agreement 0.97 to 1.00), because its 5 s forecast already crosses 88 °C. 

6.2. Hot air, against latency-only routing 

Table 2 lists miss rate, throttling, blackout length, power, energy and accuracy for every policy. Static C1 is throttled 20% of the time at 45 °C with a 12.5 s blackout, and 76% of the time at 55 °C with a 91 s blackout and 88% of deadlines missed. Belief and Reactive keep blackouts near 1 s but are throttled 11 to 13% of the mission at 45 °C and 32 to 38% at 55 °C, because they react after the governor has stepped in (Fig. 1). THR was never throttled on any trace, seed or temper-ature. Against Belief it lowers the miss rate by 3.9 to 5.4 points at 45 °C and 6.7 to 7.5 at 55 °C, significant on every trace at both temperatures (Table 3). Energy per on-time frame falls by 12% at 45 °C and 13% at 55 °C, also on every trace. The price is accuracy: mean agreement at 45 °C drops to 0.950, against 0.975 for Belief. 6.3. Simple temperature rules and the trade-off curve 

A thermostat at 88 °C also never throttles. THR misses 1.3 to 1.9 points fewer deadlines than it at 45 °C and 2.9 to 4.4 at 55 °C (all seven traces), and has higher on-time utility on 6 and 7 traces, while the thermostat keeps 0.8 to 1.6 points more accuracy. The thermostat threshold is a dial, though. Sweeping it between 84 and 89 °C (E8) traces an accuracy-timeliness curve, and THR lands on that curve, between the 86 and 87 °C settings at 45 °C and at the 85 °C setting at 55 °C. Fig. 3 shows the same thing for duty caps: every temperature-aware policy sits on the curve traced by fixed C1 caps, and the matched-budget control, which spends THR’s C1 share with no temperature input, matches THR’s miss rate on 6 of 7 traces at 55 °C and all 7 at 45 °C. At a single ambient and on the assumed chip, the share of C1 frames determines the outcome, and the forecast adds little beyond choosing that share well. Choosing it well across temperatures is harder than it looks. A cap tuned at 45 °C matches THR at 45 °C, then throttles 16% of the mission at 50 °C and 31% at 55 °C, where THR misses 3.1 to 6.6 points fewer deadlines on every trace (Table 4). Below 45 °C the same cap gives up 3.6 to 5.4 points of accuracy that THR keeps. A cap tuned at 55 °C costs 7.1 points of accuracy at 45 °C and 12.1 at 35 °C. The matched budget also needed THR to supply its number, and at 55 °C it still throttled up to 15.7% of the mission on three traces. 

6.4. A different chip, with a calibrated forecast 

The thermostat’s threshold and the caps were set for one plant. In E6 the true chip differs from Table 1 and THR is given the matching model, as if the chip had been characterized (Table 5). THR stayed at 0.0% throttling on all five variants, both temperatures and every trace. The 88 °C thermostat throttled up to 29.0% of the mission at 55 °C when Rth was 20% higher, and up to 2.7% when the chip heated twice as fast ( τ × 0.5). It reacts to the current reading, and a chip that overshoots after the reading crosses 88 °C reaches 90 °C anyway. THR looks 5 s ahead and sees the overshoot coming. THR’s miss reduction over Belief was significant on all seven traces in every variant except the cooler chip at 45 °C, where nothing throttled and the difference was exactly zero. 6.5. Physically consistent frame timing 

In E11 each decision lasts one deadline period D of wall-clock time. The thermal plant integrates over ∆t = D

per decision. At each decision the router takes the newest recorded frame, so decision k uses the trace frame at time kD

6.6. When the deadline has slack 

With D at C1’s median, C1 misses about half its frames even on a cool chip, so part of every policy’s miss reduction comes from running C1 less. E5 removes that effect (Table 7). At 1.25 × and 1.5× the median, C1 meets the deadline on a cool chip and only throttling causes misses. Belief then misses just 0.5% of frames at 45 °C and 1.0% at 55 °C, even though it is throttled 12 and 36% of the time, because C2 to C4 still finish in time on a slow chip and Belief moves to them after one slow frame. THR’s miss rate is 0.0%, a small but significant difference on every trace. On-time utility goes the other way: Belief is significantly better on 6 of 7 traces (0.970 against 0.950 at 45 °C), since THR’s rationing of C1 costs more accuracy than throttling does. THR still uses 5% less energy per on-time frame (2.17 against 2.28 J at 45 °C). With deadline slack, THR is worth running only when throttling has costs outside this metric, such as energy, a planner sharing the SoC, or wear.

6.8. THR’s settings and a wrong model 

Because THR forecasts from the measured temperature, no setting we tried brought throttling back in a meaningful way. Over H ∈ { 20 , 50 , 100 } frames and m ∈ { 0, 2, 4} °C (E7), throttling stayed at 0.0% except for the most aggressive setting ( H = 20 , m = 0 ) at 55 °C, which throttled up to 0.6% of the mission. Longer horizons and larger margins move THR down the same curve: at 45 °C the miss reduction over Belief is between 0.5 and 11.0 points and mean agreement between 0.923 and 0.969, significant on all traces at every setting. A wrong thermal model has the same effect (E4, Table 9). Underestimating Rth by 30% shrinks the miss gain to 0.8 to 1.8 points and keeps more accuracy, and overestimating it does the opposite. THR has one dial that matters, its effective conservatism, and getting the model roughly right is enough to keep the chip out of throttling.

## 7. Discussion and Limitations 

Reading the temperature is what keeps a router out of throttling; routers that only watch latency throttle in every hot run we tried. At one ambient on a known chip, the share of heavy-model frames sets the accuracy-timeliness point, and a thermostat or duty cap at the right setting does as well as THR. The forecast earns its place when the ambient changes or the chip heats faster or settles hotter than the rule assumed. it. On a chip that has been characterized well, a tuned rule is just as good. The deadline matters as much as the temperature. With the deadline at the large model’s median latency, avoiding throttling reduces misses and energy. With 25% slack, the cheaper models absorb throttling, and a latency-only router delivers more on-time accuracy than THR. Deployments should check which side of that line they are on. The study is a simulation built on real camera traces and real profiled latencies. The plant is one RC node with parameters we chose; heating measured on an Apple M5 fits that model with parameters close to ours, but the M5 never throttled, so throttling reuses the profiled contended latency distribution instead of real DVFS states. The real throttle points reported for the Jetson AGX Orin are higher (95.6 and 99.0 °C [17]), and a lighter model in a ventilated enclosure did not throttle at 40 °C. Our plant variations cover part of that range, but only hardware measurements can settle it. Latencies come from an Apple M5 GPU. Traces are repeated to reach mission length. Accuracy is agreement with the largest model, which tracks real annotations poorly [9]. Seeds change noise on fixed frames and do not stand in for independent missions. Next we plan to measure Rth , τ and throttle behaviour on a Jetson Orin in an environmental chamber at 25 to 55 °C with the included tegrastats harness, score the routers against real annotations, and test whether a duty budget learned online from temperature logs can replace the RC model. 

## 8. Conclusion 

On a hot robot, choosing the large detector now can cause throttling a minute later. THR gives the router a closed-form temperature forecast that costs microseconds per frame. With a tight deadline it kept the chip out of throttling on all seven traces at 45 and 55 °C and cut deadline misses by 3.9 to 7.5 percentage points and energy per on-time frame by 12 to 13% relative to latency-only routing. A tuned thermostat reaches the same operating points on the chip it was tuned for, but throttled for up to 29% of the mission on a chip 20% hotter than assumed, where THR stayed at zero. With deadline slack, throttling is cheap and THR’s rationing is not worth its accuracy cost. For teams building perception on hot hardware, the practical advice is to give the router a temperature input, and to use a forecast when the chip’s thermal behaviour is uncertain.
