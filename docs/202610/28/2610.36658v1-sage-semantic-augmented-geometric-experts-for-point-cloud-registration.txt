Abstract
Point cloud registration is an essential task for scene recon-
struction, requiring the alignment of partially overlapping
point clouds. Under low-overlap conditions, this task re-
mains challenging because existing geometry-only methods
cannot distinguish geometrically similar but semantically
distinct structures (e.g., TV corners vs. desk corners and
chair legs vs. table legs), resulting in incorrect correspon-
dences. To address this geometric ambiguity, we present
Semantic-Augmented Geometric Experts (SAGE), which
integrates frozen pre-trained point cloud semantic represen-
tations into the registration pipeline without requiring addi-
tional sensors or fine-tuning the semantic encoder. To effec-
tively leverage these semantic distinctions, SAGE employs
a Semantic-Guided Mixture of Experts (SG-MoE) architec-
ture. Through semantic-aware routing, SG-MoE enables
each expert to specialize in distinct semantic-geometric pat-
terns, reducing mismatches that confound geometry-only
methods. With a structure-aware estimator, SAGE achieves
the highest registration recall on 3DLoMatch while remain-
ing competitive on 3DMatch. When evaluated with the same
number of refinement iterations, SAGE further achieves the
highest inlier ratio and registration recall on both bench-
marks. These results indicate that frozen semantic represen-
tations provide effective cues for resolving geometric ambi-
guity in low-overlap registration.
1. Introduction
Point cloud registration is an essential task for 3D percep-
tion in robotics, autonomous driving, and augmented re-
ality [18]. Despite progress, achieving robust registration
under challenging conditions with low overlap remains an
open problem. Current registration methods [17, 19, 26, 48,
50] generally follow a correspondence-based pipeline. This
pipeline first establishes point-to-point correspondences be-
tween two point clouds using deep neural networks, and
then estimates the transformation from these correspon-
dences. Thus, this pipeline’s success depends on the qual-
ity of the correspondences. However, geometrically simi-

insufficient points surrounding the target point to distin-

limitations. Even recent Mixture of Experts (MoE) methods
like PSReg [19], which use geometric feature-based routing
to activate geometry-specialized experts, depend only on
geometric representations. Although post-processing meth-
ods such as DecisionPCR [56] can boost results through it-
erative refinement, they still operate within the geometric
domain, limited by the quality ceiling of geometry-based
correspondence establishment. As one possible approach
to overcome this limitation, several methods use semantic
information for registration. However, they often require
RGB-D sensors or calibrated cameras, while others rely
on domain-specific models that generalize poorly across
datasets [15, 22, 25, 45].
To overcome the fundamental limitations of relying
solely on geometric features, we introduce Semantic-
Augmented Geometric Experts (SAGE). SAGE integrates
frozen pre-trained semantic representations into point cloud
registration through a semantic-guided mixture-of-experts
architecture that jointly exploits geometric and semantic
features.
This design requires neither additional sensors
nor fine-tuning of the semantic encoder. First, SAGE em-
ploys the frozen semantic feature extractor of the recent pre-
trained model Sonata [43] to extract semantic features of the
input source and target point clouds. Then, SAGE jointly
encodes the semantic features with input point clouds of the
source and target to extract their corresponding semantic-
geometric features.
After this feature extraction, SAGE
refines the semantic-geometric features via our proposed
Semantic-Guided MoE (SG-MoE).
Our main technical contribution is the design of SG-
MoE. If Standard MoE [13, 31] is used for feature re-
finement, as in PSReg [19], geometric knowledge be-
comes dominant and semantic knowledge is neglected in
the semantic-geometric features because the loss functions
commonly adopted in registration tasks primarily optimize
geometric fidelity. To prevent this domination, we design
the expert routing in SG-MoE to be based solely on se-
mantic features extracted from the frozen Sonata. This de-
sign enables each expert to refine the semantic-geometric
features by explicitly considering semantic knowledge. As
a result, SG-MoE performs the feature refinement process
while preserving both semantic and geometric information.
Finally, SAGE estimates the transformation between the
source and target point clouds using their refined semantic-
geometric features following the superpoint matching in
GeoTransformer [26].
As demonstrated in Fig. 1, while
geometry-only methods like GeoTransformer fail to distin-
guish geometrically similar structures (producing incorrect
correspondences shown in red), our SAGE successfully dis-
tinguishes them by leveraging semantic knowledge, achiev-
ing accurate registration even in low-overlap scenarios. Our
contributions are:
• We propose SAGE, which integrates frozen pre-trained
semantic representations into point cloud registration
through a two-stage feature extraction without addi-
tional sensors or retraining, overcoming the limitations of
purely geometric features.
• We design Semantic-Guided MoE (SG-MoE), where
routing is determined solely by semantic features while
experts process semantic-geometric features, enabling the
distinction of geometrically similar but semantically dif-
ferent structures.
• Evaluation on 3DMatch [54], 3DLoMatch [17], and
KITTI [16]1 demonstrates that SAGE achieves the high-
est registration recall on 3DLoMatch among single-pass
methods with a structure-aware estimator [26], the best
feature matching recall across all sample counts and
the best inlier ratio with 1000 or fewer samples under
RANSAC, and the lowest translation error on KITTI,
while performing on par with iterative methods [19, 51].
2. Related Work
Classical point cloud registration. Traditional methods
laid the foundation for automatic point cloud alignment
through coarse-to-fine pipelines [18]. These methods rely
on handcrafted geometric descriptors [20, 29, 38] to encode
local geometry, establish correspondences using RANdom
SAmple Consensus (RANSAC) variants [1, 5, 10, 14], and
refine alignment through Iterative Closest Point (ICP) [6,
9, 24, 28]. These methods successfully automated funda-
mental registration tasks and introduced the coarse-to-fine
pipeline that remains widely adopted. Unfortunately, be-
cause handcrafted descriptors are not learned from data,
these methods are sensitive to noise and partial observa-
tions, resulting in limited robustness.
Learning-based point cloud registration.
Deep learn-
ing addressed the discriminative power limitations of hand-
crafted descriptors by introducing learnable feature repre-
sentations.
Pioneering works such as 3DMatch [54] for
RGB-D scenes and FCGF [11] with sparse convolutions
demonstrated improvements in both feature discriminabil-
ity and robustness to noise and partial observations by learn-
ing local geometric features from large-scale datasets [3, 4,
12, 47]. However, a new challenge emerged in low-overlap
scenarios where limited spatial context makes correspon-
dence establishment particularly difficult. Unlike partial ob-
servations (which concern the quality and completeness of
individual point clouds), low-overlap refers to the limited
shared observable region between two point clouds. Even
when each scan is individually complete, establishing reli-
able correspondences becomes difficult when the common
area is small, as there is insufficient spatial context to disam-
biguate similar structures. Despite improved feature quality,
methods relying on local geometric features alone struggle

Global context modeling with transformers. Transform-
ers addressed the locality limitation by enabling global con-
text modeling through self-attention mechanisms [17, 40,
49]. GeoTransformer [26] pioneered this direction with ge-
ometric transformers and superpoint matching. Subsequent
work explored end-to-end correspondence prediction [48],
hierarchical modeling, diffusion-based approaches, multi-
modal fusion, and various architectural improvements [2, 8,
17, 22, 32–34, 39, 51, 56]. By incorporating global geo-
metric relationships, these methods improved performance
in low-overlap scenarios where spatial context is limited.
However, we observe that purely geometric features strug-
gle to disambiguate structures with similar geometric pat-
terns but different semantic meanings.
Representation learning beyond geometry. Recent ad-
vances in 3D representation learning [41, 42, 44] have
demonstrated the potential to learn semantic representations
beyond pure geometry, offering a promising direction for
addressing geometric ambiguity. Methods such as Point-
BERT [53], masked autoencoders, and auto-regressive
methods [7, 23] capture semantic patterns through pre-
training with masked point modeling. Notably, Sonata [43]
learns robust semantic representations by explicitly avoid-
ing geometric shortcuts during training using a large
dataset. This makes it possible to semantically distinguish
between objects that have similar geometric characteristics,
such as walls and floors.
Semantic-enhanced registration. Several methods lever-
age semantic information for registration. S2Reg [45] ac-
quires semantics via pre-trained 3D models or 2D-3D pro-
jection from images, while ML-SemReg [46] relies on se-
mantic labels produced by external semantic segmentation
models. ColorPCR [22], SemReg [15], and SARNet [25]
employ multi-modal fusion or semantic constraints. How-
ever, these approaches either require additional modalities
such as RGB-D data or calibrated images, or rely on ex-
plicit semantic labels produced by task-specific segmenta-
tion models. While these methods demonstrate the value
of semantic information, directly integrating frozen pre-
trained semantic representations into point cloud registra-
tion without additional sensors or retraining remains un-
explored. To address this, we propose an architecture that
leverages frozen semantic representations solely from point
clouds.
Mixture of experts. Mixture of Experts (MoE) offers an
architectural approach to handle diverse input patterns effi-
ciently. Specifically, this approach has a set of small mod-
ules, called experts, and each expert specializes in differ-
ent input patterns [13, 31, 55]. PSReg [19] pioneered the
application of MoE to registration. The method leverages
GeoTransformer [26] to determine which regions overlap
between point clouds, and uses this overlap information
for routing inputs to experts.
By enabling different ex-
perts to specialize in different geometric patterns (e.g., pla-
nar vs.
curved surfaces, indoor vs.
outdoor structures),

pre-trained semantic representations into MoE routing, en-

As shown in Fig. 2, our proposed pipeline is based on
GeoTransformer [26] but incorporates a newly developed
semantic representation extractor and an SG-MoE Trans-

representation extraction using a frozen Sonata encoder in
Sec. 3.2, (2) geometric feature extraction with semantic rep-

aggregation of semantic representations to the superpoint
level in Sec. 3.3, (4) alternating geometric self-attention
and semantic-guided MoE cross-attention for N iterations
in Sec. 3.4, and (5) correspondence establishment and reg-
istration [26].

3.4. Semantic-Guided Mixture of Experts
After the above aggregation, we perform the transformer-

geometry-only routing cannot distinguish regions with sim-

4. Experiments
4.1. Implementation Details
Network architecture. We use N = 3 transformer block
iterations. The number of experts is set to Nexpert = 4.
For the pair-wise distance embedding in geometric self-
attention, we set σd to the voxel size at the superpoint level,
and σa = 15◦for the triplet-wise angular embedding. For
local-to-global registration, only superpoint matches with
at least 3 local point correspondences are used to compute
transformation candidates.
We iteratively recompute the
transformation with the surviving inlier matches Nr = 5
times.
Training and testing. We implement and train SAGE with
PyTorch on an NVIDIA H100 GPU. Models are trained
with the Adam optimizer for 45 epochs on 3DMatch with
a batch size of 1. The learning rate starts from 10−4 and
is multiplied by 0.95 after each epoch. Loss weights are
λcoarse = 1.0, λfine = 1.0 and λmoe = 0.01. We randomly
sample Ng = 128 ground-truth superpoint correspondences
during training, and Nc = 256 putative superpoint matches
during testing. Data augmentation follows [17].
Baselines and reproduction. In the experiments, we evalu-
ate two approaches: a single-pass approach and an iterative

4.2. Evaluation on 3DMatch and 3DLoMatch
We evaluate SAGE on the 3DMatch [54] and 3DLo-
Match [17] datasets to assess its performance in point cloud
registration. We evaluate performance using three metrics
following [17, 26]: Inlier Ratio (IR), which measures the
fraction of putative correspondences whose residuals are
below 0.1 m under the ground-truth transformation; Feature
Matching Recall (FMR), which calculates the percentage of
point cloud pairs with IR exceeding 5%; and Registration
Recall (RR), which determines the fraction of pairs whose
transformation error (RMSE) is below 0.2 m4.
Results with RANSAC-50K. Following standard proto-
cols [18], we compare recent point cloud registration
methods with our SAGE. We evaluate each method us-
ing RANSAC-50K with variable sample counts.

3DLoMatch benchmarks.

SAGE: Semantic-Augmented Geometric Experts for Point Cloud Registration
Supplementary Material
A. Network Architecture Details
A.1. Semantic Representation Extractor (Sonata)
SAGE employs Sonata [43] as the semantic representation
extractor. Sonata is a point cloud foundation model that
is pre-trained through a contrastive learning objective de-
signed to align point cloud representations with semanti-
cally meaningful structures while explicitly avoiding geo-
metric shortcuts. Its encoder follows a sparse convolutional
architecture that processes voxelized point clouds and pro-
duces per-point feature vectors of 1024 dimensions.
An important property of Sonata for our framework is
that it outputs continuous, high-dimensional semantic rep-
resentation vectors rather than discrete categorical labels.
Unlike conventional semantic segmentation models that
predict specific categories such as “chair” or “car,” Sonata
captures rich semantic relationships in a continuous feature
space where semantically similar structures are grouped to-
gether without being constrained by a predefined label vo-
cabulary. This characteristic is essential for SAGE because
it allows the SG-MoE router to exploit fine-grained se-
mantic distinctions that go beyond coarse category bound-
aries. For both indoor and outdoor environments, we use
the frozen Sonata model exclusively as a feature extrac-
tor, which ensures domain-agnostic operation that remains
entirely free from the constraints of dataset-specific label
spaces.
A.2. Geometric Feature Extractor (KPConv) for
3DMatch and 3DLoMatch
Following GeoTransformer [26], we use a KPConv-FPN
backbone for feature extraction. Prior to feeding the data
into the backbone network, the input point clouds are pro-
cessed with an initial voxelization using a sampling resolu-
tion of 2.5 cm. As the data progresses through successive
downsampling stages, the voxel resolution is progressively
coarsened by a factor of two at each level.
As shown in Table A, SAGE adopts the same KPConv-
FPN backbone as GeoTransformer [26], with the only mod-
ification being the input dimension of the first KPConv
layer. Specifically, we expand the input feature dimension
from 1 (no input features) to 1088 to incorporate pre-trained
semantic representations from Sonata [43]. This enables the
backbone to learn semantic-geometric features from the ini-
tial stage.

A.3. Geometric Feature Extractor (KPConv) for
KITTI
For the KITTI odometry benchmark, we adapt the backbone
architecture to handle larger-scale outdoor environments.
The input point clouds are voxelized with a sampling res-
olution of 30 cm, significantly coarser than the 2.5 cm used
for 3DMatch, reflecting the difference in spatial scale be-
tween indoor rooms and outdoor driving scenarios.
As shown in Table B, we employ a deeper 5-stage back-
bone compared to the 4-stage backbone used for 3DMatch.
The additional downsampling stage (Stage 5) is necessary
to capture long-range spatial relationships in outdoor scenes
that can span over 100 meters, while maintaining computa-
tional efficiency. Without this additional stage, the recep-
tive field would be insufficient to establish correspondences

As shown in Table E, SAGE demonstrates a clear and

D.4. Computational Efficiency
Table F shows that SAGE requires approximately 12.4×
more model parameters and 2.4× more memory than Geo-
Transformer, due to introducing Sonata and MoE. While

SAGE has 121.71M total parameters, which is approx-

However, the majority of this increase originates from the
frozen Sonata encoder (108.41M parameters), which re-
quires no gradient computation during training or inference.
Excluding Sonata, SAGE contains only 13.3M parameters;
the SG-MoE routing networks and expert FFNs add approx-
imately 3.5M parameters beyond the GeoTransformer back-
bone, representing only a 35% increase.
Regarding inference time, SAGE requires 179ms per
point cloud pair, comparable to RoITr (140ms). In terms
of performance gain per unit of additional latency over
GeoTransformer, SAGE achieves 2.3 percentage points
of RR improvement for 104ms of additional latency,
whereas RoITr adds 65ms without improving RR (73.4%
vs. 74.0%), indicating that SAGE utilizes the additional
computation more effectively.
It is worth noting that this computational overhead is not
specific to SAGE, but rather reflects a fundamental cost of
incorporating pre-trained semantic encoders into the regis-
tration pipeline. SAGE mitigates this inherent cost by freez-
ing the encoder entirely and keeping the method-specific
trainable overhead minimal. The peak memory consump-
tion of 16.69 GB fits within commonly available GPUs such
as the NVIDIA RTX 4090 (24 GB), making SAGE prac-
tical for offline applications that prioritize accuracy, such
as 3D reconstruction for cultural heritage preservation and
architectural surveying. As more lightweight point cloud
foundation models emerge, the frozen encoder could be re-
placed with a more compact alternative without modifying
the SG-MoE architecture itself.

are more directly useful for minimizing registration errors.
This geometric shortcut phenomenon causes the semantic
representations to lose their ability to distinguish between
geometrically similar but semantically different structures
(e.g., TV corners vs. desk corners), which is precisely the
discriminative power that Sonata was pre-trained to pre-

The degradation in FMR (89.4% →86.4%) and IR
(44.3% →42.6%) further confirms that the adapted fea-

spondences in low-overlap scenarios where semantic un-
derstanding is crucial. This validates our design choice of
keeping Sonata completely frozen throughout training, en-
suring that the semantic representations maintain their orig-
inal semantic discriminability and do not degenerate into
redundant geometric features.

Number of Experts. We vary the number of experts Nexpert
from 2 to 6 to study its impact on performance. As shown
in Table G(bottom), performance generally improves as we
increase the number of experts from 2 to 4, with the best
results at Nexpert = 4 (76.3% RR). Beyond 4 experts, per-
formance degrades (75.5% RR at Nexpert = 5, 75.7% RR at
Nexpert = 6), likely due to over-specialization and insuffi-
cient training samples per expert.
With only 2 experts (74.9% RR), the model lacks suf-
ficient capacity to capture diverse semantic-geometric pat-
terns. Three experts (75.1% RR) provide some improve-
ment but still underperform the optimal configuration. The
optimal choice of Nexpert = 4 strikes a balance between
model capacity and training efficiency, allowing each ex-
pert to specialize in distinct semantic categories (e.g., pla-
nar structures like walls and floors, furniture, fixtures, and
small objects) without over-fragmenting the feature space.
Dependency on Semantic Quality. We evaluated SAGE’s
robustness by injecting three types of perturbations into se-
mantic features on 3DLoMatch.
As shown in Table H, SAGE maintains stable perfor-
mance (RR >75%) under 50% noise or dropout. This ro-
bustness stems from the implicit regularization within our
SG-MoE; through training, the router’s MLP learns to by-
pass feature noise and prioritize stable semantic cues.
Specifically, while Gaussian noise and feature dropout
represent unstructured degradations that the router can learn
to filter as out-of-distribution noise, random replacement
introduces structured, semantically plausible but incorrect
features. These “adversarial” semantic cues can mislead
the router into assigning superpoints to experts specialized
in irrelevant semantic contexts, providing them with a high
but erroneous confidence. This structural confusion disrupts
the correspondence search more severely than simple noise,
as it actively guides the geometric refinement process to-
ward semantically inconsistent regions, resulting in the ob-
served gradual performance decline as the replacement ratio
increases.

E. Baseline Reproduction Details
To make our comparison transparent and reproducible, this
section describes how we obtain the baseline results in the
main paper. All methods compared under the matched pro-
tocol (PEAL [51], PSReg [19], and SAGE) are trained from
scratch on the same NVIDIA H100 GPU and share the same
training configuration as SAGE (Sec. B.4): the Adam opti-
mizer with an initial learning rate of 10−4 multiplied by
0.95 after each epoch, weight decay 10−6, batch size 1, and
random seed 7351. They also share the KPConv-FPN back-
bone (2.5 cm initial voxel size, four stages), 128 coarse tar-
gets, 256 putative superpoint correspondences, and the ge-
ometric attention configuration (4 heads, σd = 0.2, σa =
15◦). Keeping these factors fixed isolates the effect of the
routing design rather than confounding it with training or
backbone differences.
PEAL. The PEAL [51] code is publicly released, so we
build our PEAL baseline directly on the official repository.
PEAL conditions its attention on an overlap prior. Although
the prior data is released, the procedure used to generate it
is not documented, which prevents us from reproducing it
exactly. We therefore regenerate the overlap prior from the
publicly released GeoTransformer [26] pre-trained model
and train PEAL in our own environment under the shared
configuration above. We use a budget of six refinement it-
erations, matching the iterative protocol used for SAGE and
PSReg.
PSReg. The official implementation of PSReg [19] is not
publicly released, and several key hyperparameters, most
notably the number of experts, are not reported in the paper.
We therefore re-implement PSReg on the same framework
as SAGE and train it from scratch. Following the design
described in the PSReg paper, expert routing is driven by
geometry-derived overlap information obtained from Geo-
Transformer [26], with top-1 selection. To keep the compar-
ison controlled, we set the number of experts to 4, identical
to SAGE; all remaining settings follow the shared configu-
ration above.
On the reproduced numbers. Our reproduced results for
PEAL and PSReg fall below the values reported in their
original papers. We attribute this to the absence of released

F. Limitations
While SAGE demonstrates significant improvements in
low-overlap point cloud registration, several limitations
warrant discussion for future work.
Dependency on Pre-trained Semantic Models.
SAGE
relies on the quality and generalization capability of the
frozen Sonata encoder. The semantic degradation experi-
ments in Table H quantify this dependency: while SAGE
is robust to unstructured noise (76.2% RR even at 50%
Gaussian noise), random replacement of semantic features
with plausible but incorrect ones causes a more pronounced
decline to 73.5% RR at 50% replacement. This indicates
that the SG-MoE router is sensitive to structured seman-
tic errors that mimic valid features, which could arise in
practice when the encoder encounters object categories or
scene types underrepresented in its training data (e.g., in-
dustrial equipment, medical instruments). In such domains,
Sonata may produce semantically ambiguous representa-
tions that mislead the router into suboptimal expert assign-
ments. As more diverse point cloud foundation models be-
come available, replacing or complementing Sonata with
encoders trained on broader data distributions could miti-
gate this limitation.
Limited Effectiveness in Semantically Homogeneous
Scenes. The scene-wise analysis in Table D reveals that
SAGE’s improvement over baselines is directly tied to the
semantic diversity of each scene. In the MIT Lab scene,
which consists of narrow corridors with repetitive walls
and floors, SAGE (81.2%) slightly underperforms Geo-
Transformer (83.9%), and in Hotel 3, all three methods
achieve similar results (85.4–86.4%).
The overlap-wise
analysis in Table E further corroborates this trend: at 50–
60% overlap, where geometric context is already suffi-
cient, SAGE (98.4%) is marginally below GeoTransformer
(98.7%). These results indicate that when overlapping re-
gions are dominated by uniform planar structures that lack
semantic variation, the SG-MoE router cannot assign su-
perpoints to meaningfully different experts, and the addi-
tional routing overhead provides no benefit. Fig. C illus-
trates concrete failure cases where this limitation manifests:
severe occlusion leaves only featureless walls and floors in
the overlapping area, and the truncation of semantic objects
(e.g., only a partial view of a desk) prevents the frozen en-
coder from extracting discriminative representations. We
note that these scenarios are inherently challenging for all
correspondence-based registration methods, as the lack of
distinctive features is a fundamental obstacle that is not spe-
cific to semantic approaches. Addressing robust registra-
tion in such low-texture, semantically homogeneous envi-
ronments through temporal cues from sequential scans or
multi-view constraints remains an important open problem
for the community.
Despite these limitations, SAGE establishes a strong
foundation for incorporating semantic understanding into
point cloud registration, and the analyses presented in this
work clarify the conditions under which semantic guidance
is most and least effective, providing concrete guidance for
future research in semantic-geometric fusion for 3D vision
tasks.
