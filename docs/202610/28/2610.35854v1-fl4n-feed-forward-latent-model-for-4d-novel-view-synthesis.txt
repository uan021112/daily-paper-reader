ABSTRACT
4D novel view synthesis (NVS) aims to render dynamic scenes across viewpoints
and timestamps, which requires jointly inferring 3D structure and temporal dy-
namics. Existing monocular feed-forward methods often rely on explicit 4D Gaus-
sian representations, which are prone to temporal inconsistency and severe ren-
dering artifacts. We present FL4N, the first feed-forward latent 4D NVS model.
Instead of relying on explicit 4D primitives, FL4N encodes a 4D scene prior into
a unified latent representation across space and time. Our core insight is that
learning temporal dynamics as residuals on top of a pretrained static latent prior
enables effective 4D modeling with limited dynamic training data. To this end,
we introduce a lightweight dynamic encoder that leverages static scene features to
disentangle object motion from camera-induced parallax. Trained solely with an
image synthesis objective, FL4N achieves state-of-the-art 4D NVS quality while
rendering in real time. Its learned latent representation generalizes to real-world
videos and disentangles viewpoint from time.

INTRODUCTION
4D novel view synthesis (NVS) is a cornerstone of understanding and simulating our ever-changing
physical world, enabling broad applications, e. g., immersive graphics (Li et al., 2025), world mod-
els (Zhu et al., 2025), robotics (Kim et al., 2026b), autonomous driving (Song et al., 2025), and
4D-aware vision-language models (Cho et al., 2026). Unlike static NVS (Hong et al., 2025; Jin
et al., 2025), 4D NVS requires capturing both persistent 3D structure and temporal dynamics. While

slow optimization-based methods (Pumarola et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2025b) and multi-view meth-
ods (Balice et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2024b) are impractical in the wild, monocular feed-forward
approaches offer a practical, real-time solution by inferring a 4D scene from a casual video.
However, monocular feed-forward 4D NVS is highly ill-posed, as it must jointly infer entangled 3D
structure and object motion from monocular observations alone (Wang et al., 2025b). This requires
the model to learn a strong 4D prior. To make this tractable, existing methods (Lin et al., 2026a; Xu
et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026) rely on the explicit 3D inductive bias of 3D Gaussians (Kerbl et al.,
2023) with added 3D motion and dense geometry and motion supervision. However, this reliance
on explicit 3D primitives often bottlenecks 4D rendering quality. Inaccurate prediction of Gaussian
attributes across timestamps often yields rendering artifacts, e. g., temporal ghosting, floaters, and
holes (An et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026c), as in Fig. 1. Moreover, time-varying effects (e. g., shadows
and illumination changes) must be encoded in per-primitive attributes (Li et al., 2024), and regions
with insufficient primitive coverage tend to be over-smoothed (Kim et al., 2026c; Li et al., 2024).
In recent static NVS literature, latent-based methods (Jin et al., 2025; Mitchel et al., 2025; Szy-
manowicz et al., 2026) avoid these limitations by learning view synthesis end-to-end with trans-
formers, mapping input views directly to target views conditioned on a target camera, without an
explicit 3D representation. With minimal 3D inductive biases and large-scale training, these latent
models outperform 3D-Gaussian-based methods in visual fidelity (Jin et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026a).
However, directly extending the latent approach to dynamic scenes faces a fundamental challenge.
Without explicit 3D priors, learning to disentangle 3D structure and temporal dynamics purely from
data would require massive multi-view dynamic scene datasets (Bai et al., 2025; Van Hoorick et al.,
2024), which are much scarcer than static datasets (Watson et al., 2025).
To resolve this challenge, we propose FL4N, a Feed-forward Latent-based 4D NVS model, which
augments a frozen pretrained static latent NVS model with a trainable dynamic encoder. Our key
insight is that a 4D latent model does not need to learn dynamic scene representation from scratch:
pretrained static latent NVS models already possess a strong, generalizable 3D prior, allowing the
dynamic encoder to exclusively focus on modeling temporal changes (e. g., object motion or ap-
pearance variation). Consequently, our method can be trained with a small amount of dynamic data
while fully inheriting the pretrained model’s ability to synthesize coherent novel views.
Concretely, we attach the lightweight trainable dynamic encoder alongside the frozen static encoder,
similar to ControlNet (Zhang et al., 2023). The dynamic encoder takes the same input views with a
learnable per-view time token and cross-attends to frozen static encoder features to isolate true scene
dynamics from camera-induced motion. A lightweight time module then modulates the residual
latent for the queried timestamp, which is residually added to the static latent (He et al., 2016) and
decoded by the frozen renderer at the target camera pose. Multi-view videos naturally disentangle
viewpoint and temporal variation through same-time views and fixed views across time. Sampling
unseen view–time pairs, FL4N learns this disentanglement through image synthesis with binary
dynamic mask supervision, without dense geometry or motion supervision.
We evaluate our approach on synthetic and real datasets under a systematic protocol. To effectively
demonstrate the performance of 4D novel view synthesis and the capability of view-time disentan-
glement, we separate the setting with novel view synthesis, cross-time view synthesis, and novel
time synthesis. FL4N achieves state-of-the-art or competitive performance across datasets in the
pose-free setting without test-time pose optimization, while optionally incorporating context cam-
era poses further enables state-of-the-art performance across all three settings.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• First 4D latent NVS model. We present to jointly model 3D structure and temporal variation in
a latent space, rendering novel views at novel timestamps without explicit 3D representations or
target-view/time observations.
• A residual formulation for efficient dynamic adaptation. We formulate 4D NVS as learning
a temporal residual on a frozen static representation. This leverages the pretrained scene and
viewpoint priors while concentrating learning on temporal variation through a lightweight branch.
• State-of-the-art performance. We comprehensively benchmark 4D NVS across novel view,
cross-time, and novel time synthesis on eight synthetic and real-world benchmarks, achieving
state-of-the-art rendering quality on all three settings, with the fastest runtime.

RELATED WORK
Feed-forward Gaussian splatting for 4D novel view synthesis. Explicit feed-forward 4D meth-
ods typically represent scenes with 3D Gaussians extended with temporal dynamics, such as ve-
locity (Xu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026) or deformation fields (Lin et al., 2026a;b). However, in-
dependently modeling motion per Gaussian often disrupts object rigidity and temporal consistency
due to incoherent motion among nearby Gaussians (Kim et al., 2026c), yielding artifacts such as
temporal ghosting. Anchoring Gaussian prediction to each target timestamp (Liang et al., 2025) can
sidestep the issue but is susceptible to view bias by primarily relying on nearby frames while under-
utilizing observation from distant frames. Query-based Gaussian representation (Kim et al., 2026c)
alleviates this via dense motion supervision, yet its limited number of queries constrains render-
ing quality due to insufficient Gaussian coverage, particularly for large outdoor scenes. Multi-view
approaches (Balice et al., 2026) can exploit observations from multiple time-synchronized static
cameras, but the setup is far more restrictive than a monocular moving camera. We introduce a
feed-forward latent-based method with minimal geometric inductive bias in a practical but more
challenging monocular camera setup.
Latent-based novel view synthesis. Latent-based NVS models synthesize novel views with trans-
formers conditioned on the target pose (Jiang et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026a; Sajjadi
et al., 2022; Szymanowicz et al., 2026) or latent pose variable (Mitchel et al., 2025), bypassing ex-
plicit 3D representations. Although RayDer (Prestel et al., 2026) trains on dynamic videos, it still
targets static NVS while suppressing object motion via per-view nuisance token that is dropped at
inference. Despite their strong generalization, these methods either assume static scenes or treat
dynamic content as variation to be ignored rather than modeled. Toward dynamic latent representa-
tions, DyST (Seitzer et al., 2024) extends RUST (Sajjadi et al., 2023) with a dynamics estimator, yet
keeps its scene latent time-invariant. Temporal variation is decoded via control codes extracted from
target-pose and target-time frames, which limits explicit view and time control and risks appearance
leakage from these frames. In contrast, our model constructs a time-conditioned scene latent that is
rendered from arbitrary target cameras, enabling synthesis at novel views and novel timestamps.
Generative novel view synthesis. NVS becomes inherently ambiguous when target views extend
into regions unseen in the input views. Deterministic methods maintain strong cross-view consis-
tency on observed content but tend to blur unobserved regions (Prestel et al., 2026; Szymanow-
icz et al., 2026). Generative approaches (Bai et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026; Ren et al., 2025;
Van Hoorick et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025) can extrapolate plausible content beyond the observed
regions, yet their stochastic sampling often compromises cross-view consistency and scene faithful-
ness (Jin et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026c). Although generative refinement can post-process rendered
views from Gaussian representations (Kim et al., 2026c; Yang et al., 2026) or serve as a diffusion
decoder for latent-based model (Szymanowicz et al., 2026), our work focuses on deterministic NVS
and remains orthogonal to such generative refinement directions.

METHOD
We propose FL4N, a Feed-forward Latent-based 4D NVS model. Given an N-frame monocular
video {Ik}N
k=1, our method synthesizes views from an arbitrary query view πtgt at target timestamp
ttgt drawn from the observed time range. Figure 2 shows an overview of FL4N. We formulate 4D
synthesis as a residual adaptation on top of a frozen pretrained static NVS model. Given the static
encoder and the renderer from the pretrained model, we attach a lightweight dynamic encoder that
focuses its capacity on isolating dynamic object motion.
In the following, we first review the latent static NVS baseline that forms a basis of our method
(§ 3.1). We then introduce FL4N, describing the technical details on the dynamic encoder with
target-time residual adaptation (§ 3.2). Finally, we detail the training strategy (§ 3.3) and conclude
by analyzing the learned residual features (§ 3.4).

Dynamic encoder. To learn to disentangle dynamic motion from static scene structure in monocu-
lar videos, we freeze the pretrained static encoder Est and renderer R, and introduce a lightweight
dynamic encoder Edyn. The static encoder Est already captures time-invariant content and camera-
induced viewpoint changes from its static-scene training. During our dynamic-scene training, the
frozen static encoder and renderer allow the dynamic encoder Edyn to learn temporal variations as
residuals to the pretrained static representation. Freezing the renderer R fixes latent-to-image map-
ping, forcing the residual feature to the pretrained latent space. This encourages them to complement
the static representation rather than learn a new feature space from limited dynamic data.
To provide explicit temporal cues to the dynamic encoder Edyn, we introduce a learnable time token
τk ∈RC for each context frame Ik, which is concatenated with the patch tokens xk = Pdyn(Ik)
before being fed to the dynamic encoder Edyn. Through this encoding, each time token encodes a
timestamp-related information from each frame.

ANALYSIS ON RESIDUAL LATENT
We conduct analyses to investigate what information is captured in the residual latent zres. Additional
analysis on static and residual latent is provided in Appendix C.
Residual complements static rendering. The frozen static model can answer where to render,
but not when. Having no target-time input, the static latent zst compresses the video sequence into
a single time-invariant prediction; moving content is thus discarded or frozen at observed frames
with the input pose entangled, as in Fig. 3. Our residual latent zres supplies this missing temporal
axis. Conditioned on the target time, it complements the static latent with time-dependent variation,
placing the dynamic object at its target-time position while preserving the static background.

latent zres using PCA visualization. While the static latent exhibits camera-ray-like patterns, the

EXPERIMENTS
Implementation details. Our model builds on a frozen static novel view synthesizer (Szymanowicz
et al., 2026), with only the dynamic encoder, time module, and mask head trained. The dynamic
encoder follows the architecture of the pretrained static encoder, with added cross-attention module
for each layer but uses one-third of its depth, with 8 transformer layers compared to 24 in the static
encoder, since temporal variation accounts for only a relatively small portion of the scene. Further
architectural and training details are provided in Appendix A.
Training dataset. To effectively disentangle camera and object motion, we train our model using
synchronized multi-view video datasets such as Kubric4D (Van Hoorick et al., 2024) and Camx-
Time (Huang et al., 2026), captured by V cameras with fixed poses with T frames. Unlike costly,
explicit 3D annotation (e. g., dense depth or 3D motion) that is commonly used for 3D-Gaussian-
based approaches, the multi-view datasets provide cost-effective indirect 4D supervision from 2D
images. Furthermore, our residual formulation specifically makes this RGB-only supervision di-
rectly tractable, with requiring relatively small amount as well.

5
CONCLUSION
In this work, we introduced FL4N, a feed-forward latent model that renders dynamic scenes from
a monocular video at novel views and timestamps. By learning a temporal residual on top of a
frozen static latent model, FL4N acquires a 4D representation from limited dynamic data, super-
vised only by images and binary dynamic masks. Across eight benchmarks and three evaluation
protocols, FL4N outperforms prior feed-forward Gaussian-based methods and generalizes to real-
world videos, while its latent space successfully disentangles view from time and preserves scene
geometry. Our work demonstrates the potential of latent representations for feed-forward 4D novel
view synthesis, opening new directions for latent-based 4D scene understanding and modeling more
complex dynamics.

FL4N: Feed-forward Latent Model for 4D Novel View Synthesis
A
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
A.1
ARCHITECTURE DETAILS
Static encoder and renderer. The static prior used is a pretrained feed-forward novel view synthe-
sizer whose encoder is a 24-layer transformer alternating frame-wise and global attention, followed
by a 12-layer transformer renderer. Both use a hidden size of 768 with 12 heads on 8 × 8 patches,
and remain frozen during our training.
Dynamic encoder. The dynamic encoder adopts the same core architecture as the static encoder,
augmented with cross-attention, but uses one-third of its depth, with 8 layers. It is initialized with
weights uniformly sampled from the pretrained static encoder. Each layer additionally cross-attends
to the last layer of static features, allowing the residual branch to access camera-pose-aware rep-
resentations throughout encoding. This provides a spatial anchor for learning motion residuals,
complementing the static features rather than relearning scene structure. A DPT decoder (Ranftl
et al., 2021) attached to the dynamic encoder predicts the binary dynamic mask.
Temporal conditioning. We use a single learnable time token τ ∈RC with C = 1024, and attach a
copy of it to each input view by concatenating it with that view’s tokens. Each copy becomes view-
specific (ˆτk) through attention with its own view, allowing the same token to handle an arbitrary
number and ordering of context frames. The resulting time representation is processed by a time
module consisting of two transformer blocks with adaptive layer normalization conditioned on the
target-time token, followed by a zero-initialized linear residual gate.
A.2
TRAINING DETAILS
Training setup. We train with AdamW at a learning rate of 3.5 × 10−5, weight decay 0.05, and
3k warmup steps, while keeping the static encoder and renderer frozen for whole training. FL4N
is trained on four NVIDIA RTX 5880 Ada Generation GPUs with a batch size of 8 for total of 32k
steps. Each training sample contains 8 conditioning views and 12 target views at a resolution of
336 × 512.
Training datasets. We use Kubric4D (Van Hoorick et al., 2024) and CamxTime (Huang et al.,
2026), which provide synchronized multi-view videos captured by static cameras. To disentangle
camera and object motion, we train the model to reconstruct target frames at view–time pairs not
included in the input set.
Pose dropout. To support pose-free conditioning at inference, we randomly zero out the condi-
tioning camera tokens with probability 0.4 during training. At inference time, FL4N supports fully
unposed setting by zeroing all conditioning camera tokens.
Loss. We use an ℓ2 photometric loss L2, LPIPS losses Lperc and Ldyn
perc, and an ℓ1 image-gradient
loss Ldyn
grad. The dynamic-region losses are computed using the dynamic mask Mk to select moving
pixels, on 128 × 128 crops centered on the moving content. For the image loss term, the dynamic
and static regions are averaged over their respective areas and weighted equally. Dynamic mask loss
is only comptued for context frames. We set λperc = 0.5, λdyn
perc = 2.0, λdyn
grad = 1.0, and λmask = 1.0.
Pseudo dynamic mask generation. We generate pseudo dynamic masks by thresholding the mag-
nitude of optical flow estimated by an off-the-shelf flow estimator (Wang & Deng, 2026). Since the
flow is computed between frames captured by the same static camera, camera-induced motion and
parallax are absent, making the remaining flow primarily reflect object motion. This leverages the
static-camera multi-view configuration of our training data to identify dynamic regions with binary
pseudo labels. The mask generation can also be easily replaced with off-the-shelf segmentation
models, such as SAM3 (Carion et al., 2025), without changing the training framework.

EVALUATION PROTOCOL
B.1
EVALUATION DATASETS
Our benchmark comprises five multi-view datasets for NVS and CTVS: NVIDIA, N3DV, Kubric4D,
CamxTime, and Charge, and three datasets for NTS: ADT, DyCheck, and TUM. Kubric4D and
CamxTime use held-out scenes, while the remaining datasets are unseen during training and evaluate
cross-dataset generalization.
For static camera multi-view datasets, we simulate moving camera monocular video by selecting N
diagonal frames, i. e. camera i observed at time i as input.
Kubric4D. We use 100 test scenes from Kubric4D, which contains 16 static cameras, with the
first four providing top-down views and the remaining 12 cameras arranged around the scene on a
horizontal ring. Each camera captures 60 frames. We exclude the first four cameras and sample
cameras consecutively from the horizontal ring. We use a frame interval of 8.
CamxTime. CamxTime contains four separate camera trajectories, each comprising 120 densely
spaced cameras with 120 frames per camera. We hold out the last 10% of scenes for testing and use
Trajectory 01 for evaluation, with camera and frame intervals both set to 8.
Charge. we use the Sparse Test cameras. Charge stores cameras in a non-spatial order, so
sampling by file index leaves large gaps between consecutive conditioning views. We therefore
reorder the cameras of each scene along a continuous open path through the rig and uniformly
sample along this path to approximate a smooth monocular camera trajectory. we use the following
camera order:

B.2
EVALUATION PROTOCOLS
NVS (Novel View Synthesis). We take 9 frames and hold out the 5th as a target. The remaining 8
are used as the input, and the held-out frame’s viewpoint is the target at all 8 conditioning times. For
N3DV, cam0 is used for a target camera.
CTVS (Cross-time View Synthesis). Given N diagonal frames as input, the targets are the N 2−N
off-diagonal cells of the camera–time grid, evaluating synthesis at unseen viewpoint–timestamp
combinations. NVIDIA uses all available cameras (N=12), and N3DV uses all cameras except
cam0 (N=17–20 depending on the scene). Kubric4D, CamxTime, and Charge each use N=8.
NTS (Novel Time Synthesis). Following C4G (Kim et al., 2026c), we use the following scenes
from each dataset:
• DyCheck
– apple, block, paper-windmill, spin, teddy
• ADT
– Apartment release multiuser cook seq141 M1292
– Apartment release multiskeleton party seq114 M1292
– Apartment release meal skeleton seq135 M1292
– Apartment release work skeleton seq137 M1292
• TUM
– fr2 desk with person
– fr3 sitting {halfsphere, rpy, static, xyz}
– fr3 walking {halfsphere, rpy, static, xyz}
All three datasets use a single moving camera for both context and target views. We follow the
C4G (Kim et al., 2026c) evaluation protocol and sample 16 frames with stride 6 and evaluate on the
15 interleaved frames.

D.6
ARCHITECTURAL ABLATION
Table C extends Table 4 to more variants and temporal strides {1, 2, 4, 8}. We first compare against
the static NVS backbone and its fine-tuned variant. While both provide a strong static-scene prior,
fine-tuning alone does not effectively capture scene dynamics.
We further ablate architectural
choices. Unfreezing the renderer degrades performance, suggesting that preserving the pretrained
latent-to-image mapping is beneficial. The single-branch encoder, removal of the dynamic mask
loss, and sinusoidal time embeddings all underperform our full model. Removing static feature an-
choring also reduces performance overall, highlighting the benefit of conditioning dynamic features
on the static representation. Finally, we vary the dynamic encoder depth and initialization. Our
8-layer encoder achieves the best overall performance among the tested configurations. Random
initialization also yields competitive results, although initializing from the static encoder weights
consistently improves performance across temporal strides.

Consistent with the depth probing results, this widening gap indicates that the residual captures
temporal information that is particularly useful under larger object displacement.

TEST-TIME POSE ALIGNMENT FOR LATENT MODELS
Evaluating models that do not receive conditioning poses can introduce scale misalignment with the
ground truth, resulting in poor rendering metrics even when the predicted scene is geometrically
accurate. Since the input images do not determine an absolute scale, a pose-free model may recon-
struct the scene at an arbitrary scale. Pose-free methods therefore perform test-time pose alignment
before evaluation and report rendering metrics using the aligned poses (Kim et al., 2026c; Ye et al.,
2025). This alignment only brings the prediction into the coordinate frame and scale of the ground
truth without modifying the underlying scene content.

The pose-free Gaussian baselines in our quantitative comparisons are evaluated with 2,000 step pose-
alignment steps, whereas FL4N is reported without pose alignment in the main tables. However,
FL4N is also pose-free and therefore suffers from the same scale ambiguity that motivates this
evaluation procedure. We thus additionally evaluate FL4N with the same 2,000 step pose alignment
to provide a consistent comparison.
For Gaussian models, Gaussians are predicted once from the conditioning images and kept fixed
throughout the alignment process. Differentiable rasterization allows the rendering loss to be back-
propagated to the camera pose, so each alignment step re-rasterizes the same frozen Gaussians under
an updated pose, without modifying the network or scene representation.
The analogous procedure can be done to our latent renderer. The scene latent is predicted solely
from the conditioning images and is independent of the rendering process. We therefore compute
the latent once and keep it fixed throughout the alignment process, with the renderer serving the role
of rasterization by re-rendering the cached latent under an updated target pose at each step. For both
Gaussian and latent models, we optimize a rotation and translation offset for each target pose (Ye
et al., 2025), initialized at zero such that the initial rendering exactly matches the feed-forward
prediction. We use the same objective as the Gaussian baselines, with 2,000 Adam steps using pixel
and perceptual losses. Table G reports the effect of this alignment under the NTS protocol on ADT
and TUM. Alignment is worth 5.94 dB to C4G on ADT and 5.13 dB on TUM, against 3.59 dB and
2.55 dB for our model, so a large part of the published margin of C4G on these cells comes from
fitting the pose to the image it is then scored on. How much alignment recovers therefore measures
how much error the supplied cameras carry rather than the quality of the reconstruction, and our
model without any alignment remains ahead of both aligned baselines on every metric.
G
LIMITATIONS
Our approach inherits the limitations of the pretrained static latent NVS backbone, particularly when
rendering from extrapolated viewpoints that are unseen in the input views. Latent NVS models can-
not synthesize unseen content (Jin et al., 2025), resulting in blurry or collapsed renderings under
extrapolation, as also observed in (Szymanowicz et al., 2026). In contrast, explicit Gaussian rep-
resentations construct scene content in 3D space, allowing the observed portions of the scene to
remain usable even when extrapolation leaves holes in unseen regions.
The residual formulation can compensate for certain errors in the static prediction, but its capacity is
naturally limited when the underlying scene representation or camera pose is substantially incorrect.
These limitations can be alleviated by using stronger static NVS models with broader scene coverage
and more robust viewpoint understanding.
