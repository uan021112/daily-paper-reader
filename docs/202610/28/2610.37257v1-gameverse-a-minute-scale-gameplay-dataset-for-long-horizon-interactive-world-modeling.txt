Abstract
Recent advances in interactive world models have made it possible to generate
controllable, dynamic environments from action inputs. Yet current models struggle
to maintain coherence beyond roughly one minute of rollout—a limitation rooted
not in model capacity, but in the scarcity of long-horizon training data. Existing
gameplay video datasets typically consist of clips under ten seconds, with captions

Introduction
Interactive world models [1, 28, 31] aim to learn controllable, dynamic environments that can be rolled
out from action and camera inputs, a capability central to embodied decision-making, simulation, and
game-style content generation. Recent gameplay-oriented systems such as GameGen-X [2], Matrix-
Game [34], Hunyuan-GameCraft [16], and HY-World 1.5 [13] demonstrate compelling short-horizon
controllability, yet they share a striking ceiling: rollouts collapse into drift, identity loss, and scene
incoherence after roughly one minute of generation. We argue that this ceiling is, first and foremost,
a data problem rather than a model-capacity problem. The training corpora behind these systems
consist almost entirely of clips shorter than ten seconds, paired with whole-clip captions that conflate
static context with dynamic events. Such data fundamentally cannot supervise minute-scale temporal
coherence, fine-grained controllability, or event-level semantics, no matter how large the model.
This limitation is visible across recent gameplay and exploration datasets, which exhibit a consistent
pattern of single-axis optimization (Table 1). Sekai [18, 8] provides large-scale exploration video
but lacks dense interactions, validated camera trajectories, and structured semantics. Matrix-Game
and HY-World 1.5 expose engine-level camera poses but cap continuous footage at short windows.
GameGen-X and Hunyuan-GameCraft support action-conditioned interaction but lack reliable trajec-
tories and event-aligned captions. None of them simultaneously provides minute-scale continuous
footage, validated frame-level camera trajectories, dense first- and third-person interaction cover-
age, and multi-granular event-aligned semantic annotations. So long as these four signals remain
fragmented across separate datasets, any model trained on a single source inherits its blind spot.
We close this gap with GameVerse, a minute-scale gameplay dataset purpose-built for long-horizon
interactive world modeling. Curated from 11,002 source videos at 1080p and 30–60 FPS through
a unified pipeline of scene-consistent segmentation, multi-stage quality filtering, ViPE-based [11]
pose estimation with trajectory validation, and event-aligned multi-granular captioning, GameVerse
delivers 69.7K clips, 1,655 hours, and 349.6M frames, including 1,358 hours of continuous two-
minute sequences—roughly an order of magnitude longer than any prior gameplay or exploration
dataset. Every clip is paired with a temporally stable frame-level camera trajectory that survives
explicit jump-, jitter-, and drift-filtering; a four-level structured caption (global static, local static,
dynamic events, agent actions) that disentangles scene context from foreground change; and dense
behavior coverage across first- and third-person perspectives, spanning exploration, combat, walking,
driving, climbing, and complex object interaction. To our knowledge, GameVerse is the first dataset
to ship these four signals jointly under a single quality bar, and fine-tuning a strong video generator
on it improves VBench [12] temporal-consistency and visual quality, and WorldScore [4] content
alignment and 3D consistency, over gameplay-domain baselines on a 30-second benchmark.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• The largest minute-scale gameplay dataset to date. 69.7K clips, 1,655 hours, and 349.6M frames,
including 1,358 hours of continuous two-minute sequences—roughly an order of magnitude longer
than existing gameplay corpora and the first to directly supervise the minute-scale rollout regime
where current interactive world models fail.

Related Work
2.1
Interactive World Models and Gameplay Datasets
Early work on interactive video generation established the methodological foundation but relied on
closed, narrow training corpora. Genie [1] learns latent actions from an unreleased, curated 30K-hour
platformer collection, leaving the community without a reusable substrate. GameNGen [28] and
Oasis [3] demonstrate that diffusion models can serve as real-time game engines, yet rely on a
single game (e.g.DOOM) or a closed Decart corpus, limiting cross-game generalization and broader
scientific study. WorldMem [31] introduces an explicit memory module for long-term Minecraft
simulation, showing that long-horizon coherence is methodologically tractable, but remains limited
to a narrow evaluation domain without a transferable, large-scale gameplay dataset.
More recent gameplay-oriented systems advance controllability with action and camera signals [25,
33], but fall short on the long-horizon data axis. GameGen-X [2] is trained predominantly on
gameplay clips of a few seconds, providing little supervision for minute-scale rollouts. The Matrix-
Game family—the Minecraft-centered Matrix-Game [34] and Matrix-Game 2.0 [9], and the recent
Matrix-Game 3.0 [22] targeting long-horizon coherence via memory-augmented diffusion on an
industrial-scale Unreal-Engine and AAA-game data engine—releases model weights and code but
keeps its gameplay training corpora and Video-Pose-Action-Prompt quadruplet data proprietary,
leaving no shared substrate for scaling, ablation, and reproducibility. Our work complements Matrix-
Game 3.0: while it addresses the long-horizon problem from the model side through memory on a
closed data engine, GameVerse addresses it from the data side with an openly released, multi-game,
minute-scale corpus that any model can consume. Hunyuan-GameCraft [16] and HY-World 1.5 [13]
report strong qualitative results across diverse environments but rely on unreleased internal gameplay
recordings; WorldPlay [24] pursues real-time long-horizon generation under the same proprietary-data
constraint, while VMem [17] explores surfel-indexed memory but remains bound to a method-specific
corpus. Across this landscape, the lack of a public, diverse, multi-game, minute-scale gameplay
dataset remains the principal bottleneck, rather than model capacity.

2.2
Camera-Aware Video Supervision
Explicit camera control is a key axis of controllable video generation [32]. On the method side,
CameraCtrl [7] and MotionCtrl [30] demonstrate that trajectory conditioning materially improves
geometric consistency in generated video, but both rely on the data side to provide reliable trajec-
tories for training. On the data side, ViPE [11] provides scalable monocular pose estimation from
unconstrained video and is the de-facto tool for retrofitting trajectories onto large video corpora; we
leverage it as a tool in our pipeline rather than as a competing dataset. Among existing datasets,
Matrix-Game [34, 9, 22], Hunyuan-GameCraft [16], and HY-World 1.5 [13] obtain engine-level
camera poses in their closed training pipelines, which are never released as supervision; Sekai [18, 8]
ships estimated trajectories alongside long-duration walking video but does not validate them against
sudden jumps, high-frequency jitter, or long-term drift, leaving its trajectories noisy in practice at the
minute scale; and the bulk of general-purpose video-language datasets ship no calibrated poses at all.
GameVerse instead pairs every clip with a frame-level ViPE trajectory that has passed explicit jump-,
jitter-, and drift-filtering, delivering minute-scale trajectories that remain physically coherent across
the full clip, and we release these trajectories openly together with the video sources.
2.3
Structured Caption Annotations for Video
Caption supervision drives controllable generation, but existing video datasets stop short of the
structure required for long-horizon and behavior-conditioned synthesis. HowTo100M [20] relies on
ASR transcripts that are loosely aligned and dominated by narration rather than scene description.
InternVid [29] and ActivityNet-style dense-captioning data [15, 10] contribute auto-generated or
sparse event captions but treat each clip as a flat sequence of events, with no separation of static
scene context from dynamic foreground and no isolation of agent behavior from environmental
change. MiraData [14] pushes toward longer-duration video with longer structured captions, but
its semantic schema is tuned to real-world video and does not include an agent-action vocabulary
suitable for interactive generation. On the gameplay side, GameGen-X [2] provides single-line
clip-level captions that conflate scene context with dynamic events, and Sekai [18] similarly attaches
one whole-clip description to each minute-long video; in either case the controllable channel cannot
be modulated independently of the scene. Matrix-Game [34, 9, 22], Hunyuan-GameCraft [16], and
HY-World 1.5 [13] do not publicly release caption supervision at all. GameVerse instead annotates
every clip with a four-level hierarchical schema—global static, local static, dynamic events, and agent
actions—generated by a vision-language model [26] and aligned to event boundaries rather than
fixed time windows, so that environment context is disentangled from controllable agent behavior
and can be used directly as a hierarchical conditioning signal.
3
Dataset Construction Pipeline
Existing video datasets for world modeling still exhibit limitations along three axes: fragmented
temporal structure caused by short clip segmentation, unstable or absent camera pose estimation,
and flat semantic annotations that conflate static context with dynamic events. To address these
limitations, we design a unified data construction pipeline that takes a curated pool of long-form
gameplay videos and transforms it into structured, minute-scale data units, each consisting of an
aligned video segment, a validated camera trajectory, and a multi-granular caption set. The pipeline
begins with a data collection step (Section 3.1) that builds the raw pool, followed by four processing
stages: scene-consistent segmentation, multi-stage video quality evaluation (illustrated in Figure 2),
camera pose estimation and validation, and multi-granular structured captioning. Each stage targets
a specific limitation of prior gameplay datasets.
3.1
Data Collection
Constructing a long-horizon gameplay corpus at the scale GameVerse targets requires a source that is
simultaneously diverse—across games, perspectives, and behaviors—and openly releasable to the
community. Engine recordings used by Genie [1], the Matrix-Game family [34, 9, 22], Hunyuan-
GameCraft [16], and HY-World 1.5 [13] satisfy diversity within a single title but are proprietary and
typically centered on one or two games. We instead build on YouTube, which is, to our knowledge,
the only open source that simultaneously offers long-form gameplay recordings across many AAA

3.2
Scene-consistent Segmentation
Existing datasets typically rely on short clips of a few seconds, which disrupt temporal continuity and
limit long-horizon modeling. We start from long-form videos and explicitly construct minute-level
continuous segments by removing scene transitions. We first employ TransNetV2 [23] to detect
abrupt scene transitions (hard cuts) based on frame-level transition probabilities. To capture gradual
transitions (e.g., camera panning or UI state changes) that are difficult to capture by hard-cut detection
alone, we additionally compute a CLIP-based cosine semantic distance between sampled frames,
treating large jumps in semantic distance as smooth-transition boundaries. Combining hard-cut
detection with semantic-change signals, we extract scene-consistent segments of up to 120 seconds,
which can be further concatenated into longer sequences. After segmentation, we obtain 272,128
clips totalling 4,661 hours, among which 120-second clips contribute approximately 2,773 hours.
3.3
Multi-stage Video Quality Evaluation
Even after scene-consistent segmentation, raw gameplay videos may still contain subtitles, water-
marks, UI overlays, compression artifacts, duplicated content, and unstable temporal dynamics, all of
which can introduce distracting visual signals and inconsistent supervision for world modeling. To
ensure both visual fidelity and temporal coherence, we design a multi-stage video quality evaluation
pipeline (Figure 2) that progressively performs (i) basic filtering to remove low-resolution or cor-
rupted videos; (ii) content and quality assessment using OCR-based detection to filter subtitles, HUD
elements, and other overlays; (iii) stability and UI detection to identify unstable motion patterns
and temporal artifacts; (iv) deduplication and diversity selection to eliminate highly similar samples
while preserving diverse gameplay scenarios; and (v) temporal refinement using temporal-quality
metrics such as COVER [6] and VBench [12] consistency scores. These complementary stages
jointly address frame-level visual degradation, temporal inconsistencies, and dataset redundancy. The
overall quality of each clip is computed as a weighted sum of its per-stage scores, while stage-specific
constraints prevent severe quality defects from being compensated for by high scores in other di-
mensions. Additionally, we recover a subset of previously filtered clips where the camera remains
stationary while scene content continues to evolve, preserving meaningful object and agent dynamics
under fixed viewpoints for world-model training. Only clips passing the corresponding quality checks
are retained. Detailed thresholds and per-stage criteria are provided in Appendix B.

3.4
Camera Pose Estimation and Validation
Most existing datasets lack reliable camera pose information. Even when available, pose annotations
are often noisy or unstable, limiting their usefulness for geometric modeling. To address this, we
apply ViPE [11] to estimate frame-wise camera poses for each video segment, obtaining both intrinsic
and extrinsic parameters.
However, pose estimation in dynamic scenes is inherently challenging and often suffers from noise
and tracking failures. To ensure trajectory reliability, we validate every estimated pose against
three common error modes: sudden jumps (large non-physical pose changes between consecutive
frames, typically from tracking failures or relocalisation errors), high-frequency jitter (local instability
producing unnatural oscillations), and long-term drift (accumulated errors over time). We score each
clip with a unified trajectory-quality function combining local relative-pose increments (capturing
jumps and jitter) and a global trajectory smoothness term (capturing drift), and apply threshold-based
filtering to remove unreliable samples.
3.5
Multi-granular Structured Captioning
Semantic annotations in prior gameplay datasets, such as GameGen-X [2] and Hunyuan-
GameCraft [16], typically take the form of single-level captions or simple action labels that mix static
scene context with dynamic behavior. Even structured annotations often provide limited separation
across temporal and semantic scales.
To capture fine-grained gameplay semantics, we introduce a multi-granular structured captioning
framework for GameVerse using the vision-language model Kimi-K2.5 [26]. As illustrated in
Figure 1(e.2), the framework separates static context from dynamic processes and organizes each
clip’s semantics into four complementary components: global static, clip-level environment and scene
context (terrain, weather, lighting, and viewpoint); local static, relatively stable scene configurations
within each temporal segment (functional areas, landmarks, and visible objects); dynamic events,
changes in the surrounding world (crowd movement, traffic, and environmental transitions); and
agent actions, the main subject’s behavior, described using a unified action vocabulary that separates
action semantics from viewpoint. Together, these components provide explicit semantic supervision
at multiple granularities, supporting hierarchical and conditional generation. Annotation details and
examples are provided in Appendix E.

4.1
Scale and Long-Horizon Distribution
Figure 3(a) shows the clip-duration distribution of GameVerse. Unlike prior gameplay video datasets
such as GameGen-X [2], Matrix-Game [34, 9, 22], and Hunyuan-GameCraft [16], which are domi-
nated by clips shorter than ten seconds, GameVerse preserves substantially longer temporal continuity.
Over 40.6K clips reach the 120-second upper bound and together contribute approximately 1,358
hours of continuous gameplay video, an order of magnitude longer than the continuous footage
exposed in any prior gameplay corpus that we are aware of. Clips of 60 s or longer account for
a further substantial portion of the dataset, providing the kind of minute-scale supervision that is
essentially absent from existing gameplay corpora and is required to extend interactive world-model
rollouts beyond the one-minute coherence ceiling discussed in Section 1.
4.2
Interaction and Environment Diversity
GameVerse spans at least 110 distinct game titles, with the genre composition shown in panel (II)
of Figure 1. The distribution is long-tailed: the most-represented titles—God of War, Red Dead
Redemption 2, Ghost of Tsushima, Elden Ring, and The Witcher 3—each contribute over 1,800
clips, while the tail extends across dozens of additional AAA and indie titles. Across the corpus,
approximately 24% of clips are first-person, 73% are third-person follow camera, and 3% use
specialised perspectives (drone, vehicle-mounted, gimbal-follow, stationary). This 1:3 first-to-third-
person ratio supplies substantial supervision for both FPV and TPV settings, in sharp contrast
to the Minecraft-only Matrix-Game [34, 9] and to Sekai [18], which is restricted to first-person
walking footage. Figure 1 also presents the distribution of player-behavior categories—exploring,
combat, walking, driving, interacting, climbing, running, and idle—spanning basic locomotion
to complex object interaction and high-speed vehicle control; exploring, combat, walking, and
driving together contribute the largest share of total video duration. Figure 3(b) further shows the
hierarchical scene-attribute distribution: the corpus spans urban, rural, forest, highway, temple, and
industrial environments under varied architectural styles, weather conditions, time-of-day, seasons,
and crowd-density levels.
4.3
Camera Trajectory Diversity
Figure 4(a) shows the distribution of camera-motion patterns in GameVerse. The corpus covers
static, rotational, translational, and complex motions. Forward motion accounts for the largest share
(34.7%), reflecting the prevalence of continuous navigation and exploration in gameplay. Complex
motions that couple translation with rotation contribute a further 22.0%; these clips are particularly
challenging for long-horizon world modeling because they require simultaneous geometric and
identity consistency under non-trivial viewpoint change. Lateral movement, orbit motion, and mixed
rotational patterns are also well represented, indicating substantial diversity along the trajectory
axis. By contrast, real-world walking-video datasets such as Sekai are typically dominated by
forward translation with limited rotational complexity, and gameplay datasets that obtain engine-level
poses are restricted to a single title’s controller scheme. The richer camera dynamics in GameVerse

therefore enable supervision for geometry-aware generation, camera-controllable video synthesis,
and interactive world modeling under genuine viewpoint change.

5
Experiments
5.1
Experimental Setup
We evaluate 60-second image-to-video generation on the same 103 inputs using LingBot [5], HY-
World 1.5 [13, 24], HY-World-FT, and LTX-FT. HY-World-FT is obtained by fine-tuning HY-
World 1.5 on approximately 600 hours of GameVerse for 3,000 steps, using the step-3,000 checkpoint
without changing the architecture. LTX-FT is a GameVerse-adapted LTX-Video 2.3 model [19]. The
fixed-backbone comparison measures the benefit of additional GameVerse fine-tuning under a shared
evaluation protocol; comparisons across model families characterize complete systems with different
architectures and control interfaces.
5.2
Evaluation Metrics
We evaluate generation performance from two complementary perspectives. Video generation quality
is assessed using VBench [12] across six dimensions: Subject Consistency, Background Consistency,
Motion Smoothness, Aesthetic Quality, Imaging Quality, and Overall Consistency, collectively
measuring temporal coherence, motion stability, visual fidelity, and text–video alignment. World
generation quality and controllability are evaluated using WorldScore [4], covering camera and
object control, content alignment, 3D consistency, photometric consistency, style consistency, and
subjective quality. We report all seven normalized WorldScore components alongside their arithmetic
mean (WS-Static). Higher scores indicate better performance across all reported metrics. Detailed
definitions and evaluation protocols are provided in Appendix C.1.

6
Conclusion and Limitations
We present GameVerse, a minute-scale gameplay dataset for long-horizon interactive world model-
ing, comprising 69.7K clips totaling 1,655 hours, including 1,358 hours of continuous two-minute
gameplay. Built through a unified pipeline, GameVerse provides quality-filtered frame-level camera
trajectories and multi-granular structured captions, jointly capturing long-term scene evolution, cam-
era motion, and fine-grained interaction dynamics. Experiments on 60-second generation demonstrate
improvements in temporal coherence, geometric consistency, and visual quality, with same-backbone
comparisons further validating the benefits of GameVerse fine-tuning. Additional annotation-quality
assessments support the reliability of its semantic and geometric supervision. We envision GameVerse
as a valuable resource for advancing long-horizon interactive world modeling and enabling more
coherent, controllable, and persistent virtual worlds.
Limitations.
GameVerse focuses on gameplay environments, with cross-domain generalization
to real-world scenarios remaining unexplored. Despite multi-stage validation, estimated camera
trajectories and generated captions may retain residual errors. Continuous clips are limited to two
minutes, while generation experiments cover up to 60 seconds, leaving longer rollouts and closed-
loop interaction for future work. Moreover, the individual contributions of clip duration, camera
supervision, and structured captions remain to be isolated, and large-scale data construction is
computationally intensive.

A
Implementation Details
Training Configuration.
We fine-tune LTX-Video 2.3 (22B parameters) from the official base
checkpoint on a curated GameVerse subset of 24,380 game-video segments (12–15 s each) at 540p /
24 fps. Since GameVerse targets visual interactive world modeling, we remove the audio branch of
LTX-Video 2.3 prior to fine-tuning and train only the video stream. Training uses a mixed objective
with weights 60% image-to-video (i2v), 20% video-to-video (v2v), and 20% text-to-video (t2v), runs
for 3,700 steps at batch size 1 per GPU under FSDP across 64 GPUs. Camera conditioning is provided
by additive Plücker injection and action conditioning by 81-class direction-angle embeddings. We
optimise with AdamW at learning rate 1 × 10−4 on a constant schedule with a 200-step warmup,
and retain the base model’s RoPE (max_pos = 20) under fractional-position normalisation so that all
training samples remain within the position range seen during pretraining.
Data Processing Efficiency.
To support large-scale data construction, we optimize each stage of
the GameVerse pipeline for high-throughput processing. For scene-consistent segmentation, we adopt
FFmpeg with NVENC acceleration, which significantly improves video decoding and preprocessing
efficiency. Combined with TransNetV2, the segmentation pipeline achieves a processing throughput
of approximately 400 FPS per worker. For camera trajectory estimation, we employ a distributed
ViPE inference pipeline deployed on 256 GPUs. On average, pose estimation for each gameplay
video requires approximately 6 minutes, enabling scalable processing of long-duration videos. For
structured semantic annotation, we deploy Kimi-K2.5 using the SGLang inference framework on
H20 90GB GPUs. The caption generation stage is executed on 16 compute nodes with a total of 128
GPUs, allowing efficient large-scale generation of multi-granular semantic annotations.

Detailed Multi-stage Video Quality Assessment Pipeline
Even after segmentation, raw gameplay clips often contain undesirable artifacts such as subtitles,
watermarks, UI overlays, compression noise, and unstable motion. These artifacts introduce spurious
correlations and degrade temporal consistency, which are particularly harmful for world modeling.
To address this, we design a multi-stage quality assessment pipeline with explicit criteria and
calibrated thresholds, jointly considering visual quality, content cleanliness, and temporal stability.
Overall Formulation.
We define the overall quality of a video clip x as a weighted combination of
multiple metrics:

where qk(x) denotes the k-th quality metric and wk is its corresponding weight. In practice, we adopt
a hybrid strategy that combines hard constraints with soft scoring, ensuring both strict filtering and
relative ranking.
Visual Quality.
We first evaluate low-level visual properties to remove clips with obvious degrada-
tion. Specifically, we require all clips to satisfy a minimum resolution of 720p, frame rate within
[24, 65] fps, and valid encoding formats (e.g., H.264, HEVC, or AV1). We further compute luminance
using the BT.601 standard on frames sampled at 5 fps, and discard clips that contain prolonged
underexposed or overexposed segments, defined as more than 15 consecutive frames with luminance
below 16 or above 240. In addition, we detect border artifacts such as letterboxing and pillarboxing
by measuring pixel variance along image boundaries; regions with standard deviation below 4.0 are
treated as uniform borders, and clips are removed if such regions occupy more than 10% of the frame.
Content Cleanliness.
To eliminate non-world signals, we explicitly filter out text overlays and UI
elements. We apply OCR-based detection to identify subtitles and watermarks, focusing on the lower
portion of the frame (y ∈[0.7, 1.0]). Text regions that persist across multiple frames or occupy more
than 2% of the image area trigger rejection, with a minimum detectable text height ratio of 0.008 to
capture small HUD elements. In addition, we detect UI overlays based on their temporal stability
and spatial structure. Specifically, we compute the temporal standard deviation σt, mean intensity
µt, and spatial gradient magnitude |∇I| for each pixel, and identify UI regions as those satisfying
(σt < 4) ∧(µt > 25) ∧(|∇I| > 12). Clips with UI coverage exceeding 4% are removed. This step
is crucial to prevent models from overfitting to game-specific interface signals.
14


Temporal Stability.
We then evaluate whether clips exhibit stable and physically plausible dy-
namics. Motion is estimated using optical flow (Farneback) at 4 fps, where frames with mean flow
magnitude below 0.3 are considered static; clips with more than 50% such frames are discarded. We
further detect freezing artifacts by measuring frame-to-frame luminance differences: consecutive
frames with differences below 0.5 are marked as frozen, and clips are rejected if freezing persists
for more than approximately 2 seconds or exceeds 30% of the clip. To capture unstable camera
motion, we analyze displacement statistics within sliding windows and identify jitter spikes when
instantaneous motion exceeds five times the local mean; clips with more than four such spikes per
minute are filtered out.
Beyond these rule-based filters, we incorporate learned quality metrics.
We compute multi-
dimensional quality scores using COVER, including semantic, technical, and aesthetic components,
and retain only the top 70% of clips ranked by the sum of aesthetic and technical scores. We further
evaluate temporal consistency using VBench [12] metrics, including subject consistency, background
consistency, and temporal flickering, with thresholds set to 0.65, 0.85, and 0.83 respectively.
Final Selection.
The final decision integrates all criteria. Hard constraints (e.g., format validity,
OCR detection, UI filtering, motion and freeze rejection) ensure that low-quality samples are strictly
removed, while soft scoring mechanisms (e.g., COVER ranking and VBench thresholds) provide
finer-grained quality control. Only clips that satisfy all constraints and exceed the required thresholds
are retained.
Effectiveness.
This multi-stage pipeline yields a dataset with high visual clarity, clean semantic
content, and stable temporal dynamics. Compared to simple filtering strategies, it provides a more
robust and comprehensive quality control mechanism, which is essential for training reliable and
generalizable world models.

Additional Experimental Details and Analyses
C.1
Evaluation Metrics
VBench.
We adopt six evaluation dimensions from VBench [12] to assess temporal consistency,
perceptual quality, and semantic alignment of generated videos. Subject Consistency measures
the stability of subject appearance across frames using DINO feature similarity, capturing identity
drift and unintended changes in visual attributes. Background Consistency evaluates scene-level
coherence through CLIP feature similarity, reflecting whether background structures remain visually
consistent over time. Motion Smoothness assesses the temporal continuity of generated motion
using a video frame interpolation model, penalizing abrupt or unnatural transitions. For frame-level
quality, Aesthetic Quality measures visual appeal using the LAION aesthetic predictor, considering
aspects such as composition and color harmony, while Imaging Quality employs MUSIQ to evaluate
visual distortions, including blur, noise, and overexposure. Finally, Overall Consistency measures
semantic and stylistic alignment between generated videos and their textual descriptions using ViCLIP.
Together, these metrics characterize complementary aspects of video generation quality, particularly
temporal coherence and visual fidelity over extended rollouts.
WorldScore.
We further employ WorldScore [4] to evaluate world generation quality and con-
trollability from geometric, semantic, and perceptual perspectives. The benchmark includes three
controllability metrics and four quality metrics. Camera Control measures adherence to the target
camera trajectory by comparing estimated camera poses from generated videos with the prescribed
motion, incorporating scale-invariant rotation and translation errors. Object Control evaluates whether
prompt-specified objects appear in generated scenes using open-set object detection, while Content
Alignment measures semantic agreement with the complete input description using CLIPScore. For
generation quality, 3D Consistency evaluates geometric stability across frames through reprojection
errors based on DROID-SLAM reconstruction. Photometric Consistency assesses the stability of
visual appearance and textures using forward–backward optical-flow consistency, capturing artifacts
such as texture shifts and flickering. Style Consistency evaluates whether the visual style remains
consistent by comparing feature Gram matrices across generated frames. Finally, Subjective Quality
estimates perceptual quality through an automatic combination of CLIP-IQA+ and CLIP Aesthetic
scores, rather than direct human ratings.

sequences offer ground-truth camera intrinsics and extrinsics. For general videos lacking camera an-
notations, LingBot employs MegaSAM to estimate camera poses. Its camera-conditioning mechanism
further incorporates continuous camera information through Plücker embeddings for fine-grained
viewpoint control.
HY-World 1.5.
HY-World 1.5 [13, 24] constructs its training corpus from approximately 320K
clips, including 170K AAA-game recordings, 60K reconstructed scenes from DL3DV, 50K synthetic
UE-rendered sequences, and 40K real-world videos from Sekai. The UE-rendered subset provides
ground-truth camera trajectories, while the DL3DV subset uses 3D reconstruction and customized
trajectory rendering for geometrically consistent supervision. For videos without available camera
poses, ViPE is used for trajectory estimation and subsequent motion-consistency filtering. HY-
World thus combines rendered, reconstruction-derived, and estimated trajectories with dedicated
camera-conditioning mechanisms.
Comparison with GameVerse.
Unlike these systems, GameVerse primarily collects natural game-
play recordings without direct access to game-engine camera parameters. Camera trajectories are
recovered using ViPE and subjected to multi-stage quality filtering to remove abrupt jumps, jitter,
and unreliable motion. While estimated trajectories lack the precision guarantees of engine-rendered
ground truth, they enable scalable coverage of diverse gameplay motions, including irregular naviga-
tion, coupled translation and rotation, and complex viewpoint transitions. Therefore, differences in
camera-control performance may reflect both pose-supervision sources and conditioning architec-
tures. Nevertheless, improvements from HY-World 1.5 to HY-World-FT demonstrate the utility of
GameVerse’s estimated trajectories for geometric supervision.
C.3
Camera-Trajectory Reliability by Motion Type
To assess the reliability of GameVerse’s estimated trajectories, we evaluate pose completeness,
temporal stability, and cross-estimator agreement across six camera-motion categories: forward,
lateral, orbit, tilt, mixed rotation, and complex motion. As shown in Table 6, all audited retained
trajectories contain valid poses throughout their duration, with generally low jump rates and rotational
jerk. We further compare ViPE trajectories against an independent DROID-SLAM estimator after
Sim(3) alignment. Rotation RPE ranges from 0.75 to 1.47 degrees, while translation-direction error
ranges from 4.84 to 11.03 degrees, with mixed rotation exhibiting the largest errors. These results
support the temporal stability and cross-estimator consistency of the retained trajectories, although
they do not establish absolute pose accuracy without ground-truth trajectories. The reported 100%
valid-pose ratios refer to the retained audit set rather than all raw source videos.

Four-level Structured Semantic Annotations
To better support long-horizon interactive world modeling, GameVerse introduces a four-level
structured semantic annotation framework that explicitly decomposes gameplay videos into comple-
mentary semantic components. Unlike conventional video captions that describe an entire clip using
a single sentence, our annotations separately model global static, local static, dynamic events, and
agent actions, enabling more structured supervision for controllable generation and temporal world
understanding.
Figure 7 summarizes the overall annotation format of GameVerse. Each gameplay video is first asso-
ciated with global metadata describing environmental conditions, camera properties, and high-level
scene semantics. The video is then divided into temporally aligned segments, where each segment
contains structured annotations for local scene structure, dynamic events, interaction behaviors,
environmental evolution, and natural-language scene descriptions.
Global Static.
The first level focuses on globally consistent environmental properties throughout
the video sequence. These annotations describe high-level scene attributes such as environment type,
architectural style, weather conditions, lighting conditions, season, camera perspective, and overall

Local Static.
The second level captures fine-grained spatial structures within temporally aligned
segments. Compared with global context annotations, this level focuses on local scene layouts
and environment details that evolve over time. For example, the annotations explicitly describe
transitions between covered stone arcades, narrow residential streets, open plazas, cave tunnels,
lakeside shorelines, and underground grotto environments. These local structural descriptions
preserve spatial continuity and geometric organization across long-duration gameplay sequences.
Dynamic Events.
The third level models temporally evolving events and environmental transitions.
Instead of only describing static scenes, the annotations explicitly capture scene evolution over time,
including entering enclosed caves, exiting urban districts, approaching environmental landmarks,
navigating through crowded markets, and transitioning between indoor and outdoor environments. As
illustrated in the examples, GameVerse also captures environmental events such as lightning flashes,
motion changes, and camera-view transitions, enabling richer supervision for temporal causality and
long-horizon scene evolution.
Agent Actions.
The fourth level focuses on interaction-centric behaviors of the player character
and surrounding agents. These annotations describe actions such as walking, exploration, climbing,
stealth movement, driving, environmental interaction, and NPC activities. For example, the examples
include navigating crowded urban streets, traversing cave systems, approaching historical landmarks,
and exploring large-scale outdoor environments. Compared with conventional action labels, the
proposed annotations jointly model both the action itself and its surrounding interaction context,
enabling richer supervision for controllable interaction modeling and embodied world simulation.
Together, these four semantic levels provide complementary supervision signals for long-horizon
video understanding and generation. By explicitly separating global context, local structure, dy-
