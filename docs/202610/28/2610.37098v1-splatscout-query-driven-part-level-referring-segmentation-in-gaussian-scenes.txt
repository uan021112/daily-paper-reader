# SplatScout: Query-Driven Part-Level Referring Segmentation in Gaussian Scenes

## Abstract 

Existing referring segmentation methods on Gaussian scenes primarily target whole objects, relying on segmenter-generated candidates from which a vision-language model (VLM) selects. Such predetermined fixed candidates may omit fine-grained parts needed as targets or cues for identifying objects. We in-troduce SplatScout, a query-driven, closed-loop approach that places percep-tion under VLM control. The model actively selects views, directs segmenta-tion, and inspects rendered 3D results to refine its predictions. For part-level evaluation, we introduce PartRef-ScanNet++, comprising 261 manually verified queries across 12 ScanNet++ scenes with mesh-derived ground truth. On PartRef-ScanNet++, SplatScout achieves 0.538 mIoU, substantially outperforming exist-ing methods. Ablations demonstrate the benefits of iterative refinement, while comparisons across VLM backbones highlight the importance of effective tool-feedback utilization. SplatScout also achieves higher scores on four existing re-ferring benchmarks. Code and the PartRef-ScanNet++ benchmark are available at 

https://github.com/kaust-violab/SplatScout .

## 1 Introduction 

3D Gaussian Splatting [Kerbl et al., 2023] represents scenes as explicit primitives that can be recon-structed and rendered in real time. Beyond rendering, a growing body of work attaches language to Gaussian scenes. Open-vocabulary methods distil features from 2D foundation models into Gaus-sian primitives, enabling category-level queries [Qin et al., 2024, Shi et al., 2024, Wu et al., 2024]. Referring methods go further, identifying specific objects described by expressions involving at-tributes and spatial relations [He et al., 2025, Ding et al., 2026c,b, Hassan et al., 2026]. However, existing referring methods for Gaussian scenes primarily focus on whole objects, while practical in-structions often refer to their parts, such as “the handle of the second drawer on the right of the table by the window”. Unlike whole objects, parts have query-dependent granularity: the same chair may be requested as a whole in one query, its backrest in another, and its armrest in a third. Parts may also serve as targets, discriminators between similar objects, or anchors for locating other objects. Grounding such expressions requires identifying the relevant scene regions and segmenting them at the granularity specified by the query. Suitable observations must be selected to obtain segmenta-tion masks across multiple views, which are then integrated to construct the target Gaussian instance. However, the required views and segmentation regions depend on the query and may only become apparent during perception. A fixed, query-independent scene decomposition therefore cannot reli-ably provide all the candidates needed for grounding. Meanwhile, modern vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly capable of recognizing visual attributes, distinguishing fine-grained regions, and reasoning about spatial relationships in 2D im-ages. Yet many existing grounding methods limit the VLM to selecting candidates proposed by a segmenter, either during preprocessing or in a single pass at query time. Even when candidates are

generated on demand, the VLM typically cannot use intermediate visual feedback to revise them. Consequently, if the target part is missing or segmented at the wrong granularity, stronger visual reasoning alone cannot recover it through candidate selection. This design limits how effectively advances in VLM capabilities translate into better grounding. We address this limitation by changing the role of the VLM from candidate selection to perception control. Rather than choosing from a predetermined set of instances, the model decides where to look, what to segment, and at what granularity, based on the expression and its observations. The segmenter and geometric operations become tools that execute these decisions, allowing the VLM to apply its visual understanding directly to the construction of query-specific 3D instances. However, query-time perception alone is insufficient if decisions are executed in a single pass. The appropriate next action often depends on previous results: an observation may require closer in-spection, segmentation may reveal an incorrectly selected region, and lifting the masks into 3D may expose errors that were not apparent in the original images. We therefore organize perception as a closed loop in which each operation returns feedback to the VLM. By inspecting intermediate results, the model can revise its decisions and progressively refine the target rather than committing to its initial predictions. On this basis, we introduce SplatScout, a query-driven, closed-loop perception system for referring segmentation in Gaussian scenes. Before a query arrives, SplatScout never constructs semantic can-didates. During query execution, the VLM coordinates three tools: the Navigator selects informative observations, the Segmenter produces query-directed masks, and the Lifter constructs 3D Gaussian instances. Each tool returns feedback that informs subsequent decisions, including rendered views of lifted instances for detecting and correcting errors. The VLM thus controls both instance con-struction and the decision of when to return an answer. To evaluate part-level grounding, we introduce PartRef-ScanNet++, a benchmark containing 261 manually verified referring expressions across 12 ScanNet++[Yeshwanth et al., 2023] scenes. It cov-ers whole objects, parts serving different roles in referring expressions, and multiple-target queries, with ground truth verified on laser-scanned meshes. On this benchmark, SplatScout substantially outperforms prior referring segmentation methods and achieves higher scores on four of five ex-isting referring benchmarks. Controlled experiments further investigate the benefits of closed-loop perception and the role of VLM capabilities in utilizing perceptual feedback. Our main contributions are: • We identify and experimentally investigate the limitations of fixed segmentation candidate pipelines and compare them to a VLM using visual feedback to guide perception, particu-larly for part-level referring expressions. • We introduce SplatScout, which transfers perceptual decision-making to the VLM and co-ordinates observation, segmentation, and 3D instance construction within a query-driven, closed-loop framework, without precomputed semantic candidates. • We introduce PartRef-ScanNet++, a part-level referring segmentation benchmark with 261 human-verified expressions across 12 ScanNet++ scenes and an evaluation protocol based on mesh ground truth. • SplatScout substantially improves referring segmentation performance on existing object-level benchmarks and the part-level tasks in PartRef-ScanNet++. Controlled experiments demonstrate the benefits of using perceptual feedback to guide grounding. 

## 2 Related Work 

Grounding without a VLM: Gaussian Splatting [Kerbl et al., 2023] represents scenes as explicit primitives that can be reconstructed and rendered in real time. Many works attach language to these primitives, primarily enabling open-vocabulary segmentation through object names or category la-bels rather than full referring expressions. They train features from 2D foundation models into per-scene fields [Qin et al., 2024, Shi et al., 2024, Zhou et al., 2024], attach semantic information to Gaussian groups [Ye et al., 2024, Wu et al., 2024, Li et al., 2025a, Xie et al., 2026], or back-project features without training [Kim et al., 2025, Marrie et al., 2025, Ding et al., 2026a, Chacko et al., 2025, Zang et al., 2026]. ReferSplat [He et al., 2025] extends language-based Gaussian seg-2mentation to full referring expressions by training a referring field per scene. These approaches rely largely on pre-established semantic representations, rather than constructing target instances through query-driven perceptual interaction. 

Grounding with a VLM: A second line brings in a vision-language model at query time, but these methods restrict its use to choosing among candidates that a segmenter has produced. On Gaussian scenes the candidates are instance groups or a scene graph built once per scene [Hassan et al., 2026, Ding et al., 2026b], or masks cut once at query time from a class label parsed out of the expression [Ding et al., 2026c]. On point clouds they are a lookup table of segmented objects that the model reasons over before selecting an ID [Yang et al., 2024, Yuan et al., 2024, 2025, Li et al., 2025b, Lin et al., 2025]. OpenGround [Huang et al., 2026] and Think, Act, Build [Wang et al., 2026] segment a missing label on demand, but only to extend the existing candidate set in a single query, and without any check in 3D. Similar to VLM-free methods, all of the above only select among pre-segmented candidates, without enabling the VLM to use its perception to explore the 3D scene or guide the segmentation with expressions based on scene and query understanding. In this work we focus on adding this capability to the VLM. 

Part-level grounding: Existing part-aware Gaussian methods such as LangSplat and its succes-sors [Qin et al., 2024, Xie et al., 2026, Chacko et al., 2025] primarily rely on SAM and CLIP models to create pre-generated whole-object, part, and sub-part masks and language embeddings. Their supported granularity is therefore limited by the regions identified during preprocessing. On point clouds, part-level understanding has been studied mainly through functional elements. Scene-Fun3D [Delitzas et al., 2024] annotates handles, knobs, and buttons referenced by task instructions. Subsequent methods ground such parts using 2D detectors and segmenters [Corsetti et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2025b] or fixed superpoints [Wu et al., 2025]. Anywhere3D [Wang et al., 2025a] eval-uates bounding-box grounding across granularities, while Articulate3D and MultiScan [Halacheva et al., 2025, Mao et al., 2022] provide detailed part annotations. Existing benchmarks primarily treat parts as predefined targets, with limited coverage of their roles in identifying other objects. In contrast, PartRef-ScanNet++ includes parts as targets, discriminators, and relational anchors, with mesh-level segmentation ground truth. SplatScout supports these different roles through query-adaptive part segmentation.

3.2 Method Overview 

As illustrated in Fig. 1, SplatScout consists of three functional tool sets coordinated by a VLM con-troller: the Navigator, Segmenter, and Lifter. Starting from the desired output, obtaining the target Gaussian subset requires constructing 3D instances from multi-view segmentation masks, which in turn requires acquiring suitable observations of the scene. These dependencies define the respon-sibilities of the three tool sets. The Navigator retrieves informative scene views, the Segmenter produces query-directed masks, and the Lifter integrates multi-view masks into 3D Gaussian in-stances. Their complete interfaces, including available actions, arguments, and feedback, are listed in Table 5 of Appendix A. Rather than executing these tools in a predetermined sequence, the VLM selects its next step action based on the referring expression and the results of previous steps. Each tool returns task-specific feedback, including scene observations, segmentation overlays, or rendered views of lifted instances. The VLM inspects these intermediate results and may invoke additional tools to refine its current hypothesis. Consequently, the target instances are constructed progressively during query execution, without precomputed semantic candidates. 

3.3 Closed-Loop Grounding 

Given a referring expression Q, let Et denote the intermediate entities constructed at step t, including segmentation groups and lifted 3D instances. Let F be the available input frames, and Ht ⊆ F the frames already observed by the VLM. The interaction history is τt = (( a0, r 0), . . . , (at−1, r t−1)) ,where ai and ri denote an action and its corresponding feedback. Initially, E0, H0, and τ0 are empty. At each step, the VLM selects an action at = π(Q, τ t, ℓ t), where π denotes the VLM and ℓt sum-marizes the execution state and remaining budget. The tool interface Φ takes the action, current entities, observed frames, Gaussian scene, and available frames as inputs, returning feedback rt and updated states Et+1 and Ht+1 . The interaction history is then updated as τt+1 = τt∥(at, r t).The tools execute operations but do not decide whether to accept their results. Based on the feedback, the VLM may explore additional views, revise segmentations, merge observations, or lift and inspect 3D instances. The execution order thus adapts to the query and previous observations rather than following a fixed pipeline. The VLM terminates by returning a valid answer, reporting that the target cannot be identified, or exhausting its budget. 3.4 Perception Tools 

The three tool sets in Figure. 1 provide the perceptual and geometric operations used by the VLM controller. The Navigator retrieves informative scene observations, the Segmenter constructs query-directed 2D masks, and the Lifter converts these masks into subsets of 2D Gaussian primitives in 3D space. Each tool returns intermediate results that the VLM can inspect before selecting its next action.

3.4.2 Segmenter: Query-Adaptive Segmentation VLM-directed segmentation: The Segmenter uses SAM3 [Carion et al., 2026] to generate query-specific masks from the frame, bounding box, and text prompt selected by the VLM. The VLM may also specify additional frames for multi-view tracking. If joint text-and-box prompting fails, the tool retries with the box alone. The resulting masks are organized into segmentation groups for subsequent 3D lifting. 

Parent constraints and feedback: For part-level queries, the VLM may restrict segmentation to an existing parent object. The Segmenter projects the parent’s Gaussian representation into the selected frame and suppresses regions outside its dilated mask mpar . A predicted mask m is retained if |m ∩ mpar |/|m| ≥ 0.8.The Segmenter returns mask overlays, cut-outs, multi-view results, and quality indicators, including box-mask agreement and 3D overlap with existing groups. These allow the VLM to inspect results and decide whether to revise them, explore views, or proceed to lifting. 

3.4.3 Lifter: Multi-View Gaussian Instance Construction 

The Lifter converts query-specific 2D masks into 2D Gaussian instances in 3D space. Given seg-mentation groups from one or more views, it aggregates their geometric evidence, identifies the Gaussian primitives, and returns a rendered view of the instance for inspection. 

Multi-view mask aggregation: Before lifting, the Lifter organizes the masks returned by the Segmenter into multi-view groups. Each segmentation call produces a mask track spanning the prompted frame and additional tracked frames. Tracks are split at tracking gaps to avoid incorrect associations. The masks are then approximately back-projected onto the Gaussians, and tracks with sufficient 3D overlap are merged into groups. The VLM can further merge groups belonging to the same physical entity using the same tool. Each resulting group provides the multi-view masks used for Gaussian lifting.

## 4 Experiments 

4.1 PartRef-ScanNet++ Dataset and Evaluation Metrics Dataset: We introduce PartRef-ScanNet++, a benchmark for part-level referring segmentation comprising 261 manually verified queries across 12 ScanNet++ scenes. The queries reference 201 object and 95 part instances annotated on laser-scanned meshes. They cover five referring roles: ob-jects, parts as targets, parts as discriminators, parts as anchors, and multiple targets. In 154 queries (59%), parts serve as targets, discriminators, or anchors. Each query was manually reviewed to en-sure unambiguous target identification and natural, contextually appropriate wording. Dataset con-struction, query statistics, and comparisons with existing benchmarks are detailed in Appendix B. 

Evaluation metrics: We evaluate on 216 held-out test frames, excluded from all methods’ per-ception and preprocessing. Predicted Gaussian subsets are rendered within their complete scenes to obtain occlusion-aware masks, which are compared against ground-truth masks ray-traced from the laser-scanned meshes. For each query, we average the intersection over union (IoU) over test frames containing at least 300 visible target pixels. We report mean IoU (mIoU) across queries and the percentage of queries achieving IoU thresholds of 0.25 and 0.5 (Acc@0.25/0.5). 4.2 3D Visual Grounding Experiments Main results: As shown in Table 1, SplatScout achieves 0.538 mIoU, compared with 0.253 for the strongest baseline, with substantial gains across all query types. These results demonstrate the diffi-culty of referring segmentation in large indoor scenes, where identifying the correct target requires scene exploration and disambiguation. The consistent improvements support the effectiveness of query-driven, closed-loop perception. Since ZeroSplat and GaussDet had not released their implementations at the time of our experiments, we reproduced both methods from their papers. Their reproduced results on Ref-LERF are reported in Appendix C. 

Evaluation on other datasets: We evaluate SplatScout on five existing benchmarks following their protocols (Appendix D). As shown in Table 2, SplatScout outperforms published baselines on four of the five benchmarks. The exception is GR-ScanNet, where SplatScout achieves 24.0 mIoU versus ZeroSplat’s 41.2. A likely factor is the mismatch in evaluation granularity: GR-ScanNet uses category-level ground truth, which can include multiple instances of the same category, whereas SplatScout identifies the specific instances referred to by the query. Consequently, instance-level predictions may be penalized for not covering other same-category regions.

4.4 Ablations and Oracle Experiments 

Table 4 examines the role of closed-loop interaction and individual perceptual feedback channels. Restricting the system to a single round observation and segmentation decision reduces mIoU from 0.450 to 0.209. Among the three feedback ablations, removing visual exploration causes the largest drop, to 0.319 mIoU. Suppressing segmentation and lifting feedback yields 0.412 and 0.440 mIoU, respectively. These results highlight the importance of early visual observations and suggest that the remaining feedback channels can partially compensate for missing information. We analyze the resulting tool-use behavior in Appendix F. Oracle experiments reveal further opportunities for improvement. Ground-truth boxes increase mIoU to 0.571, while ground-truth frame selection provides only a marginal additional gain (0.578). Replacing segmentation masks with ground truth further improves performance to 0.689, highlight-ing the importance of accurate query-directed segmentation. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We introduced SplatScout, a query-driven, closed-loop approach to part-level referring segmenta-tion in Gaussian scenes. By placing perception under VLM control, our method enables the model to select observations, construct query-specific instances, and refine its predictions through percep-tual feedback without precomputed semantic candidates. We also introduced PartRef-ScanNet++, a benchmark covering diverse part-level referring expressions with mesh-derived ground truth. SplatScout substantially outperforms existing methods on our benchmark and achieves higher scores on four external referring benchmarks. Our experiments further demonstrate the benefits of itera-tive interaction and show that feedback from different perceptual stages plays complementary roles. These findings highlight the potential of closed-loop perception for leveraging increasingly capable VLMs in fine-grained 3D scene understanding. 9Acknowledgments and Disclosure of Funding

B.2 Dataset Construction Scenes and annotations: We select 12 ScanNet++ scenes, each providing laser-scanned meshes and posed DSLR images. Following the official splits, we reconstruct one 2DGS scene using the training frames of each scene. The 216 held-out test frames are reserved exclusively for evaluation. Object annotations are obtained from ScanNet++, yielding 1,616 valid instances after filtering non-object labels. Part annotations are derived from 723 Articulate3D[Halacheva et al., 2025] instances and supplemented with 24 manually verified parts. All annotations are represented as vertex subsets of the same laser-scanned meshes. The final queries reference 201 object and 95 part instances. 

Query construction and verification: Initial query candidates are generated with AI assistance from mesh-derived geometric relations and subsequently reviewed and revised by human annotators. Scene-level relation summaries identify suitable anchors and spatial relationships that distinguish target instances. Viewpoint-dependent expressions are excluded. Each expression is checked for naturalness, target uniqueness, and the necessity of its qualifying conditions. We additionally verify target visibility and annotation validity. All 357 initial queries undergo manual review using a 3D annotation interface displaying highlighted mesh targets and corresponding images. Of these, 254 are retained, nine are revised, and 94 are discarded. After deduplication, the final benchmark contains 261 queries. 

Ground-truth generation: For each held-out test frame, we generate occlusion-aware ground-truth masks by ray tracing the laser-scanned meshes. A pixel belongs to the target only when the first ray intersection lies on its annotated mesh surface. Privacy-masked regions are excluded from evaluation. 

B.3 Evaluation Protocol Evaluation setup: We evaluate predictions against mesh-derived ground truth on 216 held-out test frames. These frames are excluded from all methods’ perception and preprocessing procedures. The evaluator supports binary Gaussian subsets, per-Gaussian soft weights, and mesh vertex subsets. For vertex-based submissions, a triangle is included when at least two of its vertices belong to the predicted subset. 

Occlusion-aware rendering evaluation: For Gaussian-based predictions, the submitted subset is rendered together with the complete scene. Each selected Gaussian is assigned a membership value of one, while all remaining Gaussians are assigned zero. The membership values are composited using the original Gaussian opacities. A pixel is classified as foreground when the accumulated target contribution exceeds 0.5 and the total accumulated opacity exceeds 0.5. For soft-weight predictions, the submitted weights replace binary membership values. The predicted masks are compared against occlusion-aware ground-truth masks obtained by ray tracing the laser-scanned meshes. ## D External Benchmark Evaluation Protocols 

Experimental setup: We evaluate SplatScout on five existing benchmarks: Ref-LERF [He et al., 2025], GR-LERF and GR-ScanNet [Ding et al., 2026c], and Causal-LERF and Causal-ScanNet [Ding et al., 2026b]. We use only the training frames of each benchmark, without modi-fying our perception or grounding pipeline, and follow its original evaluation protocol. All baseline results in Table 2 are taken from the respective publications. 

2D evaluation on LERF: Ref-LERF, GR-LERF, and Causal-LERF evaluate segmentation on the four LERF scenes using 2D mIoU. We render the predicted Gaussian subsets from the benchmark’s evaluation views and compare the resulting masks against the provided ground-truth masks, follow-ing each benchmark’s scoring protocol. 

3D evaluation on ScanNet: GR-ScanNet and Causal-ScanNet use point-level 3D mIoU, follow-ing the fixed-point evaluation setup of OpenGaussian. We initialize one Gaussian per input Scan-Net point and disable densification and position updates during reconstruction, preserving one-to-one correspondence between Gaussians and ground-truth points. Consequently, predicted Gaussian membership can be compared directly with the corresponding ground-truth point labels, without nearest-neighbor matching or label transfer. 

Generalized referring queries: GR-LERF and GR-ScanNet additionally include zero-target and multi-target queries. Following ZeroSplat, zero-target queries receive a score of one when the pre-diction is empty and zero otherwise. For the remaining queries, we evaluate the predicted union of target instances against the corresponding ground truth. 16 E VLM and Perception Analysis 

Experimental setup: All methods use only training frames, with fixed method-specific config-urations across VLM backbones. SplatScout uses identical prompts, tools, and execution budgets across models. ZeroSplat follows its published settings, while OpenGround uses a temperature of 0.2. Decoding settings differ between some ZeroSplat runs, and API filtering reduces the evaluable queries in a few runs from 256 to 254–255. 

Ground-truth interventions: We conduct three distinct interventions. For ZeroSplat, GT masks replace segmentation outputs without correcting the VLM’s instance selection. Performance de-creases because incorrect selections can still produce accurate masks of distractors. Separately, GT-guided prompting supplies the correct category and ground-truth bounding boxes while retaining ZeroSplat’s segmentation and lifting pipeline, reaching 0.293 mIoU. For OpenGround, GT table replaces its candidates with official ScanNet++ instances, reaching 0.424 mIoU. However, this table has limited part coverage. Oracle selection instead chooses the highest-overlap candidate from OpenGround’s static table augmented with candidates recovered across four runs, reaching 0.435 mIoU. This oracle is restricted to one candidate per query, including multi-target queries. 

Tool-use behavior: The 30B model struggles to use SplatScout’s feedback effectively: it reaches the execution budget without a valid terminal action on 65 queries, compared with none for 397B. After unsuccessful segmentation, the respective re-segmentation rates are 52% and 69%. These observations help explain the stronger backbone’s larger gains under closed-loop perception. 

## F Ablation and Oracle Details 

Interaction and feedback ablations: All experiments use Qwen3.5-397B with the same tool in-terfaces and execution budget, except for the specified restrictions. In the one-look, one-cut setting, the VLM receives four overview images and makes a single frame selection and segmentation deci-sion without further interaction. Automatic multi-view lifting remains enabled. For the feedback ablations, we suppress one feedback channel at a time while preserving the other two. No visual exploration prevents the Navigator from returning images and disables box-based local inspection, while retaining frame identifiers and other necessary information. No segmentation feedback hides segmentation visualizations and quality diagnostics, returning only the identifiers and status needed for subsequent operations. No lifting feedback similarly suppresses rendered inspection views and lifting-quality feedback while preserving the underlying lifting operations. The VLM remains free to invoke all permitted tools and revise its decisions. 

Compensation between feedback channels: The feedback ablations reveal distinct patterns of tool use. Without visual exploration, segmentation calls increase from 2.5 to 4.6 per query, including 58 attempts to specify segmentation boxes in frames not previously visualized through the Navigator. The number of queries reported as not found increases from 15 to 62, indicating that subsequent feedback cannot fully compensate for missing early visual observations. Without segmentation feedback, the VLM increasingly relies on lifting to inspect its intermediate results. Lifting calls increase from 1.8 to 4.9 per query, while the average interaction length increases from 11.0 to 13.4 steps. Token consumption also increases from 118k to 131k per query, despite the reduced segmentation feedback. This behavior suggests that lifting feedback can partially substitute for direct inspection of segmentation masks, but requires additional interaction. Removing lifting feedback has a smaller effect on overall accuracy. With segmentation feedback still available, the VLM can continue to inspect and revise its 2D predictions. However, part-level mIoU decreases from 0.453 to 0.429, indicating that lifting feedback retains value for some queries. 

Progressive oracle experiments: We progressively replace the model’s decisions with ground truth while retaining the same lifting pipeline. GT boxes replaces predicted boxes and parent scopes with mesh-derived annotations on the model’s selected training frames, while SAM3 performs seg-mentation as usual.
