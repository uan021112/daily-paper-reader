Abstract
Long-sequence panoramic reconstruction requires detailed scene geometry and globally consistent
camera poses across distant revisits. Panoramic SLAM and incremental SfM rely on feature
correspondences, but repeated, similar structures and small partial overlap make reliable long-range
connections difficult to establish, leaving accumulated drift unresolved. Feed-forward models offer
detailed local geometry, but their high computational costs limit inference window size, necessitating
additional constraints for global consistency. This motivates combining continuous feature-based
tracking and sparse mapping with rapid, high-quality local reconstruction from feed-forward
models to achieve accurate localization and globally consistent dense mapping over long sequences.
We therefore propose a panoramic reconstruction framework that couples an ORB-based sparse
panoramic mapping frontend with a fine-tuned feed-forward model. Our key insight is that predicted
local geometry enables 3D verification of feature matches across distant revisits, yielding long-range
camera constraints even between regions. We calibrate predicted depths with sparse landmarks
and use verified connections to optimize the global pose graph, updating dense geometry with the
corrected camera poses. We also introduce PanoRecon, a seven-scene benchmark with repetitive
architecture, large and small loops, and reference camera poses and LiDAR geometry for evaluating
global consistency. Our method achieves the lowest mean absolute trajectory error and high F-1
Score among the evaluated methods. Project Page: HoloRecon.

Introduction
Panoramic reconstruction recovers camera trajectories and dense geometry from equirectangular projection
(ERP) images covering the full surroundings. Along extended routes through buildings and streets, recon-
struction requires detailed surfaces and consistent placement of observations, particularly across distant
revisits.
Feature-based panoramic SLAM and incremental structure-from-motion (SfM) register cameras and maintain
sparse landmarks through image correspondences. SLAM systems use loop closure to correct accumulated drift,
with acceptance thresholds controlling which revisits enter global optimization [2, 28]. Relaxed thresholds
admit false associations, whereas strict thresholds reject valid loops with limited matching support. Repeated
facade textures and symmetric layouts make distinct places visually similar, while distant revisits often
share only partial overlap. False loops distort the map, and missed loops leave drift uncorrected, producing
misaligned structural layers. Reliable global correction of panoramic reconstruction therefore requires stronger
evidence for distinguishing true revisits from repeated scene patterns.
Feed-forward reconstruction rapidly estimates camera poses and detailed local geometry from panoramic views,
as demonstrated by PanoVGGT [8] and panoramic adaptations of Pi3 [34]. Memory and computational costs
limit the number of jointly processed views, while separate inference windows still require global constraints to
remain consistent. This motivates combining continuous feature-based tracking and mapping with rapid local
feed-forward reconstruction for accurate localization and globally consistent reconstruction over long sequences.
Based on the above mentioned discussion, we therefore introduce an incremental panoramic reconstruction
framework that couples ORB-based sparse mapping with a fine-tuned feed-forward panoramic model (Fig. 1).
Our key insight is that the predicted local geometry can enable 3D verification of feature matches across
distant revisits. We first calibrate predicted geometry with sparse landmarks and verify a common relative
transform across views to establish long-range camera constraints. Accepted connections further drive pose-
graph correction, which establishes shared observations for panoramic bundle adjustment with a sparse
differentiation GPU backend. After refinement, we recalibrate cached depths and update each dense point
using its source camera’s corrected pose, without repeating inference.
To evaluate global consistency under these conditions, we further introduce the PanoRecon benchmark,
comprising seven real-world scenes with repeated architectural textures and geometric structures. Every scene
contains multiple revisits separated by long travel distances. We also collect LiDAR data, providing reference
camera poses and dense geometry for evaluating trajectory accuracy and reconstruction consistency across
distant revisits.
Our contributions are as follows:
• We propose a long-range panoramic reconstruction framework that couples feature-based sparse mapping
with feed-forward geometry to recover globally consistent camera trajectories and detailed dense maps.
• We introduce a long-range global connection mechanism that integrates feed-forward panoramic geometry
with feature matching to enable 3D verification and establish camera constraints without requiring shared
sparse landmarks between revisited regions.
• We introduce the PanoRecon benchmark with repetitive architecture and long-range revisits to evaluate
trajectory accuracy and dense reconstruction consistency.

Related Work
2.1
Structure from Motion
SfM recovers cameras and structure through incremental registration [9, 26] or global motion estimation [20,
24, 37]. Resent study extends these operations to spherical imagery [3], including spherical pose estimation
and bundle adjustment [13], dense matching [14], and structural constraints from lines and architectural
layouts [10, 12]. These advances improve registration and geometry estimation. Distant regions with partial
overlap still require reliable associations for global refinement, motivating our use of predicted depths to
support feature correspondences across views.
2


2.2
Visual Odometry and Panoramic SLAM
Visual odometry estimates sequential motion, with direct methods optimizing photometric error [5], while
feature-based, direct, and learned SLAM maintain maps with multi-view constraints [6, 21, 29]. Open-
VSLAM [27] supports purely visual equirectangular mapping. Visual–inertial estimation [25] extends to
panoramic and spherical multi-camera observations [35, 36, 38, 41], while HDPV-SLAM [1] incorporates
LiDAR depth. Their global accuracy depends on distinguishing true revisits from repeated appearance.
2.3
Feed-Forward Reconstruction
Feed-forward geometry and matching models [16, 31, 33, 34] supply dense reconstruction priors. CUT3R
maintains persistent state [32], SLAM3R learns local-to-world registration [17], and StreamVGGT and
InfiniteVGGT use causal memory [42, 45]. MASt3R-SLAM, VGGT-SLAM, VGGT-Long, and VGGT-SLAM
2.0 couple predictions with graph optimization and loop closure [4, 18, 19, 22]. VGGT-Long processes
overlapping chunks, while VGGT-SLAM 2.0 uses attention to verify revisits. For panoramas, PanoVGGT [8]
predicts poses and geometry, while HALO-SLAM [40] exploits attention, and dense geometry for tracking and
loop verification.
3
Method
Given an ERP image sequence, we reconstruct camera poses and dense geometry by combining a ORB-based
sparse map with local feed-forward predictions (Fig. 2). We first calibrates and places the dense predictions
in a common coordinate (Sec. 3.1). We then use these geometries to verify feature matches across views and
construct long-range camera constraints (Sec. 3.2). Optimizing the sparse map with these constraints updates
the cameras and landmarks, which we use to recalibrate and reposition the cached dense points (Sec. 3.3).
3.1
Anchoring Feed-Forward Geometry to the Sparse Map
Local predictions and the sparse map.
An ORB-based ERP frontend [28] incrementally estimates camera-
to-world poses Ti = (Ri, ci) and sparse landmarks Lj, where i indexes keyframes, j indexes landmarks, and
Tix = Rix + ci. We group successive keyframes into overlapping windows and render their panoramas into
calibrated perspective views. Pi3 [34], fine-tuned on Holo360D [23], jointly processes these views to predict
local geometry. Each window defines a submap, whose predicted depths we anchor to the frontend’s cameras
and landmarks.
We additionally replace the per-view sky segmentation network with a lightweight sky head supervised by
Holo360D geometry ground truth. It predicts a sky mask directly on each ERP image and projects to the
perspective views, reducing inference time per submap from 40 s to 13 s.

3.2

each candidate pair, we compute mutual ORB matches across all pairs of keyframes with predicted depths.
Combining matches across image pairs lets us use overlap distributed across views.

Graph correction and observation fusion.

similarity pose graph alongside existing sparse-map constraints. We optimize the graph, remove incompatible

constraints and a limited number of their covisible neighbors. Merging duplicate landmarks establishes
observations shared across revisited regions. We denote the updated camera–landmark observation set by O,
which supplies cross-region reprojection factors for global BA.

The PanoRecon Benchmark
We independently capture panoramic videos and RTK-assisted LiDAR data along the same planned routes in
seven scenes with low-texture surfaces and repeated architecture. Each route includes a large loop and distant
revisits with limited overlap, testing reconstruction methods’ ability to correct accumulated drift.
Reference geometry and camera poses.
We optimize the LiDAR trajectory with RTK corrections and export
the point cloud and calibrated poses of its three onboard cameras. In RealityScan, these define LiDAR
reference images with fixed poses. ERP frames sampled every 0.1 s are split into calibrated perspective rigs
and globally matched to these images. We optimize only the ERP rig poses, then recover one pose per
panorama using the rig extrinsics. The reference poses share the LiDAR’s metric coordinate system. The
mean reprojection RMSE is 0.1917 pixels (Fig. 4). Appendix A.1 details acquisition and processing.
Reconstruction evaluation.
For a reconstruction in frame E, trajectory alignment to the reference poses gives
the similarity SL←E into the LiDAR frame L. We apply it to the reconstructed geometry and refine registration
with ICP. Following Tanks and Temples [15], bidirectional nearest-neighbor distances yield precision, recall,
and F-score. The complete transformation is

Experiments
Implementation.
We process submaps of 16 panoramic keyframes with four shared keyframes between
consecutive submaps. Depth scale calibration uses a log-ratio Huber threshold of 0.05. Connection verification
uses bidirectional distance and bearing thresholds of 0.3 map units and 1◦, with support from at least three
keyframes on each side. GPU bundle adjustment uses double precision and at most 250 PCG iterations with
a tolerance of 10−5. Further settings are provided in Appendix B. All experiments are conducted on a single
NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Workstation Edition GPU.
Datasets.
We use the mapping sequences from all four scenes of 360LOC [11]: Atrium, Concourse, Hall,
and Piatrium. We also evaluate all four sequences (A–D) of YUTO MMS [44], a panoramic dataset covering
20.1 km and the Sequence C alone spans 9.137 km with more than 9,000 ERP images. Finally, we evaluate the
seven PanoRecon scenes introduced in Sec. 4. Both YUTO MMS and the PanoRecon benchmark contain long
trajectories and complex loop structures, which challenge sustained tracking and global drift correction.

Baselines.
We compare against PanoVGGT [8] and Scal3R [39] for feed-forward reconstruction, 360DVO [7],
Open-vSLAM [27], and Stella-vSLAM-Dense [28] for panoramic visual odometry and SLAM, and COLMAP [26]
and Spherical-SfM for SfM (Table 1). Scal3R receives distortion-free perspective views rendered from ERP
images using COLMAP’s official panorama renderer, with a 90◦field of view (front view). We evaluate
COLMAP in two configurations: directly on ERP images with an equirectangular camera model, and on 12
such perspective overlapping views per panorama with known intrinsics and fixed per-ERP rig extrinsics.
Metrics.
We evaluate camera poses after one global Umeyama Sim(3) alignment to the reference. ATE,
translational RPE (RPE-t), and rotational RPE (RPE-r) are reported as RMSE. For dense reconstruction, we
apply the trajectory similarity to the point cloud. We report mean scene-wise precision, recall, and F-score at
20 cm on 360LOC, YUTO MMS, and PanoRecon (Table 2). Appendices D and E provide per-scene results
and evaluation details.
5.1
Trajectory Comparison
Our method achieves the lowest mean ATE on all three datasets, with the largest advantage on YUTO MMS
(Table 1). False loop closures distort ORB-based Stella-vSLAM trajectories on Residence2 and Sequence C,
where buildings share similar appearance and geometry (Fig. 5). Scal3R exhibits scale drift on large scenes
with one perspective view per panorama, as does COLMAP during sequential reconstruction in both ERP
and pinhole configurations. Our trajectories follow the reference street layouts and loop structures. On our
single GPU, PanoVGGT [8] ran out of memory (OOM) on every evaluated scene under the input settings in
Appendix A.2. HALO-SLAM [40] had no public implementation at evaluation time.

Dense Reconstruction Comparison
Accurate camera poses keep local predictions aligned across distant revisits, reducing drift-induced misalignment
in the global point cloud. Our largest F-score gains occur on the long-range YUTO MMS sequences (Table 2),
consistent with the trajectory results. Feed-forward geometry also improves local completeness on weakly
textured walls and reflective surfaces, where photometric matching is unreliable.

Observation fusion and global refinement.
Low reprojection error after local BA can coexist with global drift.
PG reduces ATE while slightly increasing reprojection error, consistent with optimizing camera constraints
before pixel-level refinement (Table 4, Fig. 7). Fusion merges landmark tracks across revisits, increasing
long-range support and changing the reprojection constraints. Subsequent BA reduces residuals and further
improves trajectory accuracy.
Effect of depth scale calibration.
Frame-specific calibration improves the precision–recall balance after
registration, showing the value of accommodating scale variation within a prediction window (Table 6).
Whole-submap alignment retains higher recall. The advantage of per-frame calibration lies in the fidelity of
the registered geometry and F-score.
Multi-view support and geometric verification.
On the selected Residence2 pairs (small overlap long-range
connections), all-frame matching increases support, while balanced groups allow verification to pass at
unchanged thresholds (Table 7). Predicted depth expands 3D support, and pooling views improves correct
recall.

Conclusion
In this paper, we study how local feed-forward geometry can support global consistency in long-sequence
panoramic reconstruction. The local geometry of feed-forward model can provide 3D evidence for verifying
distant feature matches and establishing camera constraints. The key insight is this mutual dependence:
sparse mapping anchors local geometry, and local geometry strengthens the constraints used to correct
the sparse map. Our seven-scene PanoRecon benchmark evaluates trajectory accuracy and reconstruction
consistency under repetitive architecture and long-range revisits using reference poses and LiDAR geometry.
Experiments across 360LOC, YUTO MMS, and PanoRecon show the improved mean ATE and high fidelity
dense reconstruction results.

Limitations and Future Work.
Severe motion blur or prolonged low-texture segments can reduce the reliable
feature observations needed for tracking and depth calibration. Our system also does not explicitly handle
dynamic objects, such as moving vehicles and pedestrians, and does not yet achieve real-time reconstruction.
Future work will focus on more robust correspondences and efficient feed-forward models for streaming
reconstruction from 360◦video in dynamic scenes.

A
Additional Experiment Details
A.1
Datasets.
360LOC.
We use the four daytime mapping sequences of 360LOC [11]: Atrium, Concourse, Hall, and
Piatrium. The released reference split contains 2,244 panoramas (491–632 per scene) at 6144 ×3072 resolution,
with reference poses and LiDAR maps. Their indoor and outdoor settings, symmetric layouts, repeated
structures, and moving pedestrians let us assess reconstruction consistency in visually ambiguous scenes.
For dense evaluation, we back-project the official per-ERP depth maps along spherical viewing rays and
transform the resulting points into the world frame using the official camera poses. Valid points across
mapping frames are merged by voxel averaging to form the reference point cloud.
YUTO MMS.
We evaluate all four sequences (A–D) of YUTO MMS [44], captured by a vehicle-mounted
panoramic camera. The released sequences cover 20.1 km and contain 24,360 panoramas. Sequence C alone
spans 9.137 km over 25 minutes with 10,778 images. The routes traverse campus and residential areas with
large and small loops, frequent turns, and dynamic traffic. This scale and revisit structure let us evaluate
accumulated drift and global consistency over extended trajectories, using reference poses obtained through
offline GPS and LiDAR adjustment.
For dense evaluation, we construct the reference point cloud from the released LiDAR scans, ground-truth
camera poses, and sensor calibration. We apply the calibrated LiDAR-to-camera transform, and accumulate
the transformed scans in a common world frame.
Figure 8 Visualization of ours PanoRecon benchmark ground-truth LiDAR geometry.
Our PanoRecon.
The PanoRecon benchmark evaluates global consistency under repeated architecture and
limited overlap between distant revisits. We select seven scenes with low-texture surfaces and buildings that
share similar geometry and appearance (Fig. 8). Each planned route contains a large loop, smaller loops, and
repeated visits to the same buildings after long travel distances and time intervals. At revisits, the buildings
are viewed from farther away or at oblique angles and occupy only a small region of the ERP image. The
resulting limited overlap tests whether reconstruction methods can establish long-range connections and
correct accumulated drift.
13


Data acquisition. We independently acquire panoramic video and LiDAR data along the same planned route in
each scene. The panoramic recordings use an Insta360 X4 at 5K resolution and 30 fps. We mount the camera
on a 3 m extension pole to reduce the operator’s presence in the ERP images. We export the recordings as
ERP videos and sample 2K frames every 0.1 s. The separate LiDAR survey uses RTK positioning and three
onboard cameras for point-cloud colorization.
Data processing. The two acquisition sessions are registered through reference images tied to the LiDAR
coordinate system. We first post-process the LiDAR SLAM trajectory with RTK differential corrections
and export the resulting point cloud, the three onboard cameras’ poses, and their intrinsic and extrinsic
calibration. We import these data into RealityScan to render LiDAR reference images, whose camera poses
remain fixed throughout registration.
Each ERP frame is split into perspective views with known intrinsics, a shared optical center, and fixed
relative rotations. We import these views as a calibrated rig and globally match them to the fixed LiDAR
reference images. Pose optimization updates only the panoramic rigs, with the reference cameras and rig
calibration held fixed. We then convert the estimated perspective-view poses back to one ERP pose per frame
using the rig extrinsics. This yields reference panoramic poses and LiDAR geometry in the same metric
coordinate system.
A.2
Baselines Settings.
All methods use 2K input images except PanoVGGT and Scal3R, which use their official default resizing
settings.
Input sequences and evaluation frames.
On 360LOC, SLAM methods process all mapping images and retain
frame-wise poses for evaluation. On PanoRecon, SLAM methods use all images for localization and retain
evaluation keyframes at 1 s intervals. On YUTO MMS, SLAM methods process the full image sequences, and
evaluation poses for both SLAM and feed-forward methods are sampled every three images. For these vehicle
sequences with varying speeds, sampling follows frame indices. SfM methods use all 360LOC mapping images,
sample PanoRecon inputs every 1 s, and use every 3 images, following the corresponding evaluation sampling
intervals.
Feed-forward inference.
Feed-forward methods receive the full YUTO MMS sequences, except that Scal3R
uses every third image on Sequences C and D, where full-sequence inputs cause GPU out-of-memory (OOM)
errors. PanoVGGT encounters OOM on every evaluated scene with its official default resizing and our
single-GPU setup.

Solver settings and map updates.
The GPU solver uses the precision and PCG settings in Implementation,
with trust-region damping and at most 100 outer iterations. After at least two iterations, relative objective
improvement below 10−3 stops refinement. Accepted steps have non-increasing objective values, and the final
solution updates the camera poses and landmarks. The CPU backend uses analytic ERP Jacobians, g2o
robust linearization, and a CSparse direct solver with OpenMP support. Initialization and local BA use the
CPU backend, while the selected backend handles global BA.
B.3.3
Dense reconstruction after global refinement
Updated poses and landmarks change both dense point positions and their depth references. For each affected
window, we recheck its landmark observations, recompute Eq. (7), refit Eq. (2), and adjust overlapping
predictions through their common samples. Equation (1) then places the cached predictions using the updated
poses and scales, yielding the realigned geometry in Fig. 9(e).
At each submap boundary, accepted connections trigger graph correction, landmark fusion, and global BA
before dense updates. Scheduled global BA also updates dense geometry. Frame input resumes once these
operations finish.
