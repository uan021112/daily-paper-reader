Abstract
World-action models (WAMs) offer a promising framework for embodied intelligence,
yet mainstream approaches remain centered on video foundation models or vision-
language models. Even when geometric features extracted by 3D models such as VGGT
are incorporated, they are typically passed into a video model or VLM that serves as
the primary backbone, leaving 3D geometry as an auxiliary input or prediction target
rather than the native foundation for world modeling. We present E0.5, a geometry-
native world-action model built directly on 3D foundation models. E0.5 introduces
two key innovations. First, it adopts a 3D foundation model as the primary backbone,
making 3D geometry the native representation for both world modeling and action
prediction. Second, it explicitly predicts future 3D states together with actions, enabling
the model to capture geometric world dynamics beyond action-only modeling or future
video prediction. Experiments on RoboTwin 2.0 first demonstrate the effectiveness of
E0.5 without embodied-data pretraining, achieving success rates of 89.90% and 50.09%
under clean and randomized settings, respectively. Building on these results, we further
pretrain E0.5 on more than 160k robot trajectories from AgiBotWorld-Beta, jointly learning
robot actions and future geometry within the pretrained 3D foundation. This embodied
pretraining improves mean real-world manipulation success rates by approximately
15 and 7 percentage points on AgileX Piper and Zerith H1, respectively. Together,
these results demonstrate the potential of geometry-native foundations to acquire robot
interaction knowledge and transfer it to physical manipulation. E0.5 embodies this
geometry-native approach as a member of the VWA family of world-action models.
1. Introduction
Robotic manipulation requires understanding not only the current spatial structure of a scene,
but also how that structure may change as the robot acts. World-action models (WAMs) provide
a framework for learning this relationship by jointly modeling robot actions and future world
states (Song et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2026). A central design question is therefore what kind of
representation should underpin this joint modeling of actions and their physical consequences.
Mainstream WAMs build on video foundation models or vision-language models, leveraging
their capabilities in visual generation and semantic understanding. Although these models can
incorporate geometric information, their primary computation remains organized around a
video or vision-language backbone, rather than a native geometric foundation. This distinction
motivates us to investigate an alternative: building WAMs directly on 3D foundation models,
so that action generation and future-state prediction are learned within a shared geometric
backbone.

Introducing 3D information into an existing model only partially addresses this requirement.
Recent methods incorporate features extracted by geometric models into a vision-language
or video backbone, or use these features as auxiliary supervision for the backbone’s represen-
tations (Yang et al., 2026c; Guo et al., 2025). Such approaches enrich spatial understanding,
but retain a vision-language or video foundation model as the primary backbone, rather than
building directly on a 3D foundation model. More generally, geometry can serve as an additional
input or prediction target without becoming the representational foundation for world modeling
and action generation. We investigate a complementary approach: making a 3D foundation
model the core backbone, training it to predict geometric futures together with actions, and
expanding its capacity within this geometric modeling framework.
We present E0.5, a geometry-native world-action model built directly on 3D foundation
models. Following this principle, E0.5 brings together two key innovations: 3D foundation
backbones that provide native geometric representations, explicit future 3D state prediction coupled
with action generation. These components jointly define the model’s representational basis and
predictive objective. Figure 1 summarizes these distinctions, contrasting E0.5 with video and
VLM-centered models.
First, E0.5 adopts a 3D foundation model as its primary backbone, placing geometric represen-
tations at the center of both world modeling and action prediction. The geometric backbone
serves as the core model on which these capabilities are learned, extending the role of 3D
models beyond front-end feature extraction or auxiliary supervision. Our implementation uses
VGGT-Omega (Wang et al., 2026a) as an example of a pretrained 3D foundation model. While
such models recover the geometry of observed scenes, E0.5 develops this foundation into a
predictive geometric backbone that learns future world states jointly with robot actions.
Second, E0.5 explicitly predicts future 3D states together with actions. Reconstructing current
geometry provides information about the scene at the time of observation; predicting future ge-
ometry additionally requires modeling how its spatial configuration evolves during interaction.
This distinction aligns the geometric objective with the predictive role of a WAM. It also offers
an alternative to future video prediction, whose targets encompass both geometric changes and
2


variations in visual appearance. Our hypothesis is that directly supervising future 3D states
encourages the backbone to learn spatial structure and dynamics that remain useful across
environments, supporting generalization when observations differ from those encountered
during training.
This design also advances the use of 3D backbones beyond current-state reconstruction and
restricted adaptation. VGA (Song et al., 2026b) uses a native 3D backbone with current-scene
geometric supervision and LoRA adaptation; its ablations report substantial degradation under
full-parameter training. E0.5 instead unfreezes the geometric backbone and jointly optimizes it
for future 3D prediction and action generation. Our experiments demonstrate that backbone
optimization is effective in this formulation, allowing pretrained geometric representations to
adapt to predictive world-action modeling. Thus, the distinction concerns both what the backbone
predicts and how its representations are learned.
These findings further motivate us to investigate whether a geometry-native foundation can
acquire broad robot interaction knowledge while retaining its pretrained geometric representa-
tions. The strong performance observed in simulation, achieved without large-scale embodied
pretraining, suggests that 3D foundation models provide an effective starting point for robot
action learning. Building on this observation, we extend E0.5 to large-scale pretraining on
AgiBotWorld-Beta, where robot actions and future geometric states are jointly modeled. Rather
than adapting the backbone solely for action prediction, we continue to supervise future geome-
try throughout pretraining, allowing the geometric foundation to learn manipulation-relevant
dynamics under explicit spatial constraints. We subsequently evaluate this pretrained model on
distinct physical robot embodiments to examine how large-scale robot knowledge improves
real-world manipulation.
Experiments on widely used simulation benchmarks and real-robot tasks demonstrate the
effectiveness of E0.5. Predicting future 3D states improves the average success rate by 9.43
percentage points over the variant without geometric reconstruction and by 7.93 percentage
points over current-state reconstruction, highlighting the benefits of predictive geometric su-
pervision. The advantage is especially pronounced under distribution shifts: in our evaluated
out-of-distribution (OOD) setting, E0.5 achieves approximately 30 times the accuracy of the video-
prediction-based WAM baselines. Together, these results establish 3D foundation backbones
and future geometry prediction as an effective basis for generalizable world-action modeling.
Our contributions are twofold:
• World-action models built on 3D foundations. We introduce E0.5, which adopts 3D
foundation models as its primary backbones and develops their geometric representations
for joint world modeling and action prediction.
• Explicit future 3D state prediction. We jointly learn future geometry and actions while
optimizing the geometric backbone, achieving approximately 10-percentage-point im-
provement over current-state reconstruction and strong OOD generalization relative to
video-prediction-based WAMs.
2. Related Work
Vision-Language-Action and Video-Based World-Action Models.
Vision-language-action
(VLA) models transfer pretrained visual and linguistic representations to robot control (Zhan
et al., 2026b,a; Zhou et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b). OpenVLA (Kim et al., 2024) adapts a vision-
language backbone using large-scale robot demonstrations, while 𝜋0.5 (Intelligence et al., 2025)
3


combines heterogeneous robot data, web data, and semantic supervision to improve open-world
generalization. These approaches demonstrate the value of broad pretraining and multimodal
knowledge transfer, with vision-language models providing the principal representational
foundation for action generation. Despite these advances, strong benchmark performance does
not necessarily translate into robust real-world generalization, with recent evaluations revealing
sensitivity to physical perturbations and limitations in spatial reasoning (Chen et al., 2026c).
A complementary line of research couples action learning with future visual prediction.
Unified Video Action Model (Li et al., 2025) learns a shared video-action latent representation
with separate diffusion decoders, while Video Policy (Liang et al., 2025) jointly trains video and
action generation and leverages action-free videos for policy generalization. PAR (Song et al.,
2025, 2026a) and DreamZero (Ye et al., 2026) builds a world-action model on a pretrained video
diffusion backbone, jointly predicting future video and actions to transfer learned dynamics to
new tasks and environments. These methods establish visual prediction as a useful source of
supervision for embodied behavior. E0.5 shares the objective of learning actions together with
future world states, but changes both the representational foundation and the prediction target:
a 3D foundation model serves as the primary backbone, and future states are explicitly modeled
through geometry rather than video appearance.
Geometric Foundations and Future 3D Prediction.
Learning 3D structure from visual evi-
dence is an important foundation for spatial intelligence (Wang et al., 2023; Shen and Wang,
2024; He et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2024a; Feng et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026a;
Xiang et al., 2025). More broadly, geometric foundation models learn transferable spatial repre-
sentations. DUSt3R (Wang et al., 2024b) regresses point maps from image pairs to reconstruct
scene geometry. VGGT (Wang et al., 2025) and VGGT-Omega (Wang et al., 2026a) jointly esti-
mates multiple geometric quantities from visual observations. Their reconstruction-oriented
pretraining offers a geometric starting point for embodied learning. E0.5 builds on this class of
models, extending their role from recovering observed geometry to learning geometric futures
jointly with robot actions.
Several approaches introduce these geometric priors into existing VLA architectures. Yang
et al. (2026c) investigate geometric feature fusion and representation alignment within VLAs,
while GLaD (Guo et al., 2025) distills features from a frozen geometric teacher into the language
backbone’s visual-token representations. Such methods incorporate geometric knowledge while
retaining a vision-language-centered policy architecture. Geometry also appears as a generative
target: 3D-VLA (Zhen et al., 2024) connects a 3D-aware language model with diffusion models
that generate goal images and point clouds. Thus, using 3D information or generating geometric
goals is distinct from making a geometric foundation model the primary, trainable backbone for
world-action modeling.
VGA (Song et al., 2026b) is more closely related in its use of a native geometric backbone
for action generation. Its geometric supervision reconstructs the observed scene, and it uses
LoRA adaptation to preserve pretrained backbone weights; its ablations report substantially
lower performance with full-parameter training under the evaluated configuration. E0.5 instead
explicitly predicts future 3D states and actions while unfreezing and jointly optimizing the
geometric backbone. The distinction is therefore not simply the presence of a 3D backbone, but
its transformation into a trainable predictive world-action model. Our comparison of future-
state prediction with current-state reconstruction directly evaluates this distinction, while the
OOD experiments examine its implications for generalization.

3. Method
E0.5 is designed around a single principle: the foundation used to generate actions should itself
learn to anticipate geometric change. This principle unifies our two key contributions. A 3D
foundation model provides the primary representation for world-action modeling, while future
geometry supervision adapts this representation to anticipate spatial changes induced by robot
actions. We therefore develop E0.5 as a future-predictive geometric foundation that jointly supports
world modeling and action generation. Figure 2 shows how future geometry supervision and
flow-matching action generation are coupled within one fully trainable geometric foundation.

Prediction-driven, full-parameter adaptation.
Future prediction supervises the backbone,
not only a decoder attached to fixed features. We jointly optimize the geometric backbone,
input-conditioning modules, geometric prediction heads, and action decoder, while keeping
the pretrained language encoder frozen. Action learning shapes the representation for control,
while future geometry supervision provides an additional structural learning signal throughout
the trainable geometric backbone.
This distinguishes E0.5 from using a native 3D backbone for current-scene reconstruction
with restricted adaptation, as in VGA (Song et al., 2026b). E0.5 combines a future-state objective
with full-parameter world-action learning: the geometric foundation is adapted to anticipation
as well as perception. The design therefore changes both the temporal target and the trainable
representation, rather than merely replacing a prediction head.
The same joint learning formulation also provides a natural mechanism for adapting pre-
trained 3D foundations to large-scale robot data. Starting from a geometry-pretrained backbone,
E0.5 jointly optimizes future geometric prediction and robot action generation without freezing
the geometric backbone. The geometric objective continues to provide structural supervision as
the model learns manipulation behaviors from robot trajectories. This allows embodied learning
to remain grounded in the pretrained geometric representation, rather than relying solely on
action supervision during adaptation.

4. Experiment
4.1. Implementation Details
We use VGGT-Omega (Wang et al., 2026a) as the geometry backbone, including its depth and
camera decoding heads, and initialize it with pretrained weights to provide a strong foundation
for robotic spatial perception. The action expert follows the DiT architecture of WAN2.2 (Team
Wan et al., 2025), with the hidden dimension reduced to 1024. VGGT-Omega and the action
expert are coupled through a mixture-of-transformers (MoT) architecture, while the first and last
three layers of the action expert operate independently without interacting with the geometry
backbone. This design improves performance, as demonstrated in our ablation study. We use a
frozen T5 encoder to provide language conditioning to both the action expert and the geometry
backbone.
We set the action horizon to ℎ= 32. Following WAN2.2, we adopt a logit-normal distribution
over 𝑡as the noise schedule during both training and inference. At inference time, we use 10
denoising steps with a classifier-free guidance (CFG) scale of 1.0. Across all training configu-
rations, we optimize all trainable modules using AdamW with a learning rate of 1 × 10−4, a
weight decay of 0.01, and a cosine annealing learning rate schedule. The loss weight for future
geometry prediction is set to 0.01.

Main Results.
Table 1 reports the results on RoboTwin 2.0 under both clean and randomized
evaluation settings. Despite its comparatively small 2.32B-parameter policy core and the absence
of embodied-data pretraining, E0.5 achieves 89.90% and 50.09% success rates in the Clean

4.5. Ablation Study
Action Adapter Depth.
We investigate how the depth of action-only layers before and after
geometry interaction affects policy performance. Keeping the 24 layer-wise interactions between
the action expert and the geometry backbone unchanged, we place 𝑘∈{0, 3, 6} action Trans-
former layers before and after the interaction modules, resulting in action experts with 24, 30,
and 36 layers, respectively. These additional layers do not directly interact with the geometry
backbone and are referred to as action adapters. To reduce experimental cost, all three variants
are trained without geometric reconstruction supervision.
As shown in Table 4, the variant with three adapter layers at each end achieves the highest
mean success rates, reaching 73.22% and 19.84% under the Clean and Randomized settings,
respectively. Increasing the adapter depth to six layers at each end reduces these rates to
69.44% and 14.66%, indicating that additional action-only layers do not consistently improve
performance under the given training budget. We hypothesize that a moderate adapter depth
facilitates action representation transformation before geometry interaction and refines action
predictions after fusion. Further analysis is needed to verify this interpretation. These results
provide empirical support for using three adapter layers at each end.

Reconstruction Target.
To investigate the effect of the reconstruction target, we compare
three settings: no reconstruction loss, reconstructing the current geometry, and reconstructing
future geometry. All three variants are trained for 5 epochs to ensure an equal training budget.
While the main results are obtained with a larger training budget, this ablation compares the
effectiveness of different reconstruction targets under a fixed training budget. The absolute
scores should therefore not be directly compared with the main results.
As shown in Table 6, reconstructing the current geometry improves performance over
the variant without reconstruction loss by 2.72 and 0.28 percentage points across the two
settings, respectively. Reconstructing future geometry yields larger gains of 9.50 and 9.36
percentage points over the variant without reconstruction loss, and further outperforms current-
geometry reconstruction by 6.78 and 9.08 percentage points. These results suggest that future-
geometry reconstruction provides a more effective training signal under the same training
budget, supporting its use as our reconstruction objective.

Auxiliary DiT reconstruction versus native future geometry learning.
Existing geometry-
aware world-action models introduce dedicated DiT-based depth prediction branches to incor-
porate future geometry into world and action modeling. For example, X-WAM (Guo et al., 2026b)
constructs a depth branch by replicating the final blocks of a pretrained video DiT. Motivated by
this design, we introduce an auxiliary DepthDiT into our framework to predict future depth
through flow matching. We compare this variant against a baseline without reconstruction and
a variant that uses native geometry reconstruction heads. This comparison examines whether
adding a dedicated future depth generator translates into improved action learning in our
framework. All variants use the same training and evaluation configurations, but their training
configuration differs from that used in the paper’s main experiments.
As shown in Table 7, the baseline without reconstruction achieves 73.22% and 19.84% success
rates in the Clean and Randomized settings, respectively. Adding the auxiliary DepthDiT
yields 72.60% and 17.20%, corresponding to decreases of 0.62 and 2.64 percentage points. Thus,
introducing an additional future depth prediction objective does not improve average task
success under our experimental settings.
In comparison, applying future geometry supervision directly to language-conditioned
visual features through native geometry reconstruction heads achieves 82.72% success on Clean
and 29.20% on Random. These results exceed the baseline by 9.50 and 9.36 percentage points,
and the auxiliary DepthDiT variant by 10.12 and 12.00 percentage points, respectively. The
native-head variant improves over the baseline on 38 out of 50 Clean tasks and 44 out of 50
Random tasks, indicating that the gains extend across most tasks.
These results support the value of native joint representation learning for future geometry
and action. In this formulation, the same VGGT features receive future geometry supervision
and provide visual conditioning for action prediction, directly tying geometric learning to the
representations used by the policy. Compared with introducing an auxiliary depth generation
expert, this approach is more effective in our framework, with particularly pronounced gains
under environmental randomization. The comparison supports this design at the system level;
it does not establish that dedicated DiT depth prediction is ineffective in other world-action
models.
5. Conclusion
We presented E0.5, a geometry-native world-action model that extends reconstruction-oriented
3D foundations toward future-predictive modeling for robotic manipulation. Rather than treat-
ing geometry as auxiliary information within video or vision-language backbones, E0.5 builds
directly on a trainable 3D foundation model and jointly learns robot actions and future geometric
states. Experiments on RoboTwin 2.0 demonstrate the effectiveness of this formulation without
embodied-data pretraining, achieving success rates of 89.90% and 50.09% under clean and ran-
domized settings, respectively. Predicting future geometry further improves the average success
rate by 7.93 percentage points over current-state reconstruction, highlighting the importance of
anticipating spatial changes rather than merely recovering observed geometry. Building on these
results, we extend E0.5 through large-scale embodied pretraining on AgiBotWorld-Beta, where
continued geometric supervision accompanies the learning of robot interaction knowledge. This
pretraining improves real-world manipulation success rates by 15.0 and 6.7 percentage points on
AgileX Piper and Zerith H1, respectively. Together, these findings demonstrate the potential of
3D foundation models to evolve from geometric reconstruction toward predictive world-action
modeling and transferable robotic manipulation.

A. Pretraining Settings
A.1. Dataset and Preprocessing
We pretrain on AgiBotWorld-Beta (Bu et al., 2025), a large-scale dataset of real-world robot
manipulation demonstrations collected using mobile dual-arm robots. The dataset covers
diverse objects and environments, with tasks ranging from fine-grained manipulation and tool
use to long-horizon activities and multi-robot collaboration. The official collection spans 217
tasks and 87 skills across more than 100 scenarios in five application domains, providing a broad
foundation for learning manipulation policies.
For this pretraining, after required-modality checks and filtering, the training corpus contains
177 tasks, 160,010 trajectories, and 285,728,736 synchronized time steps. At a sampling rate
of 30 Hz, this corresponds to approximately 2,645.64 hours of trajectory data. Each time step
provides head, left-wrist, and right-wrist views together with the corresponding robot state and
21


camera parameters. Duration is measured on the synchronized trajectory timeline; the three
views represent simultaneous observations.
Admission requires readable RGB from all three views, valid joint and gripper states, corre-
sponding depth, and camera calibration supported by the processing pipeline. Trajectories with
missing required modalities, invalid state values, or unsupported calibration are excluded, and
identical duplicate annotations are collapsed. Episodes with optional end-effector-field issues
may be retained when their joint-space training inputs remain valid, with the relevant validity
information recorded. All admitted trajectories are used for pretraining without a separate
validation split.
The original data provide sensor depth for the head camera but lack depth observations
for both wrist cameras. Moreover, the available head-camera depth maps contain substantial
holes and noisy measurements, including missing regions around the robot arms, leaving
manipulation-relevant areas without valid geometric supervision. To address both the missing
wrist-camera depth and the incomplete head-camera depth, we generate depth pseudo-labels
for all three views offline over complete RGB videos using DA3METRIC-LARGE from Depth
Anything 3 (Lin et al., 2025). This provides denser depth targets through a uniform estimation
procedure across views; these targets constitute estimated geometric supervision rather than
sensor-measured ground truth. Predictions are aligned with robot states using source frame
indices and timestamps and stored together with camera parameters. RGB and depth undergo
consistent rectification. Original camera extrinsics are inverted as complete rigid transforms to
obtain robot-reference-to-camera transformations, while intrinsics are adjusted to the resized
image coordinates. Depth is encoded losslessly with 16-bit values in millimeters, converted to
meters during loading, and accompanied by validity masks.
The processed corpus is stored in LeRobot v3.0 format. RGB observations are stored as
videos; robot states, action labels, and camera parameters are stored in Parquet, and PNG16
depth bytes are embedded in the same dataset format. Shared episode identifiers and frame
indices provide aligned access across modalities. Joint and gripper values are standardized
using fixed per-dimension means and standard deviations computed before training.
The depth targets are model-generated pseudo-labels, and the three-dimensional targets
are derived from those predictions and dataset-provided calibration. Their accuracy therefore
depends on both depth estimation and camera calibration.
A.2. Training Samples and Prediction Targets
Samples are constructed as consecutive temporal windows within each trajectory. A trajectory
of length 𝑇provides 𝑇−32 valid windows with a stride of one. Each window spans the current
state and the following 32 time steps, without crossing an episode boundary. The corpus yields
280,608,416 valid training windows, which are globally shuffled each epoch and distributed
across training processes.
Model inputs comprise the three current RGB views, the current 16-dimensional proprio-
ceptive state, and task-language features. Images are stored at 480 × 640 and transformed to an
actual input resolution of 224 × 288 by VGGT-Omega (Wang et al., 2026a) balanced preprocess-
ing; dimensions are stated as height × width. Language features are extracted offline with the
pretrained UMT5-XXL (Chung et al., 2023) encoder, using at most 128 tokens per instruction and
4096 features per token. Cached features are read during training, and the text encoder remains
fixed.
22


Action labels are constructed from the next observed robot state. Denoting the current state
by 𝑠𝑡, the action target is 𝑎𝑡= 𝑠𝑡+1) , and the complete target sequence spans 𝑠𝑡+1 through 𝑠𝑡+32,
with shape 32 × 16. These targets represent absolute future joint positions and gripper values
before per-dimension normalization. Only the current state is supplied as proprioceptive input;
subsequent states are used to construct supervision. The action objective uses continuous flow
matching. Normalized action sequences are mixed with Gaussian noise at randomly sampled
noise levels, and the model predicts the flow from actions to noise using a timestep-weighted
mean-squared error. The loss is aggregated over action dimensions and valid time steps within
the 32-step horizon. The scheduler uses 1000 nominal training timesteps, with both training and
inference shifts set to 5.0.
Geometric supervision is applied at the final time step 𝑡+ 32, approximately 1.067 seconds
into the future. It consists of three-view depth and camera parameters, together with three-
dimensional points obtained by unprojection. The model predicts this future geometry from
current observations. Future RGB and target geometry are not conditioning inputs. The
geometry objective uses the official VGGT-Omega (Wang et al., 2026a) loss implementation to
supervise future camera parameters, depth, and three-dimensional points. Camera, depth, and
point losses each have an internal weight of 5.0, with an overall geometry weight of 0.01. The
depth objective includes confidence modeling and a separate regression term. Point supervision
is computed by unprojecting depth with camera parameters, coupling the two predictions
without an independent point-cloud prediction head.
Before loss computation, geometry targets are expressed in the first camera’s reference
frame and normalized by a per-sample scale derived from the mean distance of valid three-
dimensional points. Point unprojection uses target intrinsics during the first 60% of training and
predicted focal lengths with target principal points thereafter. Invalid depth pixels are masked,
and geometric losses are computed in FP32. These operations preserve the relationship between
cameras, depth, and points within the joint learning objective.
A.3. Architecture and Initialization
The model consists of a single language-conditioned VGGT-Omega (Wang et al., 2026a) visual
stream and a 30-layer ActionDiT action stream. The visual stream processes the three current
images and integrates language information within cross-view attention, producing visual
features for action prediction and features for future-geometry prediction. The action stream
combines visual features, language, current proprioception, and a noisy action sequence to learn
action generation.
The streams are connected through layer-wise attention bridges. Twenty-four VGGT layers
are paired with ActionDiT layers (from 3 to 26 using zero-based indices), and each bridge
has independent projection parameters. ActionDiT uses a hidden dimension of 1024, an FFN
dimension of 4096, and 24 attention heads with 128 dimensions per head. VGGT uses a feature
width of 1024 and 16 attention heads.
We initialize the VGGT aggregator and camera/depth heads with pretrained weights, while
ActionDiT is randomly initialized. The attention bridges, proprioceptive encoder, and language
projection are jointly optimized during training. The VGGT backbone remains fully trainable,
receiving gradients from both action and geometry objectives.

A.4. Optimization and Distributed Training
Training uses 7 servers, each equipped with eight PRO6000 GPUs with 96 GB of memory,
for a total of 56 GPUs. Distributed execution uses Accelerate and DeepSpeed ZeRO Stage
1 (Rajbhandari et al., 2020) with BF16 mixed precision and no parameter or optimizer CPU
offloading. A per-GPU micro-batch size of eight and two gradient-accumulation steps yield an
effective global batch size of 896 for regular optimizer updates.
Optimization uses AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017) with 𝛽1 = 0.9, 𝛽2 = 0.95, weight
decay of 0.01, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. Both the action-related and VGGT parameter
groups use a peak learning rate of 1 × 10−4. A linear warmup over the first 2000 optimizer
updates is followed by cosine decay to 1 × 10−6.
The pretraining budget is two epochs. At the current dataset size and batch configuration,
each epoch contains 313,180 optimizer updates, giving a total of 626,360 updates. A step denotes
an optimizer update after gradient accumulation. Distributed sampling introduces a small
amount of epoch-tail padding, and the final update of an epoch may contain fewer than two
micro-batches. The full budget defines both the learning-rate schedule and the geometry-loss
schedule.
Each GPU process uses 12 data-loading workers with prefetching and persistent workers.
The reader combines Parquet column projection with bounded caches, decoding current RGB
on demand and loading the required consecutive robot labels and geometry. Full training states
are saved every 2000 updates, and standalone model weights are exported every 10,000 updates.
Resumption restores model and optimizer states, the learning-rate schedule, sampler position,
and random-number-generator states to preserve training continuity.
B. Task Descriptions
This section describes the tasks and evaluation settings used in our simulation and real-world
experiments. We distinguish the task objective, the demonstration procedure, and the evaluation
criterion. In particular, demonstration procedures specify how data are collected, whereas
success rate and completion score measure the task outcomes defined below.
B.1. Simulation Tasks and Evaluation Settings
Training details.
We train E0.5 on the clean RoboTwin dataset, containing 2,500 demonstration
episodes and 549,787 frames. The policy receives three synchronized RGB views from the head,
left-wrist, and right-wrist cameras, together with a 14-dimensional proprioceptive state and a
language instruction. The original images have a resolution of 240 × 320 and undergo the VGGT
spatial preprocessing pipeline with a target image size of 256. Actions and proprioceptive states
are standardized using dataset-level z-score statistics. Language conditioning uses cached text
embeddings with a maximum context length of 128 tokens. The policy predicts chunks of 32
actions, each with 14 dimensions. All demonstrations are used for training, with no held-out
validation split.
Training is distributed over 32 GPUs across four nodes using DeepSpeed ZeRO Stage 1. The
batch size is eight per GPU, with no gradient accumulation, yielding an effective global batch
size of 256.The full training schedule comprises 70 epochs, or 150,360 optimizer updates. Results
reported in table 8 correspond to the checkpoint at 109,000 updates, equivalent to approximately
50.75 epochs.

During training, domain randomization is applied to the current VGGT RGB inputs with
probability 0.8. Photometric augmentation includes brightness, contrast, saturation, hue, gamma,
exposure, color-temperature variation, Gaussian noise, and Gaussian blur. The brightness,

sampled from [0.7, 1.4], exposure from [−0.5, 0.5], and the color-temperature magnitude is 0.15.
Gaussian noise has a standard deviation of 0.03; blur uses 𝜎∈[0, 1.5] with probability 0.3.

[0.5, 1.5] and an 8 × 8 envelope with strength in [0, 0.6]. Augmentation parameters are sampled
independently across cameras. Actions, proprioceptive states, language inputs, and geometry
supervision are not augmented.

B.2. Real-World Task Suite and Evaluation Protocol
We evaluate real-world manipulation on the AgileX Piper dual-arm system and the Zerith
H1 humanoid robot. Each platform is evaluated on three tabletop tasks: mouse placement,
charger packing, and trash disposal. The corresponding task identifiers are mouse_operation,
cable_storage, and trash_disposal. These tasks cover object placement, charger-and-cable
containment, and repeated object transfer.
Post-training and evaluation.
Policies are post-trained separately for each platform using
200 demonstrations per task. The resulting 600 demonstrations are combined for five epochs
of multitask post-training. We evaluate each method with and without large-scale robot-data
pretraining. For each method and pretraining regime, we conduct 10 trials per task and setting
on each platform. This gives 60 trials per platform and 120 trials across the two platforms.
Evaluation settings.
Each task is evaluated under in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution
(OOD) conditions, corresponding to the EASY and HARD settings. ID trials use objects and
initial configurations within the training distribution. OOD trials introduce unseen mouse poses,
unfamiliar chargers and cables, and larger trash collections containing unseen objects. The task
objectives and scoring criteria are shared across the two settings. Table 9 summarizes the task
objectives and OOD conditions.
