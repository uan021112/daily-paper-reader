Abstract
Novel view synthesis and 3D reconstruction have advanced
rapidly with radiance fields and splatting, yet their mem-
ory and computational demands limit their use in resource-
constrained settings. We introduce Hexels: novel planar
hexagonal surface elements with a first-order spatial color
field for efficient 3D scene and 2D image representation.
Hexels exploit locally planar regions with smoothly vary-
ing appearance using fewer, larger primitives. Their hexag-
onal support provides an area- and boundary-efficient do-
main, while the local color field captures appearance varia-
tion within each primitive. Across 34 scenes, Hexels improve
PSNR over memory-matched 2DGS by 4.53–7.38 dB across
datasets while requiring lower training VRAM. We further ab-
late Hexels by showing that per primitive, Hexels outperform
2DGS by upto 4.74 dB while using lower training VRAM and
having 2.31× faster FPS. Even in unconstrained settings, Hex-
els offers complementary quality-memory trade-offs. For effi-
cient 3D surface reconstruction, Hexels reduce error by 28.9%
compared with memory-matched 2DGS across 15 DTU scans.
On 6 Tanks and Temples surface scenes, Hexels achieves the
highest mean F1 among compared methods (34.38%, versus
31.72% for 2DGS). We further also show that Hexels, as a gen-
eral primitive, improves 2D image representation over Image-
GS. Hexels push the Pareto frontier on the quality-efficiency
trade-off of surface-aligned primitives.
Keywords: Surface elements, hexagonal primitives, novel view synthesis, surface reconstruction, image representation

1 Introduction
Novel view synthesis and 3D reconstruction have progressed
rapidly, from neural radiance fields [25] to explicit Gaussian
primitives that render in real time [18] to surface aligned pla-
nar primitives like 2DGS [14], and Triangle Splatting [13]. These
methods differ in geometry, but all face the same question: how
much of the scene can one primitive represent, and at what cost?
This has led to the search for an overarching universal primi-
tive which is efficient, expressive, and retains high geometric fi-
delity. This is even more important in resource-constrained set-
tings, where efficiency includes not only rendering speed and
model size but also the memory required during training. Pre-
vious methods have often traded one aspect for another. For ex-
ample, conventional Gaussian and triangle splats typically rep-
resent one view-conditioned color per primitive, so spatial vari-
ation emerges through many overlapping elements, leading to
large overdraw. Others like BBSplat [33] add per-primitive tex-
tures, or use shared neural appearance fields like NeST [39] and
Nexels [30]. These methods increase per-element capacity, but
move complexity into textures, grids, or decoders. In contrast,
we target the entire quality–efficiency trade-off at the primitive
itself. For resource-constrained settings, this efficiency includes
not only rendering speed and model size, but also the memory
required during training.
At constrained representation budgets, we seek a balance
between geometric fidelity and appearance expressivity. Since
agreement with training images alone does not guarantee accu-
rate geometry [36], we therefore target both surface reconstruc-
tion accuracy and novel-view rendering quality within a compact
surface primitive.
We are motivated by the idea that natural images and scenes
are highly structured: large regions are often locally planar with
smoothly varying appearance. An efficient surface primitive
should therefore be able to exploit this underlying structure,
wherever possible, and do so in a compact and self-contained
manner.
We introduce Hexels, planar surface elements that combine
a hexagonal support with a first-order spatial color field. Natu-
ral scenes contain large regions that are locally simple in geom-
etry and appearance; a Hexel can represent such a region with
one large primitive, including smooth color variation within it,
while smaller Hexels capture boundaries, curvature, and fine de-
tail. This directly targets redundancy in surface-aligned splatting:
rather than approximating structured regions with many over-
lapping, spatially constant primitives, Hexels increase what each
primitive can represent.
Why a hexagon? Regular hexagons tile a plane without gaps
and cover more area for the same boundary length than trian-
gles or squares (other tileable primitives) [9] (discussed in Ap-
pendix G). When combined with the local color field, each Hexel
can model both the region itself and smooth appearance varia-
tion within it. In 3D, Hexels are rendered as oriented surface
elements using perspective-correct ray-plane intersection and
depth-ordered blending. The same primitive can be placed di-
rectly in the image plane for 2D image representation. Thus, Hex-
els provide a single compact primitive family for novel-view syn-
thesis, surface reconstruction, and image representation, without
requiring texture maps, neural appearance fields, or decoding
networks.
We evaluate Hexels for 3D novel view synthesis (memory-
matched, primitive-matched, and unconstrained), surface re-
construction, and 2D image representation. We primarily use
34 scenes across DTU [16], Tanks and Temples [21], and Mip-
NeRF360 [1]. At matched model sizes, Hexels improves PSNR
over 2DGS by 4.53–7.38 dB with upto 3.6× less VRAM require-
ments and over Triangle Splatting by 1.70–2.90 dB, with up to
1.87× higher rendering FPS (Table 2). At matched primitive
budgets, Hexels improves PSNR over 2DGS by up to 4.74 dB,
using 1.76–1.95× less model size and upto 7× less VRAM, and
renders up to 2.31× faster (Table 3). Under unrestricted model
sizes, Hexels still improve PSNR over 2DGS by 0.90 dB on both
Tanks and Temples and DTU, while rendering 1.37× and 2.26×
faster, respectively. For surface reconstruction, Hexels achieves
0.978 mm mean Chamfer distance over 15 DTU scans, versus
1.375 mm for 2DGS at the same model-memory budget (Table 5).
On 6 Tanks and Temples surface scenes, mean F1 for Hexels
is 34.38%, versus 31.72% for 2DGS (Table 6). Hexels also con-
verges faster (Figure 4) and improves 2D image representation
over Image-GS [40] (Table 1).

Subsequent work improves different parts of this trade-off,
but usually with a catch. Triangle Splatting [13], Deformable Beta
Splatting [23], and 3D Convex Splatting [11] redesign the primi-
tive or kernel to improve expressiveness or rendering speed, but
at cost of higher memory and VRAM usage. Hybrid methods
such as BBSplat [33], NeST [39], and Nexels [30] reduce ge-
ometric redundancy by separating geometry from appearance
through textures or neural fields; this shifts capacity into addi-
tional memory, training state, and computation. Other meth-

ter sharing, quantization, distillation, or baking [2, 5, 19, 24]
but still largely struggled to address the efficiency problem on
all of its axes. Hexels provides a solution to the efficiency prob-
lem by redesigning the primitive itself to exploit local structure
and geometry in appearance. The resulting primitive is compact
in the form in which it is learned and rendered, simultaneously
targeting storage, training VRAM, primitive count, FPS, and re-
construction quality. Further, unlike previous works, Hexels are
general surface primitives: the same underlying design applies
directly to oriented 3D surfaces and to 2D images without major
changes to the representation or optimization.
3 Hexels: Hexagonal Surface Elements
We introduce Hexels, planar hexagonal surface elements de-
signed to reduce overlap and overdraw, avoid the long tails of
Gaussian footprints, and represent more appearance within each
primitive. Each Hexel combines a soft hexagonal support with a
first-order spatial color field, allowing a single primitive to cover
a larger coherent surface region while modeling smooth appear-
ance variation within it. In 3D, a Hexel is an oriented planar
patch with a local 2D coordinate system. Following 2DGS [14],
we evaluate each primitive at the perspective-correct ray-plane
intersection and blend the resulting contributions in depth or-
der.

We use a hexagon because it provides an efficient, nearly
isotropic way to cover a plane. Regular hexagons tile a plane
without gaps and, at equal area, require less perimeter than reg-
ular triangles or squares, consistent with the honeycomb theo-
rem [9]. More concretely, under the local first-order approxima-
tion model of Appendix G, a suitably oriented and anisotropi-
cally scaled hexagon can cover about 30% more area than the
corresponding square and twice the area of the corresponding
triangle for the same bounded color error (See Proposition G.2).
Our results reflect that these ideas do help structure a more effi-
cient primitive even in non-ideal settings.

4 Experiments
We evaluate novel-view synthesis under matched model memory,
matched primitive budgets and native baseline settings. As our
focus is on fundamental parameter-based efficiency, all Hexels
results do not utilize any post-quantization or compression. For
initial runtime compression analysis, refer to Appendix I.3.
Implementation. Hexels start from sparse COLMAP points and
train for 30K iterations. Fixed configurations retain the ini-
tial population; densified configurations use mixed clone/split
growth and contribution-based pruning, with a 2M limit.
Appendix I.2 reports all configurations. Training and evalua-
tion details are in Appendix E.1.
Datasets, baselines, and metrics. We evaluate nine Mip-NeRF360
scenes [1], fifteen DTU scans [16], and ten Tanks and Temples
scenes [21], using common held-out views and resolutions. We
report PSNR, SSIM [34], LPIPS [37], training time, peak train-
ing VRAM, rendering FPS, and deployment memory size, aver-
aged over scenes. Our baselines are 3DGS [18], 3D Convex Splat-
ting [11], BBSplat [33], Nexels [30], 2DGS [14], Triangle Splat-
ting [13], and MeshSplatting [12]. Tables group methods into vol-
umetric primitives, textured or neural surfels, and native surface-
aligned primitives with local appearance. We select these base-
lines as competitive representatives of their respective classes,
spanning complementary quality-efficiency trade-offs. The final
group - 2DGS, Triangle Splatting, and MeshSplatting - provides
the closest comparison to Hexels, as these methods use explicit
surface-aligned primitives without separate texture maps or neu-
ral appearance fields; we therefore emphasize this group in our
analysis. To our knowledge, this evaluation over 34 real-world
scenes is the largest quantitative NVS evaluation, by scene count,
among the original papers introducing the compared methods.

Resource-constrained reconstruction. Compact, fixed-population
Hexels retain high reconstruction quality while requiring little
model memory and training VRAM and supporting fast render-
ing. These gains arise directly from the primitive and do not
rely on texture atlases, neural decoders, pruning, or quantiza-
tion. This makes Hexels well suited to reconstruction and ren-
dering on memory-limited and edge devices. Pruning and quan-

We have also developed an efficient renderer that directly eval-
uates the same Hexel representation used during training, with-
out baking or conversion to a separate deployment representa-
tion. We will release it together with the training code. This pro-
vides a direct path toward on-device NVS and surface reconstruc-
tion, where captured images and reconstructed scene data can re-
main local to the device.
4.4 Additional Ablation
To further isolate the effects of Hexel’s shape and spatial appear-
ance, we ablate Gaussian and hexagonal shape supports with con-
stant RGB color or first-order RGB gradients (Hexel V1). We use
planar image fitting for our ablation instead of a 3D scene to
keep surface normals and depth order fixed, therefore avoiding
confounding changes in reconstructed geometry and multiview
visibility. All four settings of our ablation use the same primi-
tive count, initialization, loss (MSE), and optimization (6K-step
Adam).
This differs from the Image-GS comparison in Section 3.1,
which uses normalized top-𝐾blending and quantization-aware
training. The objective here is to do one-to-one analysis of shape
and appearance.
Figure 9 compares the four ablative settings on flat and
smoothly shaded targets with 256 primitives. At either appear-
ance model, Hexels achieve higher PSNR than Gaussians on
both targets, isolating the effect of support shape. Adding spatial
gradients improves both shapes, showing the benefit of using
our color gradients within each primitive, and providing sup-
port to our motivation for the appearance design. The linear
color gradient Hexel achieves the lowest error: on the shaded
target, it reaches 37.07 dB versus 35.13 dB for the same appear-
ance Gaussian, while reducing the overdraw - represented using
mean blended contributions of primitives from 10.47 to 2.48 per
pixel. The error and contribution maps in the figure show these
improvements.
Figure 10 shows more results regarding the overdraw bene-
fits for complicated and detailed artworks. Hexels require signif-
icantly fewer primitives per pixel (reducing mean blends/pixel
contributions by 71–80% relative to their Gaussian counterparts)
to achieve similar perceptual quality.
5 Conclusion
We introduced Hexels, a general surface primitive, for 2D and
3D, that exploits the local structure of the data it represents:
hexagonal support efficiently covers coherent surface regions,
while a first-order spatial color field models appearance variation
within each primitive. Across our three 3D benchmarks, Hex-
els establish a new quality–efficiency frontier among the eval-
uated surface-aligned representations, improving PSNR by up
to 7.38 dB at matched model memory, using lower peak train-
ing VRAM and up to 2× less learned state at matched primitive
count, while rendering up to 2.31× faster than 2DGS. The same
primitive also performs strongly for memory-constrained sur-
face reconstruction and directly generalizes to efficient 2D image
representation. Hexels therefore move efficiency into the prim-
itive itself, opening a path toward high-quality novel-view syn-
thesis and explicit surface reconstruction on edge and resource-
constrained hardware.
Limitations and Future Work. A first-order color model can-
not represent arbitrary texture within a single primitive. High-
frequency or uncorrelated appearance requires smaller primi-
tives or a richer color model, reducing the expected benefit of
exploiting local smoothness. Sparse initialization can also leave
parts of a scene poorly covered, and the independent surface
cells do not form a watertight mesh. Sharper footprints do not
by themselves prevent sampling artifacts, especially at small
projected sizes or grazing views. Hexels does not outperform
every baseline on every metric; the appendix provides the full
comparisons. Our current primitives are planar. A natural exten-
sion is developable Hexels: curved patches with zero Gaussian
curvature that remain locally isometric to the planar canoni-
cal hexagon. Such primitives could bend along one principal
direction, for example, as cylindrical or conical patches, while
preserving the simple 2D footprint and affine appearance pa-
rameterization. This may allow individual primitives to cover
larger regions of locally near-developable geometry without in-
troducing additional overlap.

A Hexel Parameterization
Stored parameters. A 3D Hexel stores its center 𝐩𝑖∈ℝ3, two
positive tangent scales 𝑠𝑢,𝑖and 𝑠𝑣,𝑖, a quaternion defining its ori-
entation, opacity 𝑜𝑖, and support sharpness 𝛽𝑖. The quaternion
is normalized before constructing the tangent frame, and its first
two axes define the orthogonal tangent directions 𝐭𝑢,𝑖and 𝐭𝑣,𝑖.
Thus, the geometric and support parameters require 11 learned
scalars: three for position, two for scale, four for orientation, one
for opacity, and one for support sharpness.
The V1 appearance model adds a base RGB value and two RGB
spatial gradients, for nine additional scalars. A V1 Hexel therefore
contains 20 learned scalars in total. V2 adds nine directional coef-
ficients, while V3 adds 45 spherical-harmonic coefficients, giving
29 and 65 learned scalars, respectively.

C Densification and Pruning
Hexels (V1/V2/V3 - Fixed) do not primarily use densification
and pruning to avoid exceeding memory and/or primitive con-

Mixed growth. Our densification strategy is similar to 3DGS and
2DGS. Between two growth triggers, for each Hexel, we track the
norm of the loss gradient with respect to its projected 2D center,
averaged over the iterations in which the Hexel is visible. A Hexel
becomes a parent Hexel when this average is large, it was visible
often enough, and it is not too young. Small parents are cloned,
and large parents are split. A parent Hexel counts as small when
its larger tangent scale is at most 1% of the scene extent. A clone
is a copy of the parent Hexel whose center is moved within the
parent’s tangent plane by a random Gaussian offset, scaled by the
parent’s two scales. A split replaces the parent with two children,
each offset randomly in the same plane, with both scales divided
by 1.6, as in 3DGS, and an opacity 𝑜child = 1 −√1 −𝑜parent.
New Hexels copy the parent’s appearance coefficients.

G Why Hexagons and Spatial Color?
We want each Hexel to represent a large surface region with-
out losing the appearance variation inside it. This requires two
choices: a shape that can cover surfaces efficiently, and a color
model that can describe more than a constant color. The hon-
eycomb theorem motivates the first choice: regular hexagons
minimize boundary length among equal-area partitions of the
plane [9]. The spatial color field addresses the second.
We examine these choices on one planar patch at a fixed view-
ing direction. First, we show that an affine color field represents
the local color slope, with the remaining error controlled by
how that slope changes across the patch. This determines a re-
gion where one color field stays within a chosen error tolerance.
We then show that, within the same region, the largest affine
hexagon covers twice the area of the largest affine triangle and
about 30% more than the largest affine square.

H Extended Related Work
Novel view synthesis and surface reconstruction. NeRF [25] was in-
troduced for novel view synthesis and represents a scene as a neu-
ral field of density and view-dependent radiance. This represen-
tation enabled high-quality novel-view rendering. However, the
repeated network evaluations required for volumetric rendering
made the original NeRF expensive to train and slow to render.
A large body of follow-up work introduced explicit feature grids,
hash encodings, precomputed representations, and baked repre-
sentations to reduce the sampling, rendering, and computational
demands of the original neural-field models [6, 26, 28].
3DGS [18] introduced an alternative for substantially more
efficient rendering using explicit anisotropic Gaussian volume
primitives, enabling real-time rasterization. However, because of
their volumetric nature, 3D Gaussians are not naturally surface
aligned and can produce inaccurate geometry. To remedy this,
works such as SuGaR [7] regularize Gaussians toward surfaces.
An important solution was introduced by 2DGS [14], which in-
stead represents the scene using oriented planar Gaussian surfels
and perspective-correct ray–plane intersections, retaining fast
rasterization while substantially improving the geometry.
Despite the large improvements made by 3DGS and 2DGS
in training and rendering time relative to NeRF, these improve-
ments are still not enough for truly resource-constrained set-
tings. Efficiency requires several things at the same time: learned
model storage and training VRAM should be as low as possible,
primitive count should remain small relative to the cost of each
primitive, and the representation should still retain high recon-
struction quality and fast rendering. 3DGS and 2DGS can both

require large primitive populations and substantial memory. De-
spite this, we find that 2DGS remains the strongest prior na-
tive surface representation in terms of the overall performance–
efficiency balance when these factors are considered together,
even after a number of subsequent works have attempted to im-
prove different parts of the problem.

works have tried to make the primitive more expressive, re-

model storage, or address limitations of 2DGS and earlier Gaus-
sian representations, such as the infinite support of Gaussian
kernels. GES [10], for example, replaces the Gaussian with a
more flexible generalized exponential kernel, while Deformable
Beta Splatting [23] introduces bounded and deformable kernels.
3D Convex Splatting [11] instead uses smooth 3D convex prim-
itives, allowing more flexible volumetric geometry and sharper
boundaries, but remains a volumetric rather than surface-aligned
representation.
Triangle Splatting [13], on the other hand, optimizes an in-
dependent triangle soup so that high rendering speeds can be
achieved while retaining a surface-aligned primitive. However,
high-quality scenes can still require very large triangle popula-
tions, increasing both model storage and the computation and
memory required during optimization. The independent trian-
gle soup also does not provide surface connectivity. MeshSplat-
ting [12] follows this work by introducing explicit connectivity
and an opaque mesh representation, trading the independence
of individual splats for a conventional connected surface. Other
works increase geometric complexity in different ways, includ-
ing Quadratic Gaussian Splatting [41], which introduces curved
patches, and MILo [8], which brings mesh construction directly
into Gaussian optimization. In our experiments, we focus on
2DGS, Triangle Splatting, and MeshSplatting, which we find
provide the strongest overall quality–efficiency trade-offs among
prior native surface-aligned representations.
Hexels are also primitive-based renderers, but we try to solve a
more fundamental problem: can we design a more efficient prim-
itive that is able to exploit the structure found in the world, both
in geometry and appearance? Like 2DGS, we retain independent
planar elements, but change the region represented by one ele-
ment and the signal represented inside it. We use an affine hexag-
onal patch, which gives a large, nearly isotropic local region, to-
gether with a first-order color field that models smooth appear-
ance variation across a surface. We find that much of the varia-
tion on local surfaces is smooth, making such a representation
particularly efficient.
A first-order color field, by itself, is not unique to Hexels: a
vertex-colored triangle (another tiling primitive), for example,
can also represent an affine color field. The domain of our primi-
tive, however, makes an important difference. Under the same
local approximation-error ellipse, our analysis shows that the
largest admissible affine regular hexagon can cover twice the area
of the corresponding affine triangle and about 1.3× that of the
square (Appendix G). Hexels therefore increase the amount rep-
resented by a simple planar element without requiring complex
geometry, volumetric convexes, or mesh connectivity.
Hybrid primitives and representations. The most direct compar-
isons to Hexels are fundamentally native surface primitives.
However, there has also been a trend toward hybrid represen-
tations that separate geometry from appearance in order to
improve particular aspects of efficiency over 2DGS-like repre-
sentations. Gaussian Billboards [35], for example, introduces
spatially varying appearance through per-splat texture interpo-
lation. BBSplat [33] learns separate RGB and alpha texture maps
to reduce the number of geometric primitives required. This can
substantially reduce primitive count, but the textures themselves
become part of the model and training cost. NeST [39] follows
a related idea by separating sparse surface geometry from a
neural appearance representation. Nexels [30] similarly com-
bines sparse surfels with a shared neural appearance field and
per-primitive colors. These methods can substantially reduce ge-
ometric primitive count, but this capacity is moved into texture
maps, feature fields, or neural networks, which introduce ad-
ditional memory, training state, and computation. Concurrent
work such as Bake It Till You Make It [17], which is also centered
on improving the efficiency of surface splats, follows this hybrid
direction. It uses sparse surface geometry together with a hash-
based appearance representation during optimization, and later
bakes high-frequency appearance into a texture atlas for render-
ing. This can reduce the number of geometric surface primitives
and improve rendering speed, but appearance is again moved
outside the primitive and requires a separate representation and
baking pipeline.
These ideas of separating geometry and appearance, neural
textures, and texture baking are interesting, but they can in prin-
ciple be applied on top of different native surface primitives.
They also often come with a different set of trade-offs, be it
slower rendering, more memory or non-ideal decoupled opti-
mization. They therefore answer a somewhat different question
from ours. We include these methods in our comparisons, but
count their full learned representation, including textures, fea-
ture grids, and neural decoders, rather than only the number of
geometric elements. These hybrid setups, however, are not the
objective of our work.
Population control and compression. Given the multifaceted na-
ture of the efficiency problem, another body of work has devel-
oped around interventions on existing primitives to improve dif-
ferent aspects of their efficiency. Scaffold-GS [24], for example,
reduces 3DGS redundancy through anchors that generate view-
adaptive Gaussian attributes rather than storing every Gaussian
independently. 3DGS-MCMC [19] improves primitive alloca-
tion through stochastic updates and relocation. FastGS [29] in-
stead focuses on training time, using multi-view-consistent den-
sification and pruning to reduce unnecessary Gaussian growth.
A related line of work compresses already learned representa-

tions. LightGaussian [5] prunes low-importance Gaussians, dis-
tills appearance into lower-order spherical harmonics, and ap-
plies vector quantization to reduce model size. Compact 3D
Gaussian Representation [22] combines learnable masking with
shared neural appearance, vector quantization, and entropy cod-
ing. Compressed 3DGS [27] similarly uses quantization-aware
clustering and codebooks to reduce storage. Related ideas have
also been developed for surface-based Gaussian representations.
These methods are useful and complementary, but they start
with an already chosen primitive and add additional methodol-
ogy around it. They may greatly improve one aspect of efficiency
without necessarily reducing the memory and computation re-
quired elsewhere, particularly during training. Our focus is there-
fore the more fundamental question: we can add pruning, quan-
tization, codebooks, texture atlases, neural decoders, and other
post-hoc compression, but can we also start with a more effi-
cient native surface primitive? Hexels address this question be-
fore these additional techniques are applied; many of them could
still be applied on top of Hexels afterward.
Neural Image Representations. A similar line of research has de-
veloped independently in 2D, where neural image representa-
tions are used to encode visual media. Earlier approaches often
relied on autoencoders or implicit neural functions. More re-
cently, GaussianImage [38] adapted ideas from Gaussian splat-
ting to represent images using simple 2D Gaussian primitives,
demonstrating fast fitting and rendering. Image-GS [40] further
improves this representation using content-adaptive initializa-
tion and progressive optimization of anisotropic 2D Gaussians.
SAD [15] instead represents the image through a soft anisotropic
partition whose regions depend on neighboring sites rather than
independently parameterized primitives. We compare Hexels
primarily with Image-GS because both use independently pa-
rameterized image primitives, enabling a direct comparison of
quality and efficiency per primitive. SAD instead represents im-
ages through a coupled partition whose regions depend on neigh-
boring sites, addressing a different representation regime that we
leave for future comparison.
We show that Hexels can also be used directly for 2D image
representation, where they outperform Image-GS in reconstruc-
tion quality and training convergence under our evaluated set-
tings. More importantly, Hexels are designed as a general surface
primitive rather than separate 2D and 3D representations. In 2D,
the primitive is placed directly in the image plane; in 3D, it is ori-
ented on a surface patch. While the compositor changes where
required by visibility and depth ordering, the basic hexagonal
support and appearance model remain the same. This gives Hex-
els a general capability across image representation, novel-view
synthesis, and surface reconstruction, rather than requiring a sep-
arate representation for each setting.
Overall efficiency. A common pattern across methods that have
tried to make surface-aligned representations more efficient is
thatthecostisoftenmovedtoanother partoftheproblem.Faster
rasterization may still require a very large primitive population.
Sparse geometry may move capacity into external texture maps
or neural fields, increasing representation complexity, training
VRAM, or final model storage. Compression can make the de-
ployed scene small while having much less effect on the size and
memory required during training. Richer geometry can reduce
primitive count while increasing the cost of each primitive.
Hexels are designed to alleviate these problems together, with-
out first resorting to post-hoc retrofitting and before pruning or
compression is applied. We use the fact that natural images, sur-
faces, and scenes contain local structure. Large regions are often
approximately planar and vary smoothly in appearance. A Hexel
covers a bounded, efficient local region and models first-order
appearance variation inside it, allowing simple regions to be rep-
resented by larger and fewer elements. This allows us to target
high quality, low learned storage, low training VRAM, few prim-
itives, and high FPS in the same native surface representation, in
both 2D and 3D. Pruning, quantization, neural appearance mod-
els, and other post-hoc improvements remain possible additions
rather than requirements of the representation.

lossless compression analysis keeps every FP32 parameter exact.
It regroups the bytes of each parameter array by position (all first
bytes of the 4-byte values, then all second bytes, and so on), so
that the highly repetitive sign and exponent bytes are stored to-
gether. It then compresses the result with DEFLATE [4], the stan-

gzip software. PQ1024 replaces RGB appearance vectors with
1,024-entry codebooks: one per SH coefficient for V3, and corre-
sponding codebooks for the spatial and directional gradients of
V1/V2. Geometry and base color remain lossless. Parameters are
decoded to FP32 before rendering, without retraining.
