Abstract :

Achieving high-quality reconstruction and responsive camera tracking remains challenging for monocular RGB Gaussian splatting SLAM under a limited optimization budget. We present an online system that addresses this challenge by combining learned geometric priors with explicit geometric camera tracking, which can substantially outperform learned camera prediction in terms of tracking accuracy. A frozen streaming 3D foundation model serves as a virtual RGB-D sensor, providing depth estimation from monocular video. An explicit geometric solver then estimates camera motion through feature reprojection, and a streaming Gaussian mapper integrates the resulting observations into a dense scene representation. We further show that geometric tracking improves camera pose accuracy across repre-sentative streaming geometry backbones, demonstrating the broader applicability of our approach. Extensive experiments on indoor and outdoor datasets show that our method significantly improves reconstruction quality over existing approaches, achieving ∼7 FPS on a single RTX 4090 and delivering 2–3× the throughput of the evaluated prior SLAM systems. 

Keywords: 3D reconstruction, Gaussian Splatting, SLAM, Feed-forward Model. 

1 Introduction 

Monocular Gaussian SLAM aims to estimate the camera pose and build a renderable scene map online using only an RGB image stream. This capability supports immersive interaction, live scene capture, and remote interaction in AR/VR and robotics. 3D Gaus-sian splatting (GS) (Kerbl et al., 2023) provides an explicit scene representation with efficient rendering and differentiable map-ping, making it a useful representation for dense SLAM. However, unlike RGB-D Gaussian SLAM methods (Keetha et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2024; Yugay et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2024; Ha et al., 2024; L. Zhu et al., 2025), without measured depth, the monocular GS SLAM system must jointly recover camera pose, scene geometry, and the appearance, within a limited computational budget. Methods that rely on sparse feature points or incremental map optimization struggle to achieve high-fidelity mapping Matsuki et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024. Learned dense priors help fill this gap, but integrating them into a consistent map introduces further challenges. Dense depth prediction and camera joint optimiza-tion add online computational cost (Homeyer et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025), while per-frame depth predictions do not ensure cross-frame scale consistency and require additional scale recovery and geometric alignment (Deng et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2026). Consis-tent depth and camera poses are both essential to this task. Depth determines where Gaussians are initialized, while camera poses determine whether observations from different frames align. Poor initialization requires additional map refinement, and inaccurate poses introduce conflicting observations that can blur the recon-structed scene. The challenge is therefore to obtain consistent dense depth and camera poses together within a limited online computational budget. Streaming 3D foundation models offer a promising starting point. By maintaining temporal context, they predict consistent dense depth and camera poses as images arrive (Su et al., 2026; Zhuo et al., 2026; X. Chen et al., 2026; L.-Z. Chen et al., 2026). Their depth predictions provide dense surface estimates for the Gaus-sian initialization, reducing the need to recover the scene struc-ture from sparse feature points. Yet this advantage depends on the quality of the accompanying poses. We observe that directly using the models’ predicted poses can lead to cross-frame misalignment, limiting reconstruction quality even when useful depth estimates are available. To this end, we introduce an explicit geometry pose solver that combines 2D feature points with predicted depth and utilizes them to jointly constrain camera pose estimation. Our system requires no additional training and runs at ∼7 FPS on a single RTX 4090 GPU. It achieves higher rendering quality than the previous baselines across indoor and outdoor datasets, with the lowest depth error and competitive tracking error. Over-all, our main contributions are: • Depth-assisted geometric tracking for streaming 3D models. We introduce an explicit geometry solver for cam-era tracking that combines 2D feature points with predicted depth. Intensive experiments show that our geometry solver can provide better tracking than the learned streaming cam-era head. • An efficient monocular Gaussian SLAM system. We build a faster high-fidelity monocular GS SLAM system com-bining the geometric solver, a frozen streaming model, and a Gaussian mapper, leading to higher appearance quality. Across evaluated datasets, our system can improve render-ing PSNR by up to 3.1 dB. • Generalization of geometry solver across streaming models. We apply our geometry solver to multiple represen-tative streaming models. We show that the geometry solver reduces trajectory error by up to ∼97% relative to the models’ native camera head, demonstrating its effectiveness across different backbones and providing insight for future stream-ing model design. arXiv:2610.36853v1 [cs.CV] 27 Oct 2026 LingGS: Decoupling Feed-Forward Geometry Prediction from Camera Tracking for High-Fidelity Gaussian SLAM 

2 Related Work Visual tracking without depth prior. These methods recover scene geometry from RGB observations through visual tracking and map optimization. MonoGS (Matsuki et al., 2024) estimates poses by photometric alignment with the Gaussian map, making tracking dependent on the quality of the evolv-ing reconstruction. Photo-SLAM (Huang et al., 2024) com-bines ORB-SLAM3 (Campos et al., 2021) with Gaussian mapping. CaRtGS (Feng et al., 2025), RP-SLAM (Bai et al., 2026), and SEGS-SLAM (Wen et al., 2025) further develop this architecture through improvements to computation allocation, map updates, and scene modeling. MGSO (Y. S. Hu et al., 2025) instead initializes Gaus-sians from geometry recovered by direct photometric odometry. Without learned dense geometric priors, surface recovery relies on multi-view image observations and parallax. Regions with weak visual constraints may remain insufficiently reconstructed at initialization, requiring interpolation or further geometric re-finement during mapping. 

Learned geometry for tracking and mapping. Learned depth and pointmap predictions provide richer surface informa-tion for tracking and mapping. Splat-SLAM (Sandström et al., 2024), DROID-Splat (Homeyer et al., 2025), and HI-SLAM2 (W. Zhang et al., 2025) integrate learned dense depth and trajectory re-finement. MGS-SLAM (P. Zhu et al., 2024) uses multi-view depth supervision, while SplatMAP (Y. Hu et al., 2025) updates Gaussian positions using refined poses and depths from dense SLAM, and uses reliability masks to prune or add Gaussians. Some methods strengthen geometric consistency through map-to-tracker feed-back or optical flow constraints (F. Zhu et al., 2026; Seo et al., 2026), and suppress unreliable observations in dynamic scenes (Zheng et al., 2025; M. Li et al., 2025). These designs improve geometric estimation, but dense tracking and iterative refinement add com-putation to the online pipeline, limiting their application. Feed-forward prediction offers a way to reduce this burden. StreamGS (Y. Li et al., 2025) and Flash-Mono (Z. Zhang et al., 2026) predict poses and Gaussian attributes before limited map re-finement, but require intensive model retraining. Pointmap and multi-view foundation models also support tracking and mapping in OpenGS-SLAM (Yu et al., 2025), ARTDECO (G. Li et al., 2026) and S3PO-GS (Cheng et al., 2025), but with a high tracking cost. GeoGS-SLAM (Gao et al., 2026), VGGT-GS SLAM (Han et al., 2026), and M 3 (Ren et al., 2026) combine learned predictions with geomet-ric or photometric optimization to refine poses and scene estimates by processing frames in batches. OTF (Meuleman et al., 2025) ex-plores efficient incremental Gaussian construction, but loses the consistent geometry reconstruction. Combining predicted geometry with explicit pose estimation is particularly relevant to our work. GigaSLAM (Deng et al., 2025) and KiloGS-SLAM (Yu et al., 2026) use predicted depth with geo-metric solvers, while KiloGS-SLAM additionally activates learned pixel matching when needed, but they suffer from the scale drift. MonoEM-GS (Kruzhkov et al., 2026) uses the iterative closest point (ICP) to align predicted pointmap with the Gaussian representa-tion. These approaches demonstrate the value of learned geometry for explicit camera estimation. Our work focuses on using stream-ing consistent depth predictions for camera tracking and Gaussian mapping.

4 Experiment 

4.1 Setup and Evaluation 

Datasets. We evaluate on four datasets, including indoor and outdoor datasets. 7-Scenes (Shotton et al., 2013) has seven in-door RGB sequences with ground truth Kinect depth. Oxford Spires (Tao et al., 2026) consists of six outdoor clips of 1000–2001 frames selected from outdoor sites: Keble College-(02,03,04), Ob-servatory Quarter-01, Christ Church-02, and Bodleian Library-02, with poses and depth reprojected from the point cloud of a ter-restrial laser scan. From RE10K (Zhou et al., 2018) we select 60 short indoor and outdoor real-estate clips with 71–279 frames. Eleven handheld sequences (fr1, fr2, fr3) are selected from the 

TUM-D (Sturm et al., 2012) dataset. All frames are processed at 518x392 resolution across all datasets. We evaluate all methods using a single NVIDIA RTX4090 GPU with an AMD Ryzen Thread-ripper PRO 3995WX 64-Cores CPU. 

Evaluation metrics. We evaluate novel-view synthesis (NVS) quality on held-out views using PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS. We measure global trajectory accuracy using the root-mean-square Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) after aligning the estimated tra-jectory to the reference trajectory with a Sim(3) Umeyama align-ment (Umeyama, 1991). We also report the Relative Pose Error (RPE) between consecutive frames, including translational error (RPE 𝑡 ) and rotational error (RPE 𝑅 ). To evaluate reconstruction ge-ometry, we compute the mean L1 error between the rendered and ground-truth depth on held-out views. Finally, we report end-to-end throughput in frames per second (FPS), calculated by dividing the number of frames in each sequence by its total reconstruction time. 

Baselines. We compare against MonoGS (Matsuki et al., 2024), S3PO-GS (Cheng et al., 2025), Photo-SLAM (Huang et al., 2024), and OTF (Meuleman et al., 2025). MonoGS uses 3D Gaussians for monocular tracking and mapping, while S3PO-GS targets monocular SLAM with globally scale-consistent Gaussian point maps. Photo-SLAM combines feature-based tracking with Gaus-sian splatting for photorealistic mapping, whereas OTF incre-mentally reconstructs large-scale scenes from unposed image se-quences with large parallax. All baselines are evaluated using their publicly available implementations on identical input frames. To compare with Photo-SLAM fairly, we increase its mapping iter-ations from 8 to a maximum of 200 while retaining concurrent tracking and mapping, maintaining a processing rate of ∼6 FPS.

Notably, OTF achieves competitive novel-view rendering quality, but exhibits the highest depth error on all three datasets. This dis-crepancy highlights a limitation of assessing 3D Gaussian maps through RGB rendering alone: visually plausible novel views do not necessarily imply geometrically accurate reconstruction, since the observed appearance can still be explained by Gaussians that are incorrectly distributed in 3D space. 

4.3 Ablation study 

Ablation on camera tracking. Table 3 isolates the effect of the pose on the reconstruction. Four alternatives are com-pared with our solver: (A) Virtual stereo, the RGB-D front-end in ORB-SLAM3, which turns the predicted depth into a virtual right-image coordinate and optimizes the stereo residual shown in Eq. 13; (B) 2D reprojection, the same front-end with depth used only to back-project the new map points, so that tracking sees the 2D reprojection residual only, like the monocular RGB ORB-SLAM3; (C) a purely geometric point-to-plane ICP of the predicted depth against a local model and (D) the backbone’s own regressed camera head. Compared with virtual stereo (A), our direct-depth residual produces more accurate camera poses while preserving the same PSNR: ATE decreases from 79.5 to 73.8 cm, while trans-lational RPE decreases from 1.123 to 0.943 cm. This result indicates that directly modeling the uncertainty of predicted depth provides a more effective geometric constraint than encoding it through a virtual-stereo observation. The comparison with 2D reprojection (B) further demonstrates the importance of explicitly including depth in pose optimization. Removing the depth residual reduces PSNR by 3.45 dB, increases ATE from 73.8 to 162.1 cm, and in-creases translational RPE from 0.943 to 4.723 cm. Predicted depth is therefore useful not only for initializing map points, but also for constraining the camera pose at every frame. The purely unopti-mized CPU ICP variant (C) is also substantially less accurate than our solver, increasing ATE from 73.8 to 307.2 cm, translational RPE from 0.943 to 15.14 cm, and rotational RPE from 0.095 ◦ to 0.482 ◦.This result suggests that point-to-plane alignment alone is less robust to predicted-depth noise and weak geometric constraints. Finally, the backbone camera head (D) is fast but produces sub-stantially less accurate trajectories, with 5.98 m ATE and 15.39 cm translational RPE. 5 Conclusion 

We present LingGS, an online monocular Gaussian SLAM sys-tem that decouples feed-forward geometry prediction from cam-era tracking. A frozen streaming 3D foundation model serves as a virtual RGB-D sensor, while an explicit geometric solver es-timates camera poses by jointly minimizing image reprojection and direct depth residuals. Combined with incremental Gaus-sian mapping, this design enables high-fidelity reconstruction. Ex-periments across indoor and outdoor datasets demonstrate im-proved rendering quality and geometric accuracy while maintain-ing efficient processing and competitive tracking accuracy. Fur-ther experiments across multiple streaming backbones support the broader applicability of our geometric solver. Our current system does not address loop closure or dynamic scenes. Its reliance on ORB feature extraction and matching can also compromise tracking in repetitive or weakly textured envi-ronments, where correspondences may be ambiguous or reliable features scarce. Addressing these limitations with learned prior is an important direction for improving long-term consistency and robustness. More broadly, our findings motivate future streaming 3D models that integrate learned geometric priors with explicit geometric optimization to improve pose accuracy and geometric consistency in online reconstruction.
